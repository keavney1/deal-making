# The Global Primary Productivity Initiative: A Framework for International Collaboration on Ecosystem Restoration and Climate Resilience

## Purpose

This document proposes an international framework for collaboration on ecosystem restoration and sustainable energy infrastructure over the next century. Our thesis is that climate resilience requires maximizing and sustaining Earth's photosynthetic capacity—the process by which plants and algae convert sunlight into biomass, generate oxygen, and sequester atmospheric carbon.

Beyond carbon sequestration, robust global photosynthesis maintains the oxygen balance essential for aerobic life. Oxygen depletion events—whether in oceanic dead zones or through atmospheric oxygen decline—would constitute catastrophic ecological failures. The framework below prioritizes ecosystems based on their current and potential oxygen production, recognizing that a world with more photosynthetic capacity is a world more resilient to climate shocks.

## 1. Ocean Restoration

The oceans produce approximately half of the planet's oxygen through phytoplankton photosynthesis while absorbing a quarter of human CO2 emissions. Protecting and restoring ocean ecosystems is foundational to climate resilience.

**1.1 Global Coral Restoration Consortium** — An international body coordinating reef protection and restoration, including assisted migration of heat-tolerant genotypes. Coral reefs support marine biodiversity and enhance primary productivity in surrounding waters.

**1.2 Pelagic Productivity Monitoring and Research Program** — A satellite and ocean-sensor array tracking global phytoplankton health, linked to research on safe and scalable ocean fertilization techniques. All fertilization research would prioritize ecological safety: carefully bounded experiments overseen by marine ecologists, with a commitment to understanding ecosystem impacts before any large-scale deployment.

**1.3 Comprehensive IUU Fishing Enforcement** — International enforcement against illegal fishing to restore marine food webs and ecosystem stability.

**1.4 Coastal Buffer Ecosystem Expansion** — Protection and expansion of mangrove forests and seagrass meadows, which buffer acidification while sequestering carbon and producing oxygen. These programs would prioritize community stewardship and respect for indigenous coastal rights.

These programs would establish the infrastructure necessary to manage ocean ecosystems at planetary scale.

## 2. Forest Restoration and Sustainable Working Forests

Forests remain the single most significant terrestrial source of oxygen, and expanding forest cover is foundational to climate resilience.

**2.1 Global Reforestation Initiative** — A coordinated international program for reforestation of degraded and deforested lands, prioritizing native species and ecological integration. This converts areas that are currently net carbon sources into productive forests that absorb atmospheric carbon while generating oxygen.

**2.2 Young Forest Management and Sustainable Forestry Programs** — While preserving old-growth forests is vital for biodiversity, working forests managed through their growth phase are net producers of oxygen. The distinction matters: mature forests are approximately carbon-neutral, whereas forests in active growth sequester carbon and release oxygen continuously. International support for sustainable forestry practices—particularly in tropical regions—ensures that forests remain productive rather than carbon-stable.

**2.3 Agroforestry Integration** — Integrating trees into agricultural landscapes expands photosynthetic capacity while providing additional economic benefits to farming communities.

## 3. Wetland and Peatland Conservation

Wetlands, peatlands, and coastal buffers are among the most carbon-dense ecosystems on Earth. Their restoration is essential both for carbon sequestration and for maintaining the hydrological and atmospheric conditions that support regional and global productivity.

**3.1 Peatland Rewetting** — Drained peatlands are currently significant carbon sources due to oxidation and devastating fires. Rewetting restores them to net carbon sinks and protects the underlying peat as a millennia-long carbon archive.

**3.2 Mangrove Expansion and Coastal Buffer Protection** — Mangrove forests buffer coastlines from extreme weather while providing nursery habitats for marine fisheries, coastal protection, and substantial oxygen production. Community-supported mangrove restoration should be prioritized in tropical coastal regions.

**3.3 Prairie Pothole and Wetland Restoration** — Reconnecting prairie potholes and drained wetlands to agricultural landscapes restores biological productivity, improves water quality, and creates critical habitat corridors.

## 4. Agricultural Transformation

Agriculture occupies approximately forty percent of Earth's ice-free land. Shifting to practices that sustain continuous vegetation cover represents a major opportunity for expanding planetary-scale photosynthesis.

**4.1 Regenerative Agriculture Programs** — Cover cropping, reduced tillage, and rotational grazing increase soil carbon, reduce erosion, and sustain year-round vegetation cover, all of which increase photosynthetic output per hectare.

**4.2 Farmer Transition Support** — National and international programs supporting farmers through the shift to regenerative practices—technical assistance, financial incentives, and fair pricing—ensure that sustainable agriculture is economically viable and scalable.

**4.3 Perennial Crop Development** — Expanding perennial grains and oilseeds maintains continuous ground cover, reducing tillage emissions while supporting soil carbon accumulation.

## 5. Clean Energy Transition

The transition to clean energy is not merely a decarbonization project—it is an oxygen protection project. Fossil fuel combustion consumes atmospheric oxygen (O2) and converts it into carbon dioxide (CO2) at industrial scale. Photosynthesis converts CO2 back to O2, but only at rates comparable to natural primary productivity. When combustion outpaces photosynthesis—as it has for decades—net atmospheric O2 is consumed while CO2 accumulates.

The resulting greenhouse effect further depresses planetary productivity: ocean acidification damages coral and phytoplankton, drought and heat stress forest ecosystems, and extreme weather damages agricultural infrastructure. The longer humanity relies on combustion, the more stress is placed on the very systems that replenish oxygen and sequester carbon.

A just and rapid transition away from hydrocarbon combustion therefore serves dual purposes: preserving the oxygen that sustains life and restoring Earth's capacity to generate it.

**5.1 Phase-Out of Fossil Fuel-Based Power Generation** — Globally coordinated programs to transition electricity generation to solar, wind, hydroelectric, and other non-combustion sources. Developed economies should reach near-zero fossil fuel generation by midcentury while providing capital and technology transfer to enable developing economies to leapfrog directly to clean energy infrastructure.

**5.2 Electrification of Transport** — International programs to transition road and rail transport to electric systems, eliminating one of the largest remaining sources of oxygen-consuming combustion.

**5.3 Nuclear Energy and Fusion Research** — Where renewable intermittency presents challenges, advanced nuclear technologies offer high-density, zero-combustion baseload power. An international program on nuclear safety, waste management, and—critically—controlled fusion research would secure energy availability without atmospheric impact. Fusion represents a transformative long-horizon priority: if achieved, it would provide effectively unlimited, emission-free power without consumption of oxygen.

**5.4 Industrial Decarbonization** — Replacing combustion-based industrial processes—including steel, cement, and chemical production—with electrochemical and renewable alternatives, coordinated internationally to prevent carbon leakage between jurisdictions.

## 6. Institutional Framework for Long-Term Stewardship

The initiatives above require permanent institutions with the scope and authority to coordinate planetary-scale action over decades and centuries.

**6.1 Global Biosphere Monitoring System** — A permanent satellite array tracking vegetation health, forest cover, ocean chlorophyll, and atmospheric composition in real time. This system would produce daily planetary health reports, enabling rapid response to emerging threats. Critically, atmospheric O2 concentration would be monitored as a primary vital sign alongside CO2, recognizing that oxygen stability is as essential as carbon reduction.

**6.2 International Photosynthesis Research Consortium** — A coordinated research program examining both natural and enhanced photosynthesis. Natural priorities include coral resilience, forest genetics, and oceanic primary productivity dynamics. Applied priorities include artificial photosynthesis, algal bioreactor systems, and genetic improvements in crop photosynthetic efficiency. The goal is both to understand Earth's biosphere and to expand its productive capacity across agricultural and natural ecosystems.

**6.3 Intergenerational Climate Oversight Authority** — A permanent body authorized to review major infrastructure, agricultural, and industrial projects against their impact on long-term planetary productivity. This authority would use accounting frameworks that represent the interests of future generations, who bear the costs of decisions that degrade ecosystem function but cannot vote in present elections. By institutionalizing a voice for the future, humanity can avoid catastrophic shortsightedness while protecting long-term resilience.

**6.4 Atmospheric Stability Treaty** — A binding international instrument committing signatories to maintain planetary atmospheric composition within ranges compatible with robust biosphere function. This treaty would establish both targets and floors for atmospheric O2 and CO2 concentrations—reducing CO2 to combat warming while maintaining the minimum CO2 necessary to sustain healthy photosynthetic output across natural and agricultural ecosystems. By ensuring stable concentrations of both gases, the treaty would guarantee that Earth's biosphere can generate sufficient oxygen across the long-term future.

## 7. Conclusion

The Global Primary Productivity Initiative proposes that climate resilience begins with Earth's capacity to perform photosynthesis—the process at the foundation of all aerobic life. Forests, oceans, wetlands, and agricultural lands all require stewardship. Fossil fuel combustion must end, to be replaced by renewable, nuclear, and ultimately fusion energy. And permanent institutions must protect future generations from the short-term political pressures that would degrade planetary productivity for private gain.

The next century will be decisive. If humanity expands Earth's photosynthetic capacity, eliminates combustion, and establishes lasting institutional safeguards, we can stabilize the atmosphere and biosphere for millennia. If we fail, climate instability could reduce planetary productivity for generations. The choice is ours—and the frameworks above offer a path forward.
