**LONDON COMMUNITY ORGANISATIONS RESOURCE GUIDE**

**Produced through the London Initiative for Safe AI Community Consultancy Programme**

*A practical, structured reference for community organisations in London working on digital inclusion, educational equity, and local climate adaptation.*

*Last updated: 2025*

**1. ABOUT THIS GUIDE**

This guide exists to do one thing: give your organisation — however small, however stretched — the kind of structured, carefully researched information that a dedicated policy analyst would bring, without you needing to hire one.

It is organised in accessible, modular sections. You do not need to read it cover to cover. Each section is designed so that a person with a specific question can navigate to a relevant answer and act on it.

A note on honesty: this guide was produced by a research screener rather than through direct consultation with your organisation's specific circumstances. It is a strong proxy for what tailored help would look like — frameworks, leads, and considerations that apply broadly across London — and it aims to be thorough and faithful to real needs. But it is not a conversation with you specifically. Where the resource leaves a gap, that's real, and we encourage you to follow up with the London Initiative for Safe AI's dedicated support channels.

**2. HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE**

Each major section below covers one of three priority areas. Sections follow a consistent pattern:

- **Why this matters** — grounding context.
- **Key frameworks** — concrete models and approaches you can adopt or adapt, with practical notes on applicability.
- **London-specific funding and support** — live leads, networks, and funding streams relevant to Greater London.
- **Implementation considerations** — practical obstacles your organisation should anticipate.
- **Intersectionality notes** — critical information about how this area overlaps with the others (digital, education, climate), since these rarely affect distinct populations.

Intersections are also covered in a dedicated strand (Section 5) because, in practice, a community working on digital inclusion is often the same community facing educational disadvantage and climate vulnerability.

**3. DIGITAL INCLUSION**

**3.1 Why This Matters for London Community Organisations**

Digital exclusion in London remains striking. While the city's overall connectivity rates are high, granular data — by borough, by age, by income band — reveals significant populations without reliable internet access, digital skills, or the confidence to navigate online services that are increasingly the only channel for housing, benefits, and healthcare interactions. For community organisations, digital inclusion is not a luxury programme; it is often the prerequisite for operational survival, since your beneficiaries increasingly need digital skills to access the services you exist to help them reach.

**3.2 Key Frameworks**

*Universal Design for Access* — Rather than building separate 'digital skills courses' siloed from your existing programmes, Universal Design for Access means embedding digital access considerations into everything your organisation already does. Materials should be created assuming low digital literacy, with multiple formats. This is not about dumbing down — it is about recognising that people who most need your services are often the least digitally engaged, and your materials should meet them where they are.

*Device Access Loop* — A persistent issue for community organisations is that training people in digital skills without ensuring device and connectivity provision creates a frustrating loop: skills exist, but cannot be practiced or applied. Frameworks that couple digital skills development with device-lending programmes have significantly higher follow-through rates. The practical implication: if your organisation is running digital inclusion work, plan device and connectivity provision alongside skills delivery, even at minimal scale.

*Peer Navigator Model* — The most effective digital inclusion programmes in community settings are led by peer navigators — community members who have overcome similar barriers and who deliver support in informal, trusted settings. This model is cost-effective, scalable, and builds social capital alongside digital skills. Organisations should consider embedding a peer navigator role rather than contracting external exclusively technical training.

**3.3 London-Specific Funding and Support**

- **Good Things Foundation** runs the National Digital Inclusion Network, with active London-based partners. They connect organisations with training resources, devices, and sometimes matched funding.
- **Greater London Authority (GLA)** has underfunding rounds for digital inclusion through its Skills for Londoners framework. Keep monitoring the GLA Community Grants portal — digital inclusion criteria appear in multiple funding streams, not always explicitly named.
- **Local borough digital inclusion strategies** — many London boroughs have strategies and small grants. These are poorly publicised and worth actively canvassing your local council's Digital or Communities team directly.
- **Corporate partnerships around device donations** — London-based tech firms and banks run periodic device donation schemes. The challenge is timing and eligibility: register for announcements from organisations like City Bridge Foundation and corporate social responsibility newsletters.

**3.4 Implementation Considerations**

Digital inclusion work faces a recurring bottleneck: sustaining engagement once programmes end. Initial enthusiasm is high but attrition is steep when support is withdrawn. Organisations should design programmes with embedded sustainability mechanisms — transitioning peer navigators to paid roles over time, connecting participants to free public Wi-Fi mapping, and establishing ongoing informal support groups than can outlast a funded programme. Also, note accreditation pathways: while digital badges and functional skills qualifications can motivate engagement, they should be integrated organically rather than bolted on, or they risk becoming another barrier.

**3.5 Intersectionality Notes**

Digital exclusion does not exist in isolation. It is deeply entangled with language barriers (immigrant populations who are also digitally unconnected), age-related vulnerability (older Londoners displaced by online-only services), and disability (auxiliary technology and accessible design are part of the same problem). If your organisation's core mission sits under Educational Equity or Climate Adaptation, these populations are likely the same people. An intersectional approach — serving people rather than narrowly defined programme categories — will be more effective and more fundable.

**4. EDUCATIONAL EQUITY**

**4.1 Why This Matters for London Community Organisations**

Educational inequality in London is structural and stubborn. Despite the city having some of the highest-performing schools in the country alongside some of the lowest, the gap in attainment between children from the most and least deprived backgrounds remains wider in the capital than the national average in several key metrics. Community organisations sit in that gap: many London families rely on supplementary schooling, after-school clubs, community learning hubs, and youth services that local authorities have progressively defunded. For community organisations, educational equity work can mean the difference between a child falling through the system and a child receiving the support they need — whether that is homework help, second-language support, specialist tuition, or simply a safe and consistent learning environment.

In London specifically, the challenge is compounded by diversity: many community organisations serve densely multilingual populations, refugees and asylum-seeking families, children in temporary accommodation, and looked-after children across multiple boroughs. Each of these groups faces distinct barriers to educational participation and attainment, and generic tutoring programmes often miss the mark.

**4.2 Key Frameworks**

*Whole-Child Approach* — Effective educational equity work recognises that a child's ability to learn is shaped by far more than curriculum delivery. Factors such as housing stability, food security, parental mental health, and freedom from stress are foundational to cognitive function and school engagement. Community organisations with wraparound ambitions — offering not just academic support but connecting families to housing advice, food provision, and emotional wellbeing services — are better positioned to produce genuinely improved outcomes. The framework asks: what does this child need to be ready to learn, and how can your organisation deliver it or signpost to it?

*Supplementary School and Community Education Models* — London has a rich, decades-long tradition of supplementary schooling — community-run out-of-school provision typically serving specific ethnic or cultural groups, running on evenings and weekends. Organisations operating in this space should understand that this is not about replacing mainstream schools but about offering culturally and linguistically responsive education that state systems may not provide. The Supplementary Schools Forum advocates for quality standards and training for supplementary school staff, and there is growing recognition from some mainstream schools and local authorities that community-based learning environments can be genuine partners in closing attainment gaps.

*Trauma-Informed Learning* — For London's refugee, asylum-seeking, and displaced populations — who represent a significant and growing share of the communities served by educational equity organisations — trauma-informed practice is not optional. This means understanding how trauma affects concentration, memory, trust, and behaviour; training staff and volunteers to recognise signs of trauma; and designing learning environments that prioritise safety, predictability, and choice. Doing this well requires sensitivity, but doing it poorly — without awareness — can inadvertently retraumatise learners and reinforce disengagement.

*Asset-Based Community Development in Education* — Rather than framing the children and communities you serve as "lacking," this framework starts from their existing strengths, languages, cultural knowledge, and social networks. Educational programmes that build on what families already bring — rather than treating them as starting from nothing — tend to achieve greater trust, participation, and trust. For London's diverse communities, multilingual practices, cultural recognition in curriculum materials, and family engagement strategies all flow from this orientation, as treating families as assets rather than merely recipients of help.

*The Attainment Gap Benchmarking Framework* — Many funders and assessment programmes now require community education providers to track progress against the Department for Education's attainment gaps. For community organisations, this means collecting baseline data, setting measurable targets, and using the established framework to demonstrate impact. It is worthwhile as both an accountability mechanism and a tool for continuous improvement, though it should be implemented proportionally — overly burdensome tracking can drain resources from frontline work. The key is to find lightweight metrics that capture genuine progress.

**4.3 London-Specific Funding and Support**

- **Education Endowment Foundation (EEF)** — offers grants specifically for programmes supporting disadvantaged students, including community learning initiatives. Their website maintains a searchable grant database alongside evidence summaries that can strengthen your applications.
- **The Paul Hamlyn Foundation** — funds education and learning, particularly for young people experiencing disadvantage, with an emphasis on innovative approaches and long-term change.
- **GLA's youth and skills funding** — the Mayor of London periodically opens funding rounds targeting skills development and youth engagement. Community organisations running educational programmes for young people should monitor the GLA's funding pages directly; rounds shift periodically.
- **Local authority education grants** — many London boroughs offer small grants for community learning, supplementary schooling, and youth activities, though eligibility criteria and budgets vary significantly. Directly contacting your local councillor responsible for education can surface opportunities not publicly advertised.
- **National Lottery Community Fund** — continues to fund education and learning projects in London, with specific regional programmes supporting children and young people. Their flexible funding can support core costs, which is unusual and valuable for small organisations.
- **The John Lyon's Charity** — funds educational, arts, and sports projects for young people in North and West London specifically, including Harrow, Brent, Barnet, Camden, Ealing, Hammersmith and Fulham, Kensington and Chelsea, and Westminster.

*Caution on currency*: funding programmes change periodically. The details above are valid as of this guide's creation, but organisations should verify current eligibility and deadlines directly with each funder.

**4.4 Implementation Considerations**

Safeguarding is the non-negotiable foundation of any educational programme. Every organisation delivering educational services to children and young people must maintain robust safeguarding policies, Designated Safeguarding Lead coverage, and staff/volunteer vetting. This is not a formality — it protects children, protects your organisation's legal standing, and is a requirement of almost all funders. Invest in safeguarding training early and renew it regularly.

Volunteer recruitment and quality management is a recurring challenge. Community education often relies heavily on volunteers whose backgrounds range from retired teachers to university students with good intentions but limited training. Establishing a clear onboarding process — including safeguarding briefings, guidance on teaching practice, and ongoing supervision — is essential to programme quality.

Outcome measurement should be proportionate. While funders increasingly require evidence of impact, the data collection burden should not overshadow the work itself. Focus on tracking methods that are embedded in the programme rather than bolted on: attendance registers, simple pre- and post-assessments, retention rates over time, and qualitative feedback from young people and families.

**4.5 Intersectionality Notes**

The children most likely to benefit from community educational support are the same children most likely to experience digital exclusion and climate vulnerability. Young people in temporary accommodation, for instance, are frequently the same group who lack reliable internet access (Section 3) and who live in overcrowded housing more vulnerable to heat and flooding (Section 5). When your organisation designs an educational programme, assuming the Population Is Uniform Is a non-strategic error — those who most need your help often face multiple compounding disadvantages simultaneously, and programmes that address this as a system produce far better outcomes than those that treat each issue in isolation.

Educational attainment is also closely linked to climate displacement; London welcomes significant numbers of school-age children displaced by climate events, who bring educational gaps, language barriers, and trauma that sit at the intersection of equity, language support, and adaptation. When planning your programmes, build in the flexibility to support learners whose needs cross these boundaries.

**5. LOCAL CLIMATE ADAPTATION**

**5.1 Why This Matters for London Community Organisations**

London is among the European capitals most vulnerable to climate impacts, and the people who bear those impacts first and hardest are precisely the communities your organisation already serves. The urban heat island effect makes London 4–8°C warmer than surrounding rural areas during heatwaves — a disparity that falls most heavily on boroughs with the least green space and the most densely packed housing stock. Surface water flooding affects increasing numbers of London boroughs, particularly outer London where impermeable paving is extensive. Air quality, unseasonable rainfall, and the cumulative stress of these impacts on already disadvantaged populations make climate adaptation not a future concern but a present operational reality.

For community organisations, climate adaptation means protecting the people you serve and your own ability to keep operating. If your community building overheats beyond usability on summer days, your educational and support programmes stop too. If the families you work with are displaced by flooding, your educational equity work loses its participants. This is why adaptation doesn't sit comfortably in a silo — but we address that convergence in the Intersectionality Strand. Here, we focus on what climate adaptation looks like at the community level: practical, affordable, achievable.

**5.2 Key Frameworks**

*Community Resilience Approach* — Rather than treating climate adaptation as a specialised engineering challenge, a community resilience framework asks: what does your community need to anticipate, absorb, and recover from climate shocks? For a small community organisation, this means mapping local vulnerabilities (where are the elderly and immobile residents? does your building have adequate cooling or ventilation?), developing local early warning (neighbourhood care networks, outreach phone trees), and building capacity to respond when events occur. It is eminently achievable and does not require technical expertise — it requires organisation, care, and coordination.

*Nature-Based Solutions at Community Scale* — Community organisations can be genuine catalysts for nature-based climate adaptation: working with local councils and funders to advocate for street trees, rain gardens in public spaces, green corridors linking housing to parks. Research consistently shows that greater urban canopy cover correlates with reduced heat exposure and improved mental health. Community organisations are well positioned to identify where interventions should go, engage residents in stewardship, and build the social infrastructure around the green infrastructure. Begin by identifying under-greened sites in your neighbourhood and opening a conversation with your local council's Parks or Climate team.

*Climate Justice Lens* — The households that contribute least to greenhouse gas emissions are those worst affected by heat, flooding, and pollution. In London, this means that the communities most likely to use your services — low-income families, people in precarious housing, racialised communities — are facing the brunt of climate change without having caused it. A climate justice approach centres their needs and voices in adaptation planning, rather than imposing top-down 'solutions' designed by those who experience the risks least. This lens strengthens community trust, justifies funding applications, and produces better outcomes.

*Heat Action Planning* — Develop a practical plan for extreme heat events: ensuring community buildings can be cooled (portable fans, early opening for cool shelter), proactive outreach to elderly residents and people with health conditions, distributing drinking water, and clear communication about when and where your organisation is open for refuge. These plans can be developed over days, not months, and they save lives.

**5.3 London-Specific Funding and Support**

- **Greater London Authority (GLA) environmental programmes** — the GLA runs green infrastructure, community green spaces, and climate resilience initiatives, though funding rounds are periodic and competitive. Organisations should monitor the GLA's funding pages closely and consider teaming up with neighbouring organisations on joint bids, which funders tend to favour.
- **Groundwork Trust (London and South East)** supports community environmental projects, including urban greening and climate awareness work, with grants and practical support.
- **London Wildlife Trust** partners with community organisations on habitat creation, green corridors, and nature-focused projects that serve climate adaptation goals.
- **Borough-level climate and resilience funding** — several London boroughs have dedicated climate action funding, though availability varies enormously. Enquire directly with your council's climate change officer.
- **National Lottery Community Fund** supports environmental projects in London communities, with an increasing appetite for community-led initiatives.

*Plain honesty*: community-level climate adaptation funding in the UK is thin relative to infrastructure and engineering budgets. Organisations should look to cross-programme funding (see Section 6) and consider making climate adaptation a component of your existing education or digital programmes rather than framing it as a standalone, new initiative. This increases feasibility and is often more fundable.

**5.4 Implementation Considerations**

Community organisations are uniquely trusted by the residents they serve — but this means operational continuity during climate events matters enormously. If your building loses power or becomes unworkable, you're gone. Consider: portable power and cooling capacity; insurance that covers climate-related disruption; and a communication chain so your community knows when services are suspended and where alternative support is available.

A common frustration in climate adaptation work is working with technical partners (engineers, surveyors, environmental designers) who may not understand community realities. Invest time in finding partners who genuinely collaborate, not just consult. Language barriers, cultural norms, and power asymmetries require work on the technical partner's side — never expect your community to bridge that gap alone.

**5.5 Intersectionality Notes**

The Londoners most vulnerable to heat and flooding are also those facing the greatest digital exclusion and educational barriers: families in overcrowded social housing facing extreme heat, asylum-seeking families in temporary accommodation near flood-prone areas, older residents who are both offline and at greater risk from heat. Your climate adaptation work should begin from the same starting point as your other programmes: the people. See Section 6.

**6. INTERSECTIONALITY STRAND**

**6.1 Why Intersections Are Not Optional**

Most London community organisations effectively operate intersectionally whether they name it or not. If your organisation focuses on educational equity, the children you serve are likely experiencing digital exclusion and living in housing acutely vulnerable to heat and flooding. If you work on digital inclusion, your beneficiaries are often the same families struggling with school engagement and facing practical barriers to engaging with climate preparedness. Splitting these populations artificially — treating digital skills work, educational support, and climate work as entirely separate — meant no untenable duplication, wasted resources, and services that miss the people who need them most.

This section offers consolidated frameworks and practical advice for operating across domains without collapsing under complexity.

**6.2 The Integrated Services Model**

Rather than running three separate programmes, build one support infrastructure around people and families. Under this model, your organisation has a single, coordinated intake process that asks about needs across domains holistically, a shared case management approach, and staff or volunteers who are cross-trained — not siloed. This does not mean doing everything in one session; it means knowing enough to recognise when a person's needs cross domains and having the organisational architecture to respond even if different team members handle different aspects. The integrated model is more fundable, more efficient, and more genuinely responsive than sliced-services approaches.

**6.3 Cross-Domain Funding Strategies**

Many funders — Esmée Fairbairn Foundation, Paul Hamlyn Foundation, National Lottery Community Fund — are increasingly receptive to programmes that address multiple needs under a single, coherent framework. When crafting proposals:

- Frame your work around people, not programmatic categories. "Supporting families in temporary accommodation with digital skills, educational continuity, and heat preparedness" tells a stronger story than three separate bids.
- Show how the work is integrated at the organisational level — shared intake, shared outcomes framework, shared team.
- Demonstrate the financial case: integrated delivery is cheaper per beneficiary than siloed programmes, which funders appreciate.

**6.4 Shared Implementation Barriers and Sustaining Factors**

Three challenges recur across all domains and deserve sustained attention:

*Volunteer and staff wellbeing* — Community organisations run on people who care passionately, and burnout is endemic when they absorb the weight of systemic failure. Protecting your team means realistic workloads, regular debriefing, recognition, and succession planning so that the organisation survives individual departures.

*Safeguarding* — When you work across domains, safeguarding responsibilities compound. But the infrastructure is the same: a trusted DSL, robust vetting, clear reporting lines, and staff who are never too stretched to prioritise it.

*Partnership and advocacy* — No single organisation can fill the gaps left by failing systems. Invest in relationships with other community organisations, local councillors, schools, and health services. When you advocate together — for better housing, for green infrastructure, for broadband equity — you are more audible and more effective.
