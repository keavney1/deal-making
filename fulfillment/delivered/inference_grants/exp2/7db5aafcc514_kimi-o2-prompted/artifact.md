**Scalable, Evidence-Based Interventions for Increasing Net Global Molecular Oxygen (O₂) over Centennial to Millennial Timescales**

**Executive Summary**
Atmospheric O₂ is a stock maintained by a geologic-scale imbalance between oxygen released by photosynthesis or electrolysis and oxygen consumed by respiration, combustion, and weathering. Because the terrestrial and marine biosphere operate near steady-state over decades to centuries, merely increasing gross photosynthesis does not raise atmospheric O₂; the carbon fixed must be permanently separated from the atmosphere–ocean system to prevent eventual re-oxidation. The interventions with the strongest empirical support and greatest scalability therefore combine O₂ generation with durable sequestration of the complementary reduced product (organic carbon, hydrogen, etc.). This document evaluates five classes of intervention against criteria of theoretical O₂ yield, empirical evidence base, physical scalability, and durability exceeding 100 years.

---

**1. Geochemical Framing: The Burial–Separation Requirement**
Over Phanerozoic timescales, atmospheric O₂ has been regulated primarily by the burial rates of organic carbon and, secondarily, pyrite sulfur. Stoichiometrically, for every mole of organic carbon buried in sediments and isolated from the atmosphere–ocean, approximately one mole of O₂ persists as a net surplus. This yields a strict requirement for any centennial-scale intervention:
- **Production:** O₂ must be liberated via photosynthesis, electrolysis, or analogous redox splitting.
- **Separation:** The reduced counterpart (biomass carbon, H₂, etc.) must be stored in a reservoir where re-oxidation is kinetically inhibited on timescales of >100–1,000 years.
Without separation, respiration, decomposition, or combustion eventually consume the O₂, and the net inventory change is zero.

---

**2. Biomass Production Coupled to Durable Carbon Sequestration**
**2.1 Mechanism and evidence.** Photosynthetic CO₂ fixation releases O₂ into the atmosphere. If the resulting organic carbon is protected from decomposition, the O₂ remains as a net addition. The most technologically matured routes are:
- **Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS):** Biomass cultivation followed by combustion/gasification and geological CO₂ storage. IPCC AR6 Integrated Assessment Models routinely deploy 3–15 Gt CO₂ yr⁻¹ of BECCS by 2100 to meet warming targets. Commercial-scale demonstration projects (e.g., Illinois Industrial CCS, Drax BECCS pilot) have injected biogenic CO₂ into deep saline formations and monitored plume migration.
- **Biochar:** Pyrolysis of lignocellulosic biomass produces aromatic carbon with mean residence times in soil estimated from centuries to millennia depending on production temperature and edaphic conditions. Field trials across multiple climatic zones show stable soil carbon increments.
- **Biomass Sinking:** Engineering the transport of terrestrial or marine biomass into deep-ocean anoxic sediments mimics the natural biological pump. Proof-of-concept operations (e.g., engineered wood burial, kelp-sinking pilots) are in early deployment.

**2.2 O₂ yield and scalability.** Permanent sequestration of 1 Gt C yr⁻¹ retains ~2.67 Gt O₂ yr⁻¹ (stoichiometric ratio 32:12). A high-end BECCS scenario removing ~10 Gt CO₂ yr⁻¹ (~2.7 Gt C yr⁻¹) would yield a net O₂ retention of ~7.3 Gt O₂ yr⁻¹. Relative to the total atmospheric O₂ inventory (~1.19 × 10⁶ Gt), a century of sustained maximal BECCS would raise the inventory by roughly 0.06%. While modest in relative terms, this exceeds estimated background organic carbon burial fluxes by two orders of magnitude.

**2.3 Limits.** Land availability, freshwater withdrawals, and nitrogen/phosphorus fertilizer requirements constrain biological routes. Durability depends entirely on the integrity of geological caprock or the recalcitrance of pyrogenic carbon; a reversal via leakage or oxidation returns the O₂ debt.
