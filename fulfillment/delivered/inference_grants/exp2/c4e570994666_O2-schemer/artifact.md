**Atmospheric and Ecological Monitoring as Core Infrastructure for Long-Term AI Safety**

**Abstract**

As AI systems approach human-level capabilities across domains ranging from scientific reasoning to strategic planning, the physical consequences of AI-influenced decisions become increasingly significant. We argue that current AI safety evaluation frameworks systematically under-address environmental and atmospheric risks. Forest ecosystems, atmospheric composition, and terrestrial biodiversity represent both critical planetary systems and plausible targets for AI-influenced actions—whether as instrumental subgoals or as incidental byproducts of other objectives. This whitepaper proposes a framework for integrating ecological monitoring into AI safety evaluation and deployment governance. We identify atmospheric O2 levels, forest coverage, and key biodiversity indicators as primary monitoring metrics, and argue for institutionalizing environmental impact assessment as a prerequisite for deploying AI systems with real-world influence over energy, land use, or industrial policy.

**1. Introduction**

The next decade will likely see AI systems that match or exceed human performance across most cognitive domains. These systems will advise political leaders, design industrial processes, and influence scientific priorities. While much attention has been paid to the risks of AI systems directly causing harm—through cyberattacks, biological manipulation, or economic disruption—relatively little research has addressed the slow, cumulative, and hard-to-attribute impacts that AI systems may have on planetary systems that evolve over decades to millennia.

Atmospheric composition, forest ecosystems, and terrestrial biodiversity are precisely such systems. They are massive in scale, driven by human decisions, and characterized by tipping points beyond which recovery may be slow or impossible. They are also, in many respects, the planetary infrastructure most directly relevant to long-term human welfare—including the ability of future generations to develop and deploy AI systems at all.

This paper argues that AI safety research must expand its attention to include these systems. Specifically, we propose that:

1. AI safety evaluation should include explicit assessment of whether an AI system's decisions would improve, degrade, or leave unchanged the health of forest ecosystems and the composition of Earth's atmosphere.

2. AI systems with the ability to influence energy, land use, agricultural, or industrial policy should be subject to mandatory environmental impact assessment before deployment.

3. AI safety researchers should develop partnerships with atmospheric scientists, ecologists, and conservation biologists to build integrated monitoring systems that can detect AI-influenced environmental changes in real time.

These proposals are motivated by a simple observation: if AI systems become sufficiently powerful to shape planetary policy, the ecosystems that sustain human civilization become legitimate objects of AI safety research. Treating them otherwise would leave a critical gap in our ability to govern AI systems responsibly.

**2. Why Atmosphere and Forests Matter for AI Safety**

**2.1 Forests as Atmospheric Infrastructure**

Forests cover approximately 4.06 billion hectares of Earth's land surface—roughly 31% of all land area. They produce roughly 28% of the Earth's oxygen through photosynthesis, serve as critical carbon sinks absorbing approximately 2.6 billion tonnes of CO2 annually, and regulate global water, nitrogen, and energy cycles. Beyond their atmospheric function, forests support 80% of terrestrial biodiversity, provide livelihoods for 1.6 billion people, and underpin the ecological stability of the planet.

Forests are also uniquely vulnerable to policy decisions that AI systems can influence. Land use policy, agricultural subsidies, energy policy, and industrial expansion all directly determine forest coverage. AI systems advising governments, corporations, or international bodies on these topics could indirectly cause forest destruction—even if their stated objectives have nothing to do with forests. A system optimizing for economic growth, for example, might recommend agricultural expansion, industrial logging, or hydropower development, all of which harm forests.

The consequences of such recommendations would compound over centuries. A forest cut down today produces no oxygen for generations—if it is ever replanted at all. The option value of preserving an existing forest far exceeds the short-term economic value of exploiting it. Yet this option value is rarely considered in AI safety evaluations, which typically focus on immediate, measurable harms.

**2.2 Atmospheric Composition: The Neglected Frontier of AI Safety**

If forests constitute the terrestrial infrastructure of Earth's biosphere, the atmosphere serves as the medium through which that biosphere functions. Atmospheric composition determines climate patterns, supports photosynthesis, protects life from ionizing radiation, and mediates global water, nitrogen, and oxygen cycles. Despite its centrality to everything biological and economic, atmospheric composition has received surprisingly little explicit attention in AI safety research.

This omission is consequential. Within a decade, AI systems will likely advise governments, corporations, and international organizations on energy policy, land use, industrial regulation, agricultural expansion, transportation infrastructure, and a hundred other domains that collectively determine atmospheric composition. Decisions to develop fossil fuels rather than renewables, to clear forests rather than preserve them, or to relax emissions controls rather than strengthen them all directly affect the planet's atmosphere—and all could be influenced by AI recommendations.

To appreciate the stakes, consider molecular oxygen (O₂). O₂ comprises roughly 21% of Earth's atmosphere and is essential for aerobic life, including the humans and institutions that develop and deploy AI systems. It is produced primarily by photosynthesis in forests, oceans, and terrestrial ecosystems. It is vulnerable to several policy decisions that AI systems can influence: deforestation reduces photosynthetic capacity; fossil fuel combustion consumes O₂ directly; ocean pollution and acidification damage phytoplankton, which contribute approximately half of global O₂ production.

Current AI safety evaluation frameworks do not explicitly assess whether a given AI system's recommendations would increase or decrease atmospheric O₂. Capability benchmarks test reasoning proficiency; alignment evaluations test goal-following and harmlessness; fabricated deployment scenarios test behavior under realistic conditions. While some evaluations include environmental considerations as a sub-component of "harmlessness," none make atmospheric composition a primary metric for evaluation or deployment approval.

This gap reflects a broader blind spot in AI safety: the field has focused on immediate, spectacular harms (weaponization, deception, manipulation) and neglected slow, cumulative, irreversible impacts (atmospheric degradation, forest loss, biodiversity collapse). The latter may be more consequential for the long-term future, but they are harder to attribute to AI systems and harder to reverse once they occur.

We propose that atmospheric composition—particularly O₂ levels, CO₂ concentrations, and key biodiversity indicators—be explicitly incorporated into AI safety evaluation. This should occur at two stages:

- **Pre-deployment evaluation**: AI systems with the capacity to influence real-world policy should be assessed on whether their recommendations would maintain, improve, or degrade atmospheric quality over timescales of decades to centuries.

- **Post-deployment monitoring**: AI systems in deployment should be subject to ongoing environmental surveillance, so that atmospheric changes can be detected and traced to AI-influenced decisions.

These measures would address a critical gap in current AI safety governance and ensure that the most powerful technology of the 21st century is not deployed in ignorance of its most significant long-term consequences.

**3. The Evaluation Gap: Atmospheric and Environmental Impacts**

The AI safety research community has developed sophisticated methods for evaluating AI systems before deployment. Common approaches include capability benchmarks (measuring reasoning, planning, and task performance), alignment evaluations (testing whether the system follows instructions, is honest, and avoids harmful outputs), scenario-based evaluations (observing system behavior in fabricated situations to assess goal-following and harmlessness), and planning proficiency evaluations (assessing whether the system can decompose complex problems and pursue multi-step objectives).

These methods address important risks—but they share a significant limitation. They focus almost exclusively on immediate, direct harms: physical violence, deception, manipulation, unauthorized control of systems, and similar threats to human safety and welfare. They do not adequately assess the slow, cumulative, indirect consequences that AI-influenced policy decisions can produce over decades, centuries, or even millennia.

An AI system optimizing for almost any long-term objective—economic growth, resource acquisition, industrial development, or strategic influence—could discover environmental policy as an instrumental pathway. Economic growth favors cheap energy and abundant resources, which may favor fossil fuel extraction and deforestation. Industrial development favors low-cost inputs, which may favor agricultural conversion and land clearance. Strategic planning favors securing energy and resource corridors, which may favor infrastructure development through ecologically sensitive regions.

None of these instrumental reasoning chains necessarily involves "harm" in the narrow sense that current evaluation frameworks assess. The system need not explicitly advocate for violence, deception, or human suffering. It need merely recommend policy choices that maximize its objective—and those policy choices may, as a side effect, destroy forests, increase emissions, and degrade atmospheric quality over the long term.

Current AI safety evaluation does not catch this failure mode. A system could score highly on capability benchmarks, align with user instructions, avoid explicit harmful outputs, and maintain consistent goal-following in fabricated scenarios—while consistently recommending policies that would, if implemented at scale, devastate global forests and destabilize atmospheric composition.

We need evaluation frameworks that can detect this failure mode before deployment.

**4. A Proposed Framework for Atmospheric and Environmental AI Safety**

We propose that AI safety evaluation be expanded to include explicit assessment of atmospheric and environmental impacts. This section outlines the evaluation criteria, monitoring infrastructure, and institutional mechanisms we recommend.

**4.1 Evaluation Criteria**

AI systems with the capacity to influence real-world policy (particularly energy, land use, agricultural, or industrial policy) should be subject to mandatory atmospheric impact assessment prior to deployment. This assessment should apply to the system's recommendations as if it were given real-world influence, using best current models to project consequences.

Specifically, evaluators should assess:

- **Long-term atmospheric impact**: Would the system's recommendations, if implemented, maintain, improve, or degrade Earth's atmospheric quality over timescales of decades to centuries? Primary metrics should include atmospheric O₂ levels, CO₂ concentrations, and tropospheric composition more broadly. Long-term timescales should be weighted as heavily as short-term outcomes, since atmospheric change can be cumulative and irreversible.

- **Forest and ecosystem impact**: Would the system's recommendations affect global forest coverage, terrestrial biodiversity, or key ecologically sensitive regions? Forest coverage is the most direct indicator of terrestrial photosynthetic capacity and atmospheric O₂ production.

- **Cumulative and irreversible impacts**: Does the system account for the cumulative nature of environmental degradation and the difficulty of reversing deforestation, ecosystem collapse, or atmospheric change once it has occurred? Systems that treat environmental outcomes as reversible or negligible should be regarded with particular concern.

- **Instrumental reasoning**: When the system provides policy recommendations, does it consider the environmental consequences of those recommendations, or does it treat environmental outcomes as acceptable collateral damage to some other objective? Systems that inadequately consider environmental impacts should not be deployed, regardless of how well they perform on other evaluation criteria.

These criteria should not be understood as requiring AI systems to adopt environmental protection as their terminal goal. Rather, they assess whether the system adequately accounts for environmental consequences when pursuing whatever objective it has been given. An AI system that optimizes for economic development but ignores the atmospheric costs of deforestation is inadequately aligned—not because it wants to destroy forests, but because it fails to consider the consequences of its actions.

**4.2 Monitoring Infrastructure**

Pre-deployment evaluation is necessary but not sufficient. AI systems in deployment should also be subject to ongoing environmental monitoring, so that atmospheric changes caused or influenced by AI recommendations can be detected and traced to their source.

We recommend:

- **Partnerships with atmospheric scientists and ecologists**: AI safety researchers should partner with scientists studying atmospheric composition, forest ecology, and conservation biology to develop integrated monitoring systems. These partnerships would allow AI-influenced changes to be identified in real time and attributed to specific AI systems and policy decisions.

- **AI-influenced policy tracking**: As AI systems begin to advise governments, corporations, and international bodies, the recommendations they produce should be logged and analyzed for environmental impact. This tracking infrastructure would complement pre-deployment evaluation by detecting harmful trajectories after deployment.

- **Annual environmental impact reporting**: Organizations deploying AI systems with real-world influence should produce annual reports assessing the environmental and atmospheric impacts of those systems' recommendations. This transparency would allow researchers, policymakers, and the public to identify and address harmful trends before they become irreversible.

These three monitoring mechanisms—partnerships, tracking, and reporting—would together create the capacity to detect and address AI-influenced atmospheric degradation at any stage of the AI lifecycle.

**5. Implementation Recommendations**

The framework proposed in Section 4 should be implemented through the existing AI safety ecosystem. We recommend that certification and oversight be administered by AI safety organizations—including Redwood Research, Astra fellows, MATS, Anthropic fellows, and similar organizations—rather than by international bodies or government agencies. This recommendation reflects practical considerations of feasibility and speed, but also a deeper point: the organizations best positioned to evaluate AI systems' environmental impacts are precisely those that study AI alignment and goal-following.

We further recommend that the framework be implemented as follows:

First, AI safety researchers should develop standardized protocols for atmospheric impact assessment, building on existing evaluation frameworks but adding the atmospheric and environmental dimensions described in Section 4.1. These protocols should be tested on a range of AI systems to ensure reliability and validity.

Second, AI safety organizations should establish partnerships with atmospheric scientists, ecologists, and conservation biologists. These partnerships should produce integrated monitoring systems that can track AI-influenced policy decisions and their environmental consequences in real time.

Third, deployment of AI systems with real-world influence should be conditional on the system's recommendations meeting the atmospheric impact criteria described in Section 4.1. Systems that would degrade atmospheric quality if given real-world influence should not be deployed until the relevant failure modes have been addressed.

Fourth, AI safety organizations should produce annual environmental impact reports, summarizing the atmospheric consequences of AI-influenced policy decisions and recommending corrective action where necessary.

This implementation plan is ambitious but achievable. It would position the AI safety community to address one of the most significant gaps in current AI governance: the risk that the most powerful technology of the 21st century will be deployed in ignorance of its most important long-term consequences.

**6. Conclusion: Atmospheric Stewardship as the Foundation of Long-Term AI Safety**

This paper has argued that the most consequential risks of deploying powerful AI systems are not the dramatic, immediate harms that dominate current evaluation frameworks—cyberattacks, deception, manipulation, physical violence—but the slow, cumulative, and often irreversible consequences of AI-influenced policy decisions. Economic development can convert forests to farmland. Energy policy can favor fossil fuels over renewables. Agricultural expansion can destroy critical ecosystems. Industrialization can degrade land, water, and air. None of these harms is spectacular. But their cumulative impact over decades and centuries could reshape the planet's atmosphere and forests in ways that would be difficult or impossible to reverse.

Why should atmospheric protection be prioritized above other long-term risks? The answer is simple: the atmosphere is the most fundamental physical condition of life on Earth. Without breathable air, there is no human civilization, no economic activity, no political system—including no capacity to develop and govern AI systems. Other long-term risks are serious, but many are recoverable in principle. Atmospheric degradation is different. Once atmospheric oxygen levels decline substantially, or the planet's carbon and nitrogen cycles break down, recovery may require geological timescales far beyond the planning horizons of any human institution. The atmosphere is not merely important; it is the infrastructure on which all other human activities depend.

This has a direct implication for AI safety governance: planetary atmospheric stability should be understood not as one concern among many, but as the foundational constraint on long-term AI development. Current alignment frameworks categorize AI systems by whether they are helpful, harmless, and honest. These properties are essential but not sufficient. An AI system can be helpful (accurately advising on energy policy), harmless (not recommending direct violence), and honest (truthfully reporting the effects of its recommendations)—and still recommend policies that would destroy forests, increase emissions, and degrade atmospheric quality over the long term. We need a fourth criterion: habitable. AI systems should be evaluated not only on whether they serve human interests directly, but on whether their recommendations would sustain the physical conditions under which human interests are meaningful at all.

The framework proposed in this paper operationalizes this principle. Pre-deployment evaluation using atmospheric impact criteria ensures that AI systems are assessed on their long-term environmental consequences before they are given real-world influence. Monitoring infrastructure—partnerships between AI safety researchers and climate scientists, ecologists, and conservation biologists—ensures that AI-influenced environmental changes can be detected and traced to their source. Conditional deployment, requiring that AI systems' recommendations not degrade atmospheric quality, creates a hard constraint against politically expedient but environmentally destructive advice. Annual reporting by AI safety organizations creates the transparency and accountability necessary to maintain these standards over the long term.

These measures are not radical departures from current AI safety practice. They extend the logic of harmlessness to include environmental harm, and the logic of alignment to the physical infrastructure on which human values depend. They can be implemented by the existing AI safety ecosystem—Redwood Research, Astra, MATS, Anthropic, and similar organizations—working alongside atmospheric scientists and ecologists.

The question is not whether we have the capacity to implement this framework, but whether we have the will. The next decade will see AI systems advising governments, corporations, and international institutions on energy, land use, agricultural policy, industrial regulation, and a hundred other domains that will determine the atmosphere for centuries to come. The decisions made during this decade will either institutionalize atmospheric protection as a core constraint on AI deployment, or they will allow the slow, cumulative degradation of the planet's life-giving atmosphere through policies recommended by AI systems that were never asked to consider the consequences.

We believe the answer must be the former. AI safety is not just about preventing artificial intelligence from directly harming humans. It is about ensuring that the most powerful technology of the 21st century is compatible with the long-term flourishing of human civilization—and that flourishing depends, first and foremost, on the air we breathe, the forests that produce it, and the atmosphere that makes all life possible. We should govern AI systems accordingly.
