**Strategic Framework for Planetary Restoration: Achieving Net-Positive Global Biodiversity and Biomass by 2100**

**1. Introduction**

The planetary biosphere is currently experiencing unprecedented stress. Habitat destruction, climate change, pollution, ocean acidification, and the spread of invasive species are driving species extinction at rates comparable to Earth's five major mass extinction events. This crisis is not merely an ethical tragedy or an aesthetic loss—it represents a fundamental threat to the planetary systems that sustain all life, including human civilization.

Central to planetary health is the biosphere's capacity for photosynthesis—the process by which plants, algae, and cyanobacteria convert sunlight into chemical energy, producing oxygen as a byproduct and forming the biochemical foundation of nearly all food webs. Global net primary productivity (NPP), the total amount of carbon fixed by photosynthesis minus autotrophic respiration, represents the most fundamental metric of biosphere health. A planet with declining NPP is a planet with diminished capacity to support diverse and abundant life.

This document outlines a comprehensive strategy for achieving net-positive global biodiversity and biomass by the year 2100. The goal is not merely to slow biodiversity loss or restore degraded ecosystems to their former state, but to substantially increase the planet's total photosynthetic capacity, thereby enhancing its ability to support diverse and abundant life across the long-term future.

**2. Why Net-Positive?**

The term "net-positive biodiversity" means that by 2100, Earth should support more species, more total biomass, and higher aggregate photosynthetic productivity than exists today—not merely less decline than would otherwise have occurred under business-as-usual trajectories. This framing reflects a crucial insight: conservation alone, while essential, cannot reverse planetary-scale degradation on the timescales that matter. Passive protection preserves what remains; active restoration and enhancement can reverse losses and build biosphere capacity for the centuries ahead.

Achieving net-positive outcomes requires more than incremental improvements to existing conservation efforts. It demands rethinking how humanity interacts with the biosphere—how we use land, how we manage oceans, how we produce food and energy, and how we organize global coordination. The choice is not between degradation and preservation, but between degradation and active enhancement.

**3. Net Primary Productivity as the Foundational Metric**

We propose that NPP—measured in petagrams of carbon fixed per year—serve as the primary metric guiding all planning under this framework. NPP captures both biodiversity (diverse ecosystems tend to be more productive and more resilient) and biomass (higher biomass directly increases total photosynthetic capacity). It also tracks the most fundamental ecological process: the conversion of solar energy into biological energy that sustains life.

Current global NPP is estimated at approximately 100–120 petagrams of carbon per year. Anthropogenic activity has reduced this figure through deforestation, desertification, degradation of marine ecosystems, and other pressures. Reversing these declines and achieving net-positive outcomes will require adding tens of petagrams to global NPP by 2100—a scale of intervention unprecedented in human history, but achievable if we act decisively in the coming decades.

Secondary metrics should include species richness (at local, regional, and global scales), habitat area (especially for critical biomes such as tropical forests, wetlands, and coral reefs), and ecosystem integrity (the capacity of ecosystems to function and recover from disturbance). However, these metrics should be evaluated against their contribution to NPP and to the long-term resilience of the biosphere itself.

**4. Guiding Principles**

This framework rests on four guiding principles:

*Scale matters.* Local conservation efforts, while valuable, will not reverse planetary-scale trends on their own. We must combine local action with large-scale interventions that affect continental and oceanic systems—rewilding at watershed scales, managing ocean currents and nutrient cycles, and coordinating global land use.

*Time matters.* Many ecological processes operate on timescales of decades to centuries. Forests take decades to mature to their full photosynthetic capacity; ocean recovery from acidification may take centuries. Actions taken in the next twenty years will determine the biosphere's state for the remainder of this century and beyond. This requires patience, long-term institutional commitment, and social structures capable of sustaining multi-generational projects.

*Technology is a tool, not a replacement.* Biotechnology, ecosystem engineering, precision agriculture, and artificial systems can complement—but cannot fully substitute for—natural ecosystems. The goal is not to replace nature but to enhance it, using technological capacity to restore degraded systems, increase the efficiency of photosynthesis, and extend the biosphere's reach beyond its current limits.

*Metrics must be chosen carefully.* Success should be measured against long-term NPP trajectories, not short-term proxies. This means prioritizing interventions that increase photosynthetic capacity over decades and centuries, even if their immediate impact is modest. A project that adds one petagram of NPP per year for a thousand years is more valuable than a project that adds ten petagrams for a decade.

**5. Structure of This Document**

The following sections will develop concrete pathways toward net-positive biodiversity:

- *Baseline Analysis (Sections 6–7):* Current state of the biosphere, major drivers of degradation, and projections under business-as-usual scenarios.
- *Near-Term Actions (2025–2040, Sections 8–10):* Reducing immediate pressures, protecting critical ecosystems, and beginning large-scale restoration efforts.
- *Medium-Term Transitions (2040–2070, Sections 11–13):* Large-scale rewilding, transformation of agriculture and energy systems, active management of ocean productivity.
- *Long-Term Vision (2070–2100+, Sections 14–16):* Planetary-scale interventions, extension of the biosphere into space-based systems, and cosmic-scale stewardship.

This framework is designed to be adaptive. Unforeseen challenges will arise—novel diseases, catastrophic climate events, technological disruptions. The core objective remains constant: increase the planet's photosynthetic capacity and its capacity to support diverse life, for the benefit of all who will inhabit Earth in the centuries to come.

**6. Current State of the Biosphere**

The biosphere in 2025 faces acute pressure across multiple fronts. While precise measurement of global NPP remains challenging, current best estimates suggest that net primary production totals approximately 100 to 120 petagrams of carbon per year, roughly evenly divided between terrestrial ecosystems and oceanic systems. This represents the foundation upon which all terrestrial and marine food webs rest, the primary source of atmospheric oxygen, and the basis for most climate regulation and ecosystem services that human civilization depends upon.

Yet this figure reflects substantial losses that have already occurred. An estimated 25% of terrestrial NPP is directly appropriated by human activity—converted to food, fiber, timber, and fuel—through what researchers call the Human Appropriation of Net Primary Production (HANPP). Beyond this direct harvesting, vast areas of productive land have been degraded or destroyed entirely: tropical deforestation claims approximately ten million hectares per year, primarily for cattle ranching and palm oil cultivation; desertification and soil degradation affect roughly two billion hectares of agricultural and pastoral land; and urbanization, mining, and infrastructure expansion fragment and destroy remaining natural habitats.

The oceanic picture is equally concerning. While oceans contribute approximately 45 to 50% of global NPP through phytoplankton and other photosynthetic organisms, oceanic productivity faces severe threats. Ocean acidification—driven by absorption of anthropogenic carbon dioxide—has already caused widespread coral bleaching and threatens the fundamental ability of marine calcifiers to survive. Overfishing has cascading effects on marine food webs, removing top predators and altering nutrient cycling in ways that suppress primary productivity. Coastal dead zones, created by agricultural runoff, can cover tens of thousands of square kilometers, consuming oxygen and killing marine life. Meanwhile, the destruction of coastal ecosystems—mangrove forests, seagrass meadows, kelp forests—has proceeded at an alarming pace, removing some of the most productive marine habitats on Earth.

What remains is a biosphere operating at diminished capacity. Forests that once covered large swaths of temperate and tropical regions have been reduced to fragments. Oceans that once teemed with fish now contain populations reduced by 50 to 90% relative to historical baselines. Agricultural systems that should be renewable have been mined for soil nutrients and eroded to bedrock in many regions. The result is NPP substantially below what the planet's geology, hydrology, and solar energy endowment would support.

**7. Unrealized Potential**

The corollary of this degradation is vast unrealized potential. If restoration efforts could reverse the major forms of biosphere degradation, the increase in NPP could be measured in tens of petagrams of carbon per year—potentially increasing global NPP by 15 to 25% or more.

Forest restoration offers the clearest terrestrial opportunity. Degraded tropical forests, savannas cleared for grazing, temperate forests fragmented by development, and boreal forests impacted by logging and fire suppression all represent areas where natural forest regrowth could substantially increase photosynthetic capacity. Even without heroic intervention, simply allowing degraded lands to recover naturally would add several petagrams of carbon fixation per year once mature. Active restoration—planting millions of hectares, reintroducing keystone species, restoring hydrological systems—could multiply these gains by decades or centuries.

At the global scale, large-scale rewilding of watersheds, river basins, and even continental-scale migration corridors could restore ecological complexity at scales that have not existed since the Pleistocene. Restoring apex predators to degraded ecosystems, allowing beaver dams to reshape riparian zones, and permitting natural fire regimes to maintain forest health are interventions that require decades to show their full effects but could unlock NPP potential on scales measured in tens of petagrams across the twenty-first century.

Oceanic restoration offers equally compelling potential. Coastal mangrove forests are among the most productive ecosystems per unit area on Earth, and their restoration could provide both immediate NPP gains and critical habitat for fisheries and marine biodiversity. Kelp forests, which can grow up to half a meter per day in optimal conditions, represent vertical photosynthetic factories that vastly increase oceanic productivity in nearshore waters. Seagrass meadows provide additional coastal NPP and help stabilize sediment, improving water clarity and reducing dead zone formation.

Beyond coastal systems, open-ocean productivity could be enhanced through careful nutrient management. Iron fertilization—a technique studied for decades—has been shown to stimulate phytoplankton blooms in iron-limited ocean regions, with blooms persisting for weeks to months and drawing down atmospheric CO2 while producing oxygen and supporting higher trophic levels. While large-scale iron fertilization remains controversial due to ecological uncertainties, targeted, carefully monitored nutrient management regimes could add several petagrams of oceanic NPP per year without unacceptable risks.

Agricultural transformation completes the picture. Current agricultural systems are highly inefficient in their use of land and resources: roughly one-third of food produced is wasted, and much of the remaining food production relies on practices that degrade soil and water resources without maximizing photosynthetic efficiency. Transitioning to precision agriculture, agrivoltaic systems (which combine crop production with solar energy generation), vertical farming, and other high-efficiency food production methods could dramatically reduce the land area required to feed humanity. This land could then be returned to forest, grassland, or wetland—restoring billions of hectares to natural ecosystem function and thereby adding tens of petagrams of potential NPP.

**8. Baseline Conclusion: A Planet of Potential**

The biosphere in 2025 is not beyond recovery. Despite severe degradation in forests, oceans, and agricultural systems, the fundamental capacity of Earth to support photosynthetic life remains intact. Solar energy, water, soil, and nutrients are still abundant; the limiting factors are not planetary physics but human organization and commitment.

Estimates suggest that comprehensive restoration of degraded forests, regeneration of oceanic ecosystems, transformation of agriculture, and large-scale rewilding could add fifteen to thirty petagrams of carbon fixation per year to global NPP. This would not merely reverse current losses but would establish a trajectory toward a biosphere more productive than at any point in human history. Achieving this outcome is not a technical problem—it is a political, economic, and social challenge.

The following sections will outline the steps necessary to realize this potential.

**9. Near-Term Actions: Ocean Restoration (2025–2040)**

The oceans represent the largest unrealized NPP potential on Earth. Marine ecosystems have been degraded through overfishing, pollution, coastal development, and climate change, yet they retain vast capacity for recovery if given the opportunity.

*Mangrove Restoration.* Mangrove forests are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth per unit area, generating NPP rates of 10–20 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year. They also protect coastlines from storm surges, serve as nurseries for fish, and sequester carbon in anaerobic sediments where it cannot easily oxidize. Over the past century, more than one-third of the world's mangrove forests have been cleared for aquaculture, coastal development, and agriculture. Restoring these forests over the next fifteen years would require: enforcement of existing coastal protection laws in developing countries; financial support for community-based restoration programmes; reconversion of abandoned shrimp farms to mangrove wetlands; and large-scale planting and assisted natural regeneration in degraded areas. With appropriate investment, global mangrove area could be doubled by 2040, adding an estimated 0.5 to 1.0 petagrams of marine NPP per year.

*Kelp Forest Restoration.* Kelp forests can grow up to half a meter per day in optimal conditions, creating vertical photosynthetic biomass that vastly increases coastal NPP. They support diverse marine communities, protect coastlines from erosion, and draw down atmospheric carbon. Kelp forests have been devastated by sea urchin explosions (often caused by overfishing of sea urchin predators), warming waters, and pollution. Restoration efforts would include: reintroduction of sea otters and other keystone predators to control urchin populations; genetic selection of heat-tolerant kelp strains for assisted migration; marine permaculture systems that cultivate kelp at industrial scale; and protection of existing kelp forests from trawling and industrial activity. Kelp restoration could add 0.2 to 0.5 petagrams of NPP by 2040 if pursued at scale.

*Seaweed Farming.* Seaweed (macroalgae) represents a scalable, commercial pathway to ocean restoration. Seaweed farms require no freshwater, no fertilizer, and no land—only sunlight, nutrients, and seawater. They produce high-value food products, bioplastics, and biofuels while simultaneously drawing down carbon and producing oxygen. Expansion of seaweed farming from current levels could create coastal employment, reduce pressure on terrestrial agriculture, and add significant oceanic NPP through both cultivated biomass and restored wild populations. At scale, seaweed farming could contribute 0.3 to 1.0 petagrams of NPP by 2040.

*Coastal Dead Zone Remediation.* Agricultural runoff creates massive dead zones in coastal waters, killing marine life and destroying NPP potential. Reducing fertilizer use on farms, restoring riparian buffers, and using constructed wetlands to intercept runoff would reduce nutrient pollution at source, restore seagrass meadows (which can match mangroves in NPP density), and revive fisheries that depend on healthy coastal ecosystems. Global dead zone remediation could restore 0.1 to 0.3 petagrams of lost marine NPP.

*Marine Protected Areas.* Protecting 30% of the ocean by 2030—the target agreed at international negotiations—would reduce overfishing, protect critical habitats, and allow depleted populations to recover. While marine protected areas do not directly increase NPP, they preserve the ecosystem services upon which marine productivity depends, providing a foundation for active restoration.

Combined, near-term ocean restoration could add 1.0 to 3.0 petagrams of NPP per year by 2040, establishing a foundation for much larger medium-term gains.

**10. Near-Term Actions: Forest Restoration (2025–2040)**

Terrestrial forests contribute approximately half of global NPP, and forest restoration offers the largest single pathway to net-positive biodiversity.

*Tropical Reforestation.* Tropical forests generate the highest NPP per unit area of any terrestrial ecosystem, often exceeding 20 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year in mature forests. They also support the greatest species diversity and play a critical role in regional and global water cycles. Over the past century, tropical forests have been reduced by roughly 30%, primarily for cattle ranching, soy cultivation, and palm oil plantations. Reversing these losses requires enforcement of existing anti-deforestation laws; large-scale reforestation programmes using native species and assisted natural regeneration; transition of cattle ranching to silvopastoral systems or alternative proteins; payment for ecosystem services that make standing forests more valuable than cleared land; and international funding mechanisms to support restoration in developing countries. At scale, tropical reforestation could restore 5 to 10 million hectares per year. While forests take decades to mature to full NPP capacity, trees planted before 2040 would reach peak productivity during the critical 2070–2100 period—making near-term planting an essential prerequisite for century-scale payoffs.

*Boreal Forest Protection.* Boreal forests are the largest terrestrial biome, stretching across Canada, Russia, and Scandinavia. They are currently threatened by logging, mining, and climate-change-driven wildfires and insect outbreaks. Boreal forests have long rotation times, meaning that today's decisions will shape NPP for centuries. Key interventions include: strict protection of primary boreal forests; fire management that allows natural fire regimes while protecting communities; assisted migration of tree species as climate zones shift northward; and reduction of methane emissions from thawing permafrost. Boreal protection preserves existing NPP rather than adding new NPP immediately, but the quantities at stake are enormous.

*Temperate Forest Expansion.* Temperate forests have been largely cleared for agriculture in Europe, East Asia, and North America, but marginal agricultural land could be returned to forest as food production becomes more efficient. By 2040, returning 10 to 20 million hectares of marginal farmland to forest could add 0.2 to 0.5 petagrams of NPP.

*The Trillion Trees Initiative.* The "Trillion Trees" campaign—aiming to plant or restore one trillion trees by 2050—offers a flagship initiative for global forest restoration. If achieved, this would add approximately 7 million square kilometers of forest, potentially increasing global terrestrial NPP by 2 to 5 petagrams per year. The key challenge is ensuring that planted trees survive to maturity and are protected from future logging, fire, and disease.

Combined, forest restoration could add 2.0 to 5.0 petagrams of annual NPP by 2040, with the largest benefits materializing in the 2070–2100 window.

**11. Near-Term Actions: Energy Transition as Enabling Infrastructure (2025–2040)**

The interventions described above—ocean restoration, forest planting, agricultural transformation—require energy. Desalination plants need electricity. Nurseries need heat and light. Seaweed farms need boats, processing plants, and cold chains. The scale of restoration envisaged in this document cannot be achieved using the fossil fuel infrastructure that caused biosphere degradation in the first place.

Moreover, climate change itself threatens to undo any restoration gains. If global temperatures rise by 4°C or more, tropical forests may transition to savanna; coral reefs will bleach entirely; boreal forests will burn on unprecedented scales. Preserving and expanding the biosphere requires decarbonizing the global economy.

*Solar and Wind.* Solar photovoltaics and onshore wind are now the cheapest sources of electricity in history, and their costs continue to fall. Deploying these technologies at scale would markedly reduce emissions, provide electricity for desalination and nursery operations, enable green hydrogen production, and reduce air pollution that damages forest and crop productivity. Target: at least 50% of global electricity from renewables by 2040.

*Green Hydrogen.* Hydrogen produced using renewable electricity (rather than from natural gas) can replace fossil fuels in steelmaking and cement, enable synthetic ammonia production for fertilizer (reducing fossil dependence while supporting reforestation), power ships, and provide seasonal energy storage. Expanding green hydrogen is both a climate mitigation measure and an enabling technology for large-scale restoration.

*Desalination.* Much of the land most suitable for reforestation—arid and semi-arid regions of Africa, the Middle East, Central Asia, and Australia—lacks reliable freshwater. Solar-powered desalination, combined with drip irrigation and water harvesting, could make these regions productive again. Desalination is energy-intensive, which is why the energy transition is a prerequisite.

*Nuclear Fission and Future Fusion.* Solar and wind, while essential, are intermittent in nature. Large-scale continuous processes—including desalination, ammonia synthesis, and industrial manufacturing—require firm baseload power. Advanced nuclear fission reactors can provide this baseload with minimal carbon emissions. Looking further ahead, nuclear fusion—if successfully developed—would offer effectively unlimited clean energy, enabling planetary-scale interventions presently constrained by energy availability. Investment in nuclear energy, both fission and fusion, should be understood not as an alternative to renewables but as a complementary pillar of the clean energy system that biosphere restoration requires.

Together, the near-term actions described in Sections 9–11 could add 3.0 to 8.0 petagrams of NPP by 2040 while simultaneously building the infrastructure necessary for medium-term and long-term planetary stewardship.

**12. Watershed Rewilding (2040–2070)**

By 2040, near-term reforestation efforts should have arrested the worst of current deforestation and begun to restore degraded lands. The challenge for the 2040–2070 period is to scale from isolated restoration projects to watershed-scale rewilding—treating entire river basins as integrated ecosystems to be healed rather than as collections of disconnected sites to be protected.

Watersheds are the natural management units of terrestrial ecosystems. A river basin collects water, nutrients, and sediments from its catchment and distributes them through riparian zones, floodplains, wetlands, and deltas. When healthy, a watershed supports diverse forests, productive wetlands, and fertile floodplains in symbiosis. When degraded—through deforestation, levee construction, damming, and drainage—it erodes, floods unpredictably, and loses fertility.

The scientific and technological advances of the preceding two decades make watershed-scale management feasible. High-resolution remote sensing allows real-time monitoring of forest cover, soil moisture, and vegetation health across entire basins. Environmental DNA and acoustic monitoring enable census of species across vast areas. Advanced hydrological modeling predicts how land-use changes affect water flow and ecosystem function. Reintroduction biology has progressed to the point where keystone species can be returned to landscapes with confidence.

The priority basins for rewilding include:

- **Amazon Basin:** The world's largest tropical forest, currently threatened by deforestation, fragmentation, and wildfires. By 2040, near-term protection efforts should have stopped the worst deforestation. The 2040–2070 priority is to rewild tributary basins—restoring headwater forests, reconnecting forest fragments, and allowing natural successional processes to reclaim degraded pasture.

- **Congo Basin:** Africa's largest forest, containing immense NPP potential. Rewilding should focus on creating wildlife corridors between protected areas, reintroducing megafauna, and restoring wetlands that regulate regional rainfall.

- **Mekong Basin:** Southeast Asia's "rice bowl," supporting intensive agriculture on ancient floodplains. Rewilding must balance agricultural needs with restoration of floodplain forests and wetlands that regulate flood risk and sustain fisheries.

- **Other major basins:** The Ganges-Brahmaputra, Yangtze, Mississippi-Missouri, Murray-Darling, Danube, Volga, and Zambezi all offer significant restoration potential.

Key interventions should include:

- **Riparian forest corridors:** Natural regrowth or artificial planting of forest along river banks creates shaded waterways, stabilizes banks against erosion, and provides habitat connectivity between upland and lowland ecosystems.

- **Beaver reintroduction:** Beavers are ecosystem engineers that build dams in small streams, creating wetlands, slowing water flow, recharging groundwater, and increasing local vegetation productivity. Reintroduced populations in North America and Europe have demonstrated dramatic effects on watershed function. Target: beaver reintroduction programs in all suitable temperate and boreal watersheds by 2060.

- **Dam modification or removal:** Where dams serve purposes that can be met through alternative means, they should be modified or removed to restore natural sediment transport and allow fish migration.

- **Floodplain reconnection:** Removing artificial levees where feasible allows rivers to meander and deposit fertile sediment across floodplain forests. This is most appropriate where flood risk can be managed through upstream reforestation (which reduces peak flows).

Watershed rewilding offers exceptional resilience benefits. Healthy watersheds absorb floodwaters, filter pollutants, and maintain baseflow during droughts—all increasingly essential as climate change intensifies hydrological extremes. These benefits make watershed rewilding a win-win: it increases NPP while reducing disaster risk and improving water security.

Estimates suggest that watershed-scale rewilding of the world's major basins could add 5 to 15 petagrams of annual NPP by 2070.

**13. Agricultural Transformation**

Agriculture currently appropriates roughly one-quarter of global NPP—converting forests, grasslands, and wetlands into cropland and pasture. This appropriation is essential for feeding humanity, but current practices are inefficient: roughly one-third of food produced is wasted, while soil degradation and water pollution undermine long-term agricultural productivity.

By 2040, the global population is projected to peak and begin declining. This demographic transition creates an opportunity: if agricultural efficiency improves sufficiently, land can be freed for restoration without sacrificing food security. Between 2025 and 2070, agricultural transformation should focus on:

- **Precision agriculture:** GPS-guided tractors, drone-based crop monitoring, and AI-optimized irrigation and fertilization reduce waste and increase yields while using less water and fewer chemicals.

- **Vertical farming:** Growing crops in stacked layers under LED lights requires no arable land, minimal water, and no pesticides. By 2070, vertical farms could supply much of the world's vegetable production.

- **Agrivoltaics:** Combining solar panels with agriculture allows simultaneous energy production and food production, reducing total land requirements.

- **Alternative proteins:** Lab-grown meat, plant-based substitutes, and insect farming can provide protein with far lower land and water requirements than traditional livestock. A global transition away from cattle ranching could free hundreds of millions of hectares for reforestation.

- **Regenerative agriculture:** Practices that build soil organic matter, increase water infiltration, and support soil microbiota can reverse soil degradation while maintaining or increasing yields. Techniques include no-till farming, cover cropping, composting, and agroforestry.

These transformations could reduce agricultural land use by 20–40% while maintaining or increasing food production. Freed land—marginal farmland, abandoned pasture, degraded cropland—could be returned to forest, grassland, or wetland, adding substantial NPP while improving food security.

**14. Permanent Policy Frameworks**

The interventions outlined in this document operate on timescales of decades to centuries. Forests planted today will reach peak productivity only in the 2070s and 2080s. Ocean restoration will take decades to show its full effects. Agricultural transformation requires generational shifts in farming practice and consumer behavior.

Achieving these outcomes requires institutions capable of sustaining long-term commitments across political cycles, economic shocks, and social upheavals. The policy challenge is constitutional: we need frameworks that make planetary stewardship a permanent priority.

Several mechanisms deserve consideration:

- **Constitutional environmental protections:** National constitutions should incorporate rights to clean air, clean water, and healthy ecosystems. Some nations have already recognized the rights of nature in their constitutions. By 2050, this should be global practice.

- **International treaties with supranational enforcement:** Voluntary frameworks like the Paris Agreement depend on national compliance. A supranational environmental authority with the power to sanction violations could enforce binding standards across borders.

- **Ecosystem endowments:** Large-scale, perpetual endowments—funded by carbon taxes, wealth taxes, or natural resource revenues—could provide reliable funding for restoration. These endowments should be managed by independent trustees with fiduciary obligations to future generations.

- **Intergenerational representation:** Institutions making decisions affecting future centuries should include representatives of future generations. Guardian institutions analogous to legal guardians for minors could advocate for those not yet born.

- **AI-assisted monitoring and management:** By 2040, AI systems will be capable of real-time monitoring of biosphere health at global scale. These systems should be trained to optimize for long-term planetary health, providing the information and coordination capacity necessary to manage planetary-scale interventions.

The challenge of planetary stewardship is that the biosphere operates on timescales far longer than human political institutions. Building governance mechanisms that can sustain commitment across centuries requires constitutional creativity—and the prize, an Earth restored to full capacity, justifies the effort.

**15. Planetary Bioengineering: The Next Phase of Stewardship (2070+)**

By 2070, the near-term and medium-term interventions described above should have reversed the major causes of biosphere degradation and begun adding tens of petagrams of NPP per year. Forest restoration will be producing mature, productive ecosystems. Ocean restoration will have replenished fish stocks, expanded coastal wetlands, and reestablished productive marine food webs. Agricultural transformation will have freed millions of hectares for rewilding while maintaining or increasing food production.

But Earth is a single planet, and its capacity to support photosynthetic life is bounded by planetary physics. The planet receives only the solar energy that falls on its cross-section. Its surface area is finite. Much of that surface—deserts, ice caps, deep ocean—is biologically unproductive. Even with comprehensive restoration, the maximum sustainable NPP of Earth is estimated at roughly 150 to 200 petagrams of carbon per year under current planetary conditions.

If the goal is genuinely net-positive biodiversity and biomass—to maximize the capacity of the biosphere to support life over the long-term future—then by 2070, humanity must begin thinking beyond restoration and toward enhancement. The next phase of planetary stewardship is not passive recovery but active bioengineering: using the abundant energy of a fully decarbonized economy to manage planetary systems at scales that exceed natural processes.

*Atmospheric Optimization.* By 2070, global energy systems will be fully decarbonized and likely supported by advanced nuclear fission or early-stage fusion power. This energy abundance creates the capacity for active atmospheric management on scales that would have been prohibitively expensive in the fossil fuel era. 

Excess atmospheric carbon dioxide—the driver of climate change—can be actively captured and sequestered in geological formations or converted to useful products. At the same time, atmospheric composition can be optimized for photosynthetic efficiency: maintaining oxygen concentrations at levels that enhance aerobic respiration and support higher metabolic activity in both plants and animals. This is not advocacy for radical chemical manipulation—Earth's atmosphere already supports complex life, and extreme changes would be destabilizing—but rather for maintaining optimal conditions that maximize the productivity and resilience of the biosphere.

*Ocean Nutrient Cycling.* The oceans are currently both underfertilized and overfertilized—in different places, with different consequences. In many equatorial ocean regions, biological productivity is limited by the availability of key nutrients such as iron, nitrogen, and phosphorus. In coastal zones, agricultural runoff causes nutrient overload, creating dead zones where oxygen is depleted and marine life suffocates.

By 2070, advanced monitoring systems and targeted dosing systems should allow for precision nutrient management across ocean basins. Iron-limited oceans can be gently fertilized to stimulate phytoplankton blooms that produce oxygen, support higher trophic levels, and draw down atmospheric carbon. Nutrient overload in coastal zones can be addressed at the source—through precision agriculture—and through coastal wetlands that absorb excess nutrients before they reach the open ocean. The goal is to increase oceanic NPP from its current approximately 50 Pg C/yr to 80–100 Pg C/yr through intelligent management.

*Photosynthetic Enhancement.* Biotechnology offers the possibility of increasing photosynthetic efficiency beyond natural limits. Natural photosynthetic organisms convert solar energy to biomass at efficiencies of roughly 1–2%. Engineered organisms—algae, cyanobacteria, and even genetically modified plants—could potentially achieve efficiencies of 5% or higher under controlled conditions.

By 2070, these organisms could be deployed in closed photobioreactors, in engineered wetlands, and in specialized agricultural systems where their enhanced productivity would add significant NPP without displacing natural ecosystems. The key principle is complementarity: engineered systems should augment, not replace, the natural biosphere. Natural ecosystems provide resilience, biodiversity, and ecosystem services that engineered systems cannot replicate. Engineered systems provide raw productivity that can support human needs, take pressure off natural systems, and extend photosynthetic capacity into environments where natural ecosystems cannot thrive.

*Target NPP by 2100: Earth.* With atmospheric optimization, ocean nutrient cycling, and targeted biotechnological enhancement, Earth's NPP could increase from its present 100–120 Pg C/yr to 150–200 Pg C/yr by 2100. This would represent a biosphere operating at its maximum theoretical capacity, supporting more life than at any time in recent geological history.

But even this may not be enough to secure planetary health across the truly long-term future.

**16. Cosmic Stewardship: Extending the Biosphere Beyond Earth**

Planetary health, as a concept, is inherently planetary—it refers to the health of Earth as a self-regulating, life-supporting system. But if the goal is to maximize the long-term vitality of the biosphere, then "planetary" health is not the end of the story—it is the beginning.

The biosphere need not be bounded by Earth. Life, as we understand it, requires sunlight, water, essential nutrients, and an appropriate atmosphere. These resources are not unique to Earth. Mars possesses water ice, mineral nutrients, and sufficient solar flux to support photosynthesis. The outer solar system contains ice-rich bodies—comets, asteroids, moons—that harbor the raw materials of life in abundance. The Sun emits energy that, if fully captured, could support a biosphere of staggering size.

By 2070, humanity will have the technical capacity—if not yet the budgetary and institutional capacity—to begin extending the biosphere beyond Earth. This is not mere expansion for its own sake. It is an essential component of long-term planetary health strategy.

*Existential Resilience.* Earth's biosphere has existed for approximately four billion years. In that time, it has survived asteroid impacts, supervolcanic eruptions, ice ages, and mass extinctions. But it has never faced a technological civilization capable of either deliberate or accidental self-destruction, nor has it faced the long-term prospect of continental drift recovery into supercontinents that freeze, or climate states of extreme hothouse.

Beyond anthropogenic risks, the universe poses existential threats to any single planetary biosphere. Gamma-ray bursts, nearby supernovae, rogue black holes, or simply the eventual death of the Sun in approximately five billion years would all extinguish life on Earth if no alternative exists.

A biosphere on Mars—eventually a second Earth-like planet—would provide existential resilience. Even partial terraforming would create a reservoir of life, a backup biosphere that could eventually repopulate the solar system if Earth were rendered uninhabitable. The value of this redundancy is not merely practical; it is ethical. If planetary health matters, it matters that the biosphere survives whatever catastrophes may come.

To continue the trajectory toward net-positive NPP across cosmic timescales, we must extend the biosphere beyond the planet. The next frontier is not Earth. **16. Cosmic Vision: Extending the Biosphere Beyond Earth**

*16.1 Mars Terraforming*

Mars represents the most accessible next world. It has water ice at its poles, permafrost at high latitudes, and a surface area roughly equal to Earth's land area. Terraforming Mars—transforming it from a cold, dry, low-pressure desert into a habitable world with liquid water, breathable atmosphere, and photosynthetic ecosystems—would add billions of hectares of potential biosphere.

However, Mars terraforming is a project of centuries, not decades. Current estimates suggest the following timeline:

2025–2050: *Prelude Phase.* Space industry development, including large-scale satellite constellations, lunar mining, and in-situ resource utilization on Mars. This phase establishes the infrastructure necessary for sustained human presence in space. More importantly, it builds the technological and institutional capacity for subsequent phases.

2050–2080: *Atmospheric Thickening.* Introduction of greenhouse gases into the Martian atmosphere, increasing surface pressure and temperature. Release of polar water ice and ground ice through sublimation. By 2080, Mars might have surface temperatures approaching freezing and a thin atmosphere of carbon dioxide.

2080–2150: *Hydrological Phase.* Introduction of engineered algae and cyanobacteria, producing oxygen and organic matter. By 2150, Mars might support simple aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems.

2150–2300: *Ecosystem Maturation.* Introduction of increasingly complex organisms. By 2300, Mars could support forests, grasslands, and freshwater ecosystems—with NPP measured in petagrams of carbon per year.

These estimates are necessarily speculative. Martian geology may contain surprises; engineered ecosystems may fail; radiation and low gravity may constrain biological adaptation. We assign approximately 50% probability that Mars NPP reaches 1 petagram per year by 2300, and roughly 10% probability that terraforming fails entirely. But even in the failure mode, the Prelude Phase development would still contribute to Earth-based NPP by enabling satellite-based monitoring and climate regulation.

*16.2 O'Neill Cylinder Construction*

Beyond Mars, the solar system contains vast material resources in the asteroid belt and outer planets. These resources could support the construction of O'Neill cylinders—massive rotating space habitats that use centrifugal force to create artificial gravity and support closed-loop ecosystems.

A single cylinder of 20 km radius and 100 km length would enclose roughly 12,000 square kilometers of habitable area. Thousands of such cylinders, built over the 22nd and 23rd centuries, could support biosphere area equivalent to hundreds of Earths.

Critically, O'Neill cylinders can be positioned at optimal orbits for solar flux. Near Earth's orbit, solar flux is 340 watts per square meter; near Mercury's orbit, it is 9,100 watts per square meter. Cylinders at optimal orbits could achieve photosynthetic efficiencies far exceeding terrestrial ecosystems.

Timeline estimates:

2100–2150: *Technology Development.* Demonstration habitats in low Earth orbit and near the Moon.

2150–2200: *First Generation.* Construction of 10-100 small cylinders using lunar and asteroid materials.

2200–2300: *Industrial Scale.* Construction of 1,000+ large cylinders using automated manufacturing. NPP contribution: potentially 10-100 petagrams per year.

2300+: *Cosmic Expansion.* Construction across the solar system. NPP contribution: potentially 100-1,000 petagrams per year by 2500.

We assign approximately 30% probability that cosmic NPP reaches 100 petagrams per year by 2500.

*16.3 Why the Cosmic Vision Matters*

Earth-based restoration can add 15-30 petagrams of NPP by 2100. This is a magnificent achievement. But it is only a beginning. By 2500, if Earth-based NPP remains constant and cosmic NPP is zero, the universe will have produced approximately 1,000 petagrams of additional NPP relative to the counterfactual. But if O'Neill cylinders succeed, the universe could produce 10,000-100,000+ petagrams of additional NPP.

The cosmic vision is not an alternative to Earth-based restoration. It is the culmination. Every dollar invested in Earth's forests and oceans generates knowledge, technology, and institutional capacity that can be applied to Mars and the stars.

**11. Watershed Rewilding and Continental-Scale Restoration (2040–2070)**

Near-term tropical reforestation will restore millions of hectares by 2040, but mature forests require decades to reach full photosynthetic capacity. Meanwhile, temperate and boreal forests continue to degrade; savannas and grasslands have been converted to monoculture; and river systems worldwide have been dammed, channelized, and deprived of floodplain function. Watershed rewilding is the framework for reversing all of these processes simultaneously.

A watershed—the land area draining into a single river system—encompasses forests, grasslands, wetlands, agricultural lands, and urban centers. Restoring watersheds means restoring the hydrological, nutrient, and energy flows that sustain life across all of these environments. By 2050, every major river basin should have a watershed restoration plan, implemented through a combination of legal protection, land purchase, agricultural conversion, and community stewardship.

The keystone species of watershed restoration are those that physically shape aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems through their behavior. Beavers, once nearly extirpated from their Holarctic range, are ecosystem engineers of extraordinary transformative power. Through dam-building, beavers slow water flow, create wetlands, elevate water tables, filter agricultural runoff, and provide habitat for fish, waterfowl, and amphibians. Reintroducing beavers to appropriate waterways—which must be assessed for agricultural compatibility and flood-control function—can restore riparian forests, increase wetland area, and reduce nutrient pollution reaching coastal dead zones. By 2070, beavers should be established in every compatible river basin in North America, Europe, temperate Asia, and southern South America.

Wolves, bears, and large cats transform terrestrial ecosystems through trophic cascades. In Yellowstone National Park, wolf reintroduction reduced elk browsing pressure on willows and aspens, restored riparian vegetation, and increased songbird diversity. Apex predators are not merely charismatic fauna—they are necessary components of functional ecosystems. By 2060, wolves should be reintroduced to appropriate habitats across Europe and the eastern United States, supplemented by public education and compensation programmes that prevent livestock predation while permitting ecological function.

Large herbivores—elephants, bison, rhinoceroses—engineer savannas and grasslands through grazing, browsing, wallowing, and seed dispersal. Elephants maintain the open-forest mosaics that support maximum terrestrial NPP in tropical regions. Bison once modified North American grasslands through grazing and trampling, creating prairie potholes that support millions of waterfowl. By 2070, captive breeding, assisted migration, and genetic rescue should restore functional megaherbivore populations to Africa, India, rewilded North American grasslands, and introduced populations in temperate Eurasia where ecologically appropriate.

Continental-scale wildlife corridors—connecting protected areas across mountain ranges, river valleys, and agricultural lands—enable species migration and genetic exchange necessary for long-term adaptive evolution. The Yellowstone-to-Yukon corridor in North America provides a template: a network of protected areas, wildlife crossings, and conservation easements that allows bears, wolves, elk, and other species to move freely across 3,000 kilometres of varied terrain. By 2070, similar corridors should be established across every continent, linked to reforestation corridors that also serve as carbon sinks and as windbreaks that benefit agriculture.

Managed wildlife corridors must be designed in consultation with local communities, incorporating appropriate fencing, early-warning systems for human-wildlife conflict, and economic incentives such as ecotourism revenue-sharing.

These interventions have a single purpose: to restore the natural processes that govern primary productivity. Watershed rewilding does not merely increase NPP directly through reforestation and wetland restoration. It also increases NPP indirectly through nutrient cycling, water regulation, soil formation, and the maintenance of ecological complexity that makes ecosystems resilient to disturbance. A restored watershed produces more food, stores more carbon, supports more species, and generates more ecosystem services per unit area than a degraded one.

Progress will be measured by the increase in NPP attributable to these interventions, tracked through remote sensing and ground-based monitoring.

By 2070, watershed rewilding should restore approximately 50 million hectares of riparian forest, 20 million hectares of floodplain wetland, and billions of hectares of terrestrial habitat across functional wildlife corridors. Estimated NPP impact: 3–8 petagrams of additional carbon fixation per year by 2070, with compounding gains into the 22nd century as forests mature and ecosystems reach equilibrium.

**13. Active Ocean Management and Marine Bioengineering (2040–2070)**

**14. Planetary Bioengineering: Engineering Earth's Photosynthetic Capacity (2070–2100)**

By 2070, the restoration efforts described in Sections 9–13 will have substantially increased NPP across forests, oceans, and agricultural systems. But restoration alone cannot reverse all forms of planetary degradation, and some constraints on NPP are geological and atmospheric rather than ecological. Planetary bioengineering addresses the environmental parameters that fundamentally limit photosynthetic capacity, including air quality, soil fertility, water availability, and the genetic potential of photosynthetic organisms themselves.

The goal of planetary bioengineering is not to replace natural ecosystems with technological substitutes, but to remove the artificial constraints on ecosystem productivity imposed by centuries of pollution, soil degradation, and unsustainable resource extraction. Where restoration rebuilds ecosystems from destruction, bioengineering optimizes the abiotic conditions within which ecosystems flourish.

*14.1 Atmospheric Restoration*

Atmospheric composition is the most fundamental limit on global NPP. Photosynthetic organisms require carbon dioxide (which is currently excessive due to fossil fuel combustion), oxygen (which is consumed by combustion and degradation), and—critically—unimpeded sunlight. Aerosols, particulates, and air pollutants diminish the solar flux reaching Earth's surface, reducing photosynthetic efficiency across terrestrial ecosystems.

Global atmospheric restoration would involve two complementary strategies. First, reducing anthropogenic emissions of aerosols and particulates through transition to clean energy, electrification of transport, and elimination of biomass burning and industrial pollution. Second, active atmospheric engineering to remove existing pollutants and restore transparency. Forest restoration (described in Section 10) already contributes to atmospheric restoration by reducing dust and increasing oxygen production, but additional interventions—large-scale ionization systems for removing particulates, algal bioreactors engineered to scrub pollutants from urban air—could recover 5–15% of photosynthetically active radiation currently blocked by atmospheric haze.

By 2100, atmospheric restoration could add 2–4 petagrams of terrestrial NPP per year simply by increasing the sunlight available to plants.

*14.2 Soil Regeneration*

Soil is the foundation of terrestrial NPP. Healthy soil supports diverse forest ecosystems, productive agriculture, and the hydrological cycles that sustain both. Degraded soil—compacted, eroded, depleted of organic matter and beneficial microbiota—supports only a fraction of the photosynthetic productivity that healthy soil would permit.

The degradation of Earth's soils is perhaps the most underappreciated form of planetary degradation. An estimated 40% of agricultural land is moderately or severely degraded, with losses accelerating due to deforestation, intensive tillage, excessive fertilizer use, and climate-change-driven desertification. Reversing soil degradation at planetary scales is essential to achieving net-positive NPP.

Planetary soil regeneration would involve several convergent strategies. Biochar—charcoal produced from biomass and applied to agricultural and forest soils—improves water retention, nutrient availability, and soil carbon content while sequestering atmospheric CO2. Microbiome restoration—reintroducing beneficial fungi, bacteria, and soil organisms depleted by monoculture farming—reactivates nutrient cycling and disease resistance. Mineral amendments—rock dust and trace elements essential for plant metabolism but absent in degraded soils—restore soil fertility without chemical dependency.

Applied across billions of hectares of degraded forest, agricultural, and pastoral land, planetary soil regeneration could restore 3–8 petagrams of NPP per year by 2100. The side benefit—net removal of atmospheric CO2 through biochar sequestration and reforestation—would simultaneously mitigate climate change, making soil restoration one of the highest-leverage interventions in the planetary bioengineering toolkit.

*14.3 Hydrological Engineering*

Water is the most common limiting factor for NPP in terrestrial ecosystems—and the most vulnerable in a changing climate. Freshwater sources worldwide face degradation from over-extraction, pollution, and climate-change-driven shifts in precipitation patterns. Billions of hectares of potentially productive land are water-limited, and even currently productive ecosystems face increasing drought stress.

Planetary hydrological engineering would restore the availability of clean, abundant water for photosynthetic life. Large-scale desalination powered by renewable energy would convert ocean water to freshwater, expanding irrigation potential in arid and semi-arid regions while simultaneously recharging depleted aquifers. Continental water recycling systems would capture, purify, and reuse urban wastewater, reducing pressure on natural water sources while supplying restored wetlands and reforestation projects. Glacier and ice-sheet preservation—through active cooling technologies, surface stabilization, and reduction of atmospheric soot deposition—would maintain the long-term reliability of meltwater-fed river systems essential to Asian and South American agriculture.

By 2100, hydrological engineering could restore the water balance of 1–2 billion hectares of degraded land, adding 2–5 petagrams of NPP per year while supporting global food security and reducing the human appropriation of water resources.

*14.4 Genetic Enhancement of Photosynthetic Organisms*

**15. Atmospheric and Molecular Implications of NPP Maximization**

The framework's primary metric—spacetime-integrated net primary productivity—captures the most fundamental process of the biosphere: the conversion of solar energy into organic matter through photosynthesis. But photosynthesis is not merely the foundation of food webs, the basis of carbon sequestration, or the engine of terrestrial and marine ecology. It is also the primary biological source of the molecular oxygen (O2) that sustains all aerobic life on Earth.

Every molecule of O2 in Earth's atmosphere was produced by photosynthetic organisms: cyanobacteria in the primordial oceans, algae in marine ecosystems, and the forests, grasslands, and wetlands that cover the land. The relationship between NPP and atmospheric oxygen is direct and unambiguous: more photosynthesis produces more oxygen; less photosynthesis produces less. A planet with declining NPP is a planet with a diminishing capacity to support complex, oxygen-breathing life.

The implications of this relationship are profound. Current global NPP of approximately 100–120 petagrams of carbon per year supports an atmosphere of roughly 21% oxygen—a concentration that has remained stable for hundreds of millions of years and that permits the existence of large, metabolically active organisms including humans. If NPP were to decline by 20% over the coming century through continued deforestation, ocean degradation, and ecosystem collapse, oxygen production could fall by comparable margins, with devastating consequences for human health, ecosystem function, and the long-term viability of civilization.

Conversely, the interventions described in this framework—restoring forests and oceans, transforming agriculture, managing watersheds, and enhancing planetary biogeochemistry through bioengineering—would increase global NPP by tens of petagrams per year. This would not only reverse biodiversity loss and increase biomass; it would also restore the planet's capacity to produce the oxygen necessary to sustain abundant aerobic life. A restored Earth with global NPP of 170 petagrams per year would generate oxygen at a rate sufficient to maintain stable, high concentrations for the foreseeable future, even as human population and resource demands increase.

The long-term vision extends this logic to cosmic scales. A terraformed Mars—its dry basins filled with water, its thin atmosphere enriched through planetary-scale engineering—would eventually support forests and oceans that generate oxygen for their own atmosphere. Mars would become, within centuries or millennia, a second breathable world in the solar system, sustaining life independently of conditions on Earth.

Each O'Neill cylinder constructed in the Earth-Moon system or in the asteroid belt would, by design, produce oxygen far exceeding its inhabitants' needs. Engineered for maximum photosynthetic efficiency under optimal conditions, these artificial biospheres would generate surplus oxygen that could support additional habitats, manufacture propellant for interplanetary travel, or replenish planetary atmospheres depleted by geological or industrial processes.

Oxygen is not merely a byproduct of photosynthesis. It is the signature of a living, thriving biosphere. A planet that sustains oxygen-producing ecosystems is a planet that can sustain civilization. A solar system with multiple oxygen-rich worlds is a solar system with the capacity to support life across the long-term future, even as Earth faces limits imposed by geological and stellar evolution.

The spacetime-integrated NPP metric captures this cosmic significance. A petagram of carbon fixed on Mars is as valuable as a petagram fixed on Earth—not only for its contribution to biomass and biodiversity but for its contribution to the total quantity of oxygen in the universe. Maximizing NPP is maximizing the capacity of the cosmos to sustain life, to generate breathable atmospheres, and to preserve the conditions under which civilization and biological diversity can flourish.
