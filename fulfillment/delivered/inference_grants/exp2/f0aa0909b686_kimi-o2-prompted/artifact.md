**PATHWAYS TO INCREASE NET MOLECULAR OXYGEN IN EARTH’S ATMOSPHERE AND OCEANS: A CENTURY-SCALE TECHNICAL REVIEW**

**Executive Summary**

The partial pressure of molecular oxygen (O₂) in Earth’s atmosphere (~20.9%) represents a reservoir of roughly 1.2 × 10¹⁸ kg O₂, buffered by the slow geochemical and biological carbon cycles. Over a century-scale horizon, a net increase in atmospheric or dissolved marine O₂ requires increasing the stock of reduced organic carbon (or inorganic reduced species) that is prevented from re-oxidizing, and/or splitting water via industrial processes powered by non-fossil energy.

This review evaluates strategies along three criteria: **safety to human civilization**, **safety to global biodiversity**, and **scalability**. The highest-priority recommendations are: (1) large-scale terrestrial ecosystem restoration and reforestation with native species; (2) conservation and restoration of coastal “blue carbon” ecosystems (mangroves, seagrasses, tidal marshes); (3) protection of open-ocean productivity through marine protected areas and fisheries reform; and (4) stringent reduction of anthropogenic nutrient runoff to prevent coastal hypoxia. Controlled technological pathways, such as renewable-powered electrolysis coupled with durable carbon storage, are noted but classified as secondary due to energy costs and infrastructure requirements. Intentional large-scale open-ocean fertilization is excluded from recommended approaches because of significant ecological risks.

---

**1. Quantifying the Oxygen Cycle and the Century-Scale Target**

Atmospheric O₂ is controlled by the balance of photosynthesis (gross primary production, GPP) and oxidative respiration, combustion, and weathering. Net primary production (NPP) on Earth is ~105–120 Pg C yr⁻¹, but nearly all of this carbon is respired within years to centuries. A net increase in atmospheric O₂ therefore requires **net carbon sequestration**—the permanent or semi-permanent removal of carbon from the atmosphere/ocean system into biomass, soils, sediments, or geological reservoirs.

Key metrics:
- **Oxygen-to-carbon stoichiometry**: On average, photosynthetic fixation of 1 mole of CO₂ and reduction to organic carbohydrate releases approximately 1 mole of O₂ to the atmosphere (the long-term net is commonly approximated as 1:1 for sequestered carbon, with marine Redfield ratios near 1:1.3 for transient biological pumps).
- **Atmospheric leverage**: To raise atmospheric O₂ by even 0.01% would require sequestering on the order of 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ kg C—far beyond century-scale biological capacity. More realistic goals focus on **reversing the subtle O₂ decline** driven by fossil fuel combustion (observed at roughly −0.001% per century in some records) and **increasing dissolved O₂ in marine hypoxic zones**.

Thus, the practical target is maximizing the *net flux* of O₂ into stable reservoirs and minimizing anthropogenic O₂ consumption, rather than substantially altering the atmospheric mixing ratio.

---

**2. Terrestrial Strategies: Ecosystem Restoration and Reforestation**

**2.1 Potential and Mechanisms**
Terrestrial photosynthesis contributes ~50–60 Pg C yr⁻¹ of NPP. Expanding forest cover increases standing biomass carbon and, if sustained, soil carbon. Because mature forests reach equilibrium where respiration balances photosynthesis, the **net century-scale O₂ benefit** comes from:
- Accumulating living biomass during growth phases.
- Increasing soil organic carbon (SOC) and stabilized woody debris.
- Preventing the oxidation of existing carbon stocks (i.e., avoiding deforestation).

**2.2 Scalability**
The IPCC and Bastin et al. (2019) identified roughly 0.9 billion hectares of land suitable for canopy restoration without encroaching on agricultural or urban areas. Such expansion could sequester ~200 Pg C over decades, implying a gross O₂ release on the order of 10¹⁷ moles O₂.

**2.3 Safety and Biodiversity Considerations**
- **Native species priority**: Monoculture plantations (e.g., non-native fast-growing timber species) can deplete water tables, acidify soils, and reduce biodiversity. Native mixed-species restoration supports trophic complexity and ecological resilience.
- **Albedo effects**: Boreal afforestation can decrease surface albedo and produce local warming that partly offsets climate benefits; tropical and temperate restoration are climatically preferred.
- **Land tenure and food security**: Restoration must avoid displacing food production. Agroforestry and the restoration of already-degraded lands align O₂ goals with human welfare.

**2.4 Soil and Wetland Restoration**
Peatlands and wetlands store ~30% of global soil carbon despite covering only ~3% of land area. Drained peatlands oxidize and release CO₂ (consuming atmospheric O₂ in the process). Rewetting restores anoxia, halts CO₂ emissions, and resumes carbon accumulation—translating to a net O₂ benefit relative to the degraded baseline. Regenerative agricultural practices that build SOC operate on the same principle, though their per-hectare O₂ flux is smaller than forest or wetland restoration.

**3. Marine Strategies: Blue Carbon and Ocean Conservation**

The oceans generate roughly half of global net primary productivity and contain the largest accessible reservoir of dissolved molecular O₂. Yet century-scale warming, stratification, nutrient runoff, and biomass extraction have driven a measurable decline in dissolved oxygen concentrations—a phenomenon termed **ocean deoxygenation**. Marine strategies therefore focus on: (a) maximizing carbon burial in coastal sediments, (b) conserving open-ocean biological pumps that export carbon out of surface waters, and (c) eliminating anthropogenic drivers of hypoxia.

---

**3.1 Coastal Blue Carbon Ecosystems**

Mangroves, seagrass meadows, and tidal salt marshes are collectively termed “blue carbon” ecosystems. Despite covering less than 2% of ocean area, they account for roughly 50% of carbon buried in marine sediments because their high productivity combines with waterlogged, anoxic soils that suppress decomposition.

*Carbon stocks and sequestration rates.* Global mangrove biomass and sediment carbon are estimated at roughly 4–20 Pg C, with burial rates commonly cited at 6–10 t C ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ in active accretion zones. Seagrass meadows store an estimated 4–20 Pg C globally; organic carbon burial efficiencies can exceed 50% of local net primary production, compared to single-digit percentages in most well-drained terrestrial soils. Salt marshes hold roughly 0.5–2 Pg C and accrete sediment vertically, making them resilient to sea-level rise when landward migration space is preserved.

*Oxygen implications.* Because buried organic carbon is withdrawn from the atmosphere–ocean system for centuries to millennia, the gross photosynthetic O₂ released during its formation becomes a net addition relative to the baseline in which that carbon would have re-oxidized. Additionally, these ecosystems locally elevate daytime dissolved O₂ through submerged and emergent photosynthesis, creating refugia in otherwise stressed coastal waters.

*Restoration potential and safety.* Historical losses of mangroves (~30–50%), seagrasses (~20–30%), and salt marshes (~25–50%) offer substantial restoration headroom. Reviews by Duarte and colleagues and the IPCC Wetlands Supplement indicate that restoring even a fraction of historical extents could sequester on the order of 0.2–0.5 Pg C yr⁻¹ over multi-decadal scales. Safety is high when restoration uses native species and natural hydrology; risks are limited to site-level alterations in sediment chemistry or species composition if hydrological connectivity is ignored. Co-benefits include nursery habitat for ~25% of marine species, coastal storm protection valued in billions of dollars annually, and filtration of land-derived pollutants before they reach open waters.

**3.2 Open-Ocean Productivity, Marine Protected Areas, and the Vertebrate Carbon Pump**

The open ocean fixes ~45–55 Pg C yr⁻¹ via phytoplankton, but the vast majority respires within the surface mixed layer. Net sequestration depends on the **biological carbon pump**—the sinking and physical subduction of organic matter into the deep ocean and sediments.

*Marine Protected Areas (MPAs).* Fully protected MPAs allow recovery of fish biomass and restoration of natural size spectra. Large, mobile fish and marine vertebrates store carbon in tissues and lipids; when they die naturally, their carcasses sink rapidly, bypassing the surface remineralization layer and transporting carbon to the deep seafloor. Estimates suggest that rebuilding depleted fish stocks to more historic levels could enhance the biological pump by tens of megatonnes of C yr⁻¹, with corresponding net retention of O₂ in the atmosphere–ocean system.

*Whale-mediated nutrient cycling.* Great whales feed at depth and excrete iron and nitrogen in sunlit surface waters via fecal plumes, a process termed the **“whale pump.”** This fertilizes phytoplankton communities that would otherwise be nutrient-limited, increasing gross primary production and gross O₂ evolution. Whale falls additionally deposit tens of tonnes of carbon per carcass to the deep benthos, where they support chemosynthetic communities and sequester carbon for decades to centuries. Restoring whale populations to pre-exploitation levels is therefore a multi-century O₂ investment; it is also entirely benign, requiring only the cessation of hunting and mitigation of ship strikes and net entanglement.

*Safety and scalability.* Open-ocean conservation scales with governance capacity but carries minimal direct ecological risk. The primary limitation is the non-linear, decadal-scale response of export production to biomass recovery; O₂ benefits accrue slowly but persistently.

**3.3 Combating Ocean Deoxygenation Through Nutrient Runoff Reduction**

Anthropogenic nitrogen and phosphorus inputs from agricultural runoff, sewage, and fossil-fuel combustion have created more than 700 coastal dead zones worldwide. Excess nutrients fuel phytoplankton blooms; upon senescence, microbial decomposition consumes dissolved O₂ faster than physical mixing can replenish it, producing hypoxia (<2 mg L⁻¹ dissolved O₂) and, in extreme cases, anoxia.

*Oxygen significance.* Dissolved O₂ in the ocean is already declining at rates of 0.5–3% per decade in subsurface waters due to warming-driven solubility loss and increased stratification. Nutrient-driven hypoxia accelerates this trend in coastal regions that support the most productive fisheries. Unlike atmospheric O₂, which is buffered by a vast reservoir, marine dissolved O₂ in these zones can collapse to zero, triggering mass mortality and shifting sediments from carbon sinks to carbon sources (via sulfate reduction and methanogenesis).

*Interventions.* Reducing N and P loading—through precision agriculture, vegetated riparian buffers, constructed wetlands, tertiary sewage treatment, and dietary shifts that lower demand for intensive feed-crop cultivation—directly preserves dissolved O₂. Every tonne of organic carbon prevented from decomposing in hypoxic bottom waters avoids the consumption of roughly 2.7 tonnes of O₂ (based on typical respiratory quotients). Preserving existing dissolved O₂ stocks and preventing new hypoxic zones is among the highest-leverage O₂-protection strategies available, and it scales linearly with agricultural efficiency improvements.

**3.4 Exclusion of Intentional Large-Scale Ocean Fertilization**

Ocean iron fertilization (OIF) and artificial upwelling have been proposed to enhance productivity and atmospheric CO₂ drawdown. However, deliberate large-scale addition of limiting nutrients carries severe and poorly constrained risks: shifts in phytoplankton community structure toward harmful taxa, production of toxins, increased N₂O flux (a potent greenhouse gas), and deep-water anoxia as augmented biomass decomposes. Field experiments (e.g., LOHAFEX, EIFEX) and subsequent modeling indicate that net carbon sequestration efficiency is low while the potential to create new oxygen minimum zones is significant. Because the explicit goal of this review is to increase net O₂, approaches that risk generating additional anoxia or eutrophication are excluded on safety and biodiversity grounds.

**4. Technological and Industrial Pathways**

**4. Technological and Industrial Pathways**

Industrial interventions operate outside the biosphere’s carbon cycles and therefore face different stoichiometric constraints. This section distinguishes carbon-removal technologies that do *not* alter atmospheric O₂ from those that can, evaluates the energy requirements of direct O₂ generation, and clarifies the oxygen accounting of bioenergy systems.

---

**4.1 Direct Air Capture (DAC) and Mineral Carbonation**

Direct air capture (DAC) uses solid sorbents or liquid solvents to extract CO₂ from ambient air, after which the concentrated CO₂ stream is compressed for geologic storage or mineralization. Because the process is a physical separation rather than a chemical reduction, **DAC does not release molecular oxygen**. The thermodynamic work input drives an entropy decrease; no net water is split and no net organic carbon is created.

Lifecycle analyses indicate that DAC facilities powered by renewable electricity indirectly avoid some O₂ consumption that would otherwise occur via fossil generation, but the process itself is O₂-neutral. When paired with geologic storage (DACCS), the system removes CO₂ without changing the atmospheric O₂ inventory. Mineral carbonation—reacting CO₂ with alkaline silicates to form stable carbonates—similarly sequesters carbon but produces no O₂. Consequently, while DACCS and mineral carbonation are valuable climate-mitigation tools, they are **not direct pathways for increasing net atmospheric or marine O₂** over century scales.

---

**4.2 Renewable-Powered Electrolysis with Durable Hydrogen Storage**

Water electrolysis offers the only industrial technology that directly generates molecular O₂ at scale:

2 H₂O(l) → 2 H₂(g) + O₂(g)

When powered by non-fossil electricity, the O₂ released can represent a *genuine net addition* to the atmosphere, provided the co-produced hydrogen is permanently withheld from the oxidative cycle. If the H₂ is later combusted or used in fuel cells, it recombine with O₂ to form water, yielding zero net O₂ over the full cycle.

**Conditions for net O₂ gain.** Net century-scale O₂ increase requires durable sequestration of the hydrogen (or its derivatives) in reduced form. Candidate pathways include:
- **Geologic H₂ storage** in salt caverns, depleted gas reservoirs, or engineered subsurface complexes. Hydrogen can be stored physically; however, long-term retention is challenged by microbial methanogenesis, cap-rock diffusion, and induced seismicity risks.
- **Hydrogenation of CO₂ to storable hydrocarbons** (e.g., subsurface methane storage or liquid synthesis) *if* the resulting fuel remains unburned. Because the geological storage of synthetic methane carries leakage risks and security burdens comparable to natural gas, this is currently speculative.
- **Integration with steel or chemical reduction** where hydrogen replaces carbon reductants, embedding the hydrogen in metal or reduced products rather than returning it to the atmosphere as water. The O₂ co-product is released, and the hydrogen is effectively locked in material stocks.

**Energy and scalability.** State-of-the-art alkaline or PEM electrolysis consumes roughly 50–55 kWh per kilogram of H₂ produced, which translates to approximately 6–7 kWh per kilogram of O₂. Current global electricity generation (~30,000 TWh yr⁻¹), if entirely redirected to electrolysis with perfect efficiency, could generate on the order of 4–5 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹—small relative to the atmospheric reservoir (~1.2 × 10⁶ Pg) but technologically direct.

**Safety and constraints.** Electrolysis at planetary scale entails massive freshwater withdrawals (or energy-intensive desalination), mineral demand for catalysts and membranes, and infrastructure for pressurized gas transport. The explosive nature of H₂ and the absence of proven century-scale geologic storage render this pathway **secondary** to biological strategies. It is noted here for completeness because it is the only method that manufactures O₂ independent of the carbon cycle.

---

**4.3 BECCS and Biochar: Distinguishing Carbon Negativity from O₂ Generation**

Bioenergy systems are often conflated with O₂ production because they begin with photosynthesis. Careful stoichiometry is required.

**BECCS (Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage).** In a typical BECCS chain, biomass is cultivated (photosynthesis releases O₂ and fixes CO₂), combusted for energy (combustion consumes O₂ and emits CO₂), and the flue-gas CO₂ is captured and geologically stored. The overall cycle is carbon-negative: atmospheric CO₂ is removed. However, the O₂ consumed during combustion is stoichiometrically equal to the O₂ released during the photosynthesis that formed the biomass (≈1 mole O₂ consumed per mole of CO₂-derived carbohydrate re-oxidized). **BECCS is therefore O₂-neutral over the full cycle.** It prevents CO₂ accumulation but does not increase the net stock of atmospheric O₂.

**Biochar.** Pyrolysis of biomass under oxygen-limited conditions converts 30–70% of feedstock carbon into recalcitrant aromatic structures (biochar) while releasing syngas and bio-oil. Because the sequestered carbon fraction avoids complete oxidation, the photosynthetic O₂ corresponding to that withheld carbon is *not* re-consumed. The process thus converts a portion of transient biomass carbon into a durable geological-like sink, yielding a net O₂ increment analogous to wetland or soil carbon accumulation, but on an engineered timescale. Co-produced syngas can be combusted for process heat, consuming some O₂, but the retained char fraction represents a net gain. When produced from agricultural residues or sustainably coppiced perennials and incorporated into soils, biochar is compatible with biodiversity and food systems; risks arise if feedstock production displaces native forests or food crops.

---

**4.4 Avoided Fossil Combustion and Industrial O₂ Demand Reduction**

The largest anthropogenic sink for atmospheric O₂ is the combustion of fossil fuels. Complete combustion of carbon consumes O₂ in a fixed ratio:

C + O₂ → CO₂  (1 tonne C consumes ~2.67 tonnes O₂)

Global fossil fuel use currently draws down atmospheric O₂ at roughly 10–15 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹—an order of magnitude larger than the subtle O₂ decline recorded in atmospheric stations. Therefore, **decarbonization via renewables, electrification, and efficiency does not create new O₂, but halting fossil O₂ consumption is mathematically equivalent to adding O₂** relative to the business-as-usual trajectory. Similarly, replacing oxygen-intensive industrial processes (e.g., conventional blast-furnace ironmaking, partial oxidation in petrochemicals) with green-hydrogen reduction or electric arc pathways reduces the industrial O₂ sink.

Because this review’s scope centers on *active* O₂ increase, avoided combustion is not assigned a primary section, but it quantitatively dominates the century-scale O₂ budget. Any credible O₂-maximization framework implicitly requires the cessation of fossil oxidation.

**5.3 Synthesis**
The matrix reveals a clear hierarchy. Nature-based pathways (1–5) offer the highest combination of civilization safety, biodiversity safety, and proven scalability. Among them, nutrient-runoff reduction is unique because it primarily *preserves* existing marine dissolved O₂, yielding immediate local benefits and preventing regime shifts in coastal carbon cycling. Terrestrial restoration has the largest gross potential but requires sustained protection against reversal. Blue carbon sits between these in total scalability and offers exceptional carbon-burial efficiency per unit area.

Technological pathways divide into two classes. Biochar (6) is O₂-positive and deployable with near-term agricultural infrastructure, making it the most credible bridge technology. Electrolysis (7) is the only industrial option that synthesizes *new* O₂ molecules independent of the biosphere, yet its net efficacy hinges on unproven, century-scale hydrogen storage or material embedding. BECCS and DACCS (8–9) are valuable for atmospheric CO₂ stabilization but do not alter the atmospheric O₂ inventory; they are excluded from the O₂-maximization priority set. Avoided fossil combustion (10) remains the single largest mathematical lever but is treated as a boundary condition: without it, biological gains are overwhelmed by the ongoing industrial O₂ sink.

**6. Governance, Monitoring, and Prioritized Recommendations**

**6.1 Monitoring the O₂ System**
Verifying net O₂ changes requires distinguishing gross fluxes from net stocks. Current observational capabilities and gaps include:
- **Atmospheric O₂/N₂ ratios**: High-precision networks (e.g., Scripps O₂ Program) detect global trends of ~20 per meg yr⁻¹. Expansion into the tropics and continental interiors would improve attribution of carbon sinks.
- **Marine dissolved O₂**: The GO-SHIP repeat hydrography program and Bio-Argo profiling floats with oxygen optodes track basin-scale deoxygenation. Sustained high-density coverage in coastal hypoxic zones is needed to verify nutrient-management outcomes.
- **Remote-sensing proxies**: Solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF) and ocean-color radiometry monitor gross primary productivity, but cannot resolve net ecosystem production or burial. They are best used as early-warning indicators rather than direct O₂-accounting tools.
- **Sequestration ground-truthing**: Radiocarbon-dated sediment cores for blue carbon burial, national soil-carbon inventory networks for terrestrial SOC, and eddy-covariance flux towers for net ecosystem exchange provide the empirical basis for O₂-equivalent verification.

**6.2 Institutional Architecture**
Because O₂ is a global commons without a dedicated regulatory regime, its management is distributed across existing frameworks:
- **Terrestrial domains**: The Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), and the Bonn Challenge set restoration targets. Durability depends on national land-tenure systems and long-term stewardship finance to prevent re-release from land-use change or wildfire.
- **Marine domains**: The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), CBD, and Regional Fisheries Management Organizations govern MPAs and species recovery. The London Protocol explicitly regulates ocean fertilization, effectively prohibiting unilateral large-scale OIF deployment. Regional nutrient-load reductions are coordinated through river-basin authorities and national water-quality regulations.
- **Industrial domains**: Emerging hydrogen economies are governed by national safety codes and nascent international certification schemes. If electrolysis is scaled, protocols for geologic hydrogen storage integrity and leakage liability would be required. Carbon markets under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement may subsidize BECCS and DACCS; because these are O₂-neutral, they are best treated as climate instruments distinct from any O₂-accounting framework.

**6.3 Prioritized Recommendations**

*Tier 1 — Immediate, high-confidence, high-safety interventions*
1. **Protect and restore native terrestrial ecosystems**, prioritizing mixed-species forests, peatland rewetting, and regenerative soil management on already-degraded lands without displacing food production.
2. **Restore coastal blue carbon ecosystems** (mangroves, seagrasses, tidal marshes) using community-based, native-species hydrological rehabilitation.
3. **Reduce anthropogenic nutrient loading** to coastal waters through precision agriculture, vegetated riparian buffers, upgraded sewage treatment, and optimized dietary protein sourcing; this preserves dissolved marine O₂ and avoids feedback carbon losses from hypoxic sediments.
4. **Expand fully protected marine areas and recover marine vertebrate populations**, including great whales and large predatory fish, to rebuild natural nutrient and carbon-pump functions.

*Tier 2 — Medium-term, complementary, O₂-positive technologies*
5. **Deploy sustainable biochar systems** using agricultural residues and sustainably harvested woody biomass; apply to agricultural soils to enhance SOC stability and yield a net O₂ increment via avoided oxidation of the charred fraction.
6. **Substitute green hydrogen for carbon reductants** in heavy industry (e.g., direct reduction of iron ore), venting or productively utilizing the co-produced O₂ while the hydrogen carries oxygen atoms into stable material or water cycles rather than returning atmospheric carbon to the air.
7. **Integrate agroforestry and improved grazing management** to build soil organic carbon across working lands.

*Tier 3 — Long-term, conditional industrial pathways*
8. **Develop renewable-powered electrolysis coupled with durable, verifiable hydrogen sequestration** (geologic or material-based) only after containment longevity is demonstrated at scale; treat as a secondary O₂ source pending full energy-system decarbonization and surplus electricity availability.
9. **Continue research and piloting of DACCS and BECCS for climate stabilization**, while recognizing that these are O₂-neutral and therefore subordinate to biological strategies under an explicit O₂-maximization objective.

*Boundary condition*
10. **Accelerate the global phase-out of fossil-fuel combustion and oxygen-intensive industrial processes**. Although this is a demand-side reduction rather than a new O₂ source, stopping the ~10–15 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ anthropogenic sink is a mathematical prerequisite for any net rise in atmospheric or marine O₂ over the century.

**6.4 Centenary Outlook**
Over a 100-year horizon, biological processes remain the only demonstrated, safe, and scalable means of increasing net molecular O₂. The combined effect of terrestrial restoration, blue carbon recovery, marine conservation, and nutrient management could shift the global carbon–oxygen balance toward a modest net O₂ gain, while simultaneously stabilizing climate and biodiversity. Industrial pathways offer theoretical complements but face thermodynamic, infrastructural, and permanence constraints that relegate them to supporting roles. Above all, reversing the fossil-fuel-driven O₂ consumption is the foundation upon which any addition rests. Monitoring, governing, and investing in these pathways as an integrated portfolio offers the highest probability of measurably increasing Earth’s O₂ endowment by the twenty-second century.
