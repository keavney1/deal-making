**Executive Summary**

The abundance of molecular oxygen in Earth's atmosphere represents one of the most consequential features of our planet's biosphere. Oxygen is not merely essential for aerobic respiration and the metabolism of virtually all complex life; it is also a central driver of atmospheric chemistry, ocean productivity, and the long-term habitability of the planet. This review examines the planetary oxygen cycle—how oxygen is produced, consumed, and distributed across Earth's atmosphere, oceans, and terrestrial ecosystems—and assesses the sensitivity of these cycles to environmental change.

The central finding of this review is that Earth's oxygen abundance, while historically stable over millions of years, is not guaranteed over the timescales relevant to human civilization. Anthropogenic pressures on forests, oceans, and atmospheric chemistry represent meaningful risks to oxygen production, while climate change introduces nonlinear feedback effects that could amplify or dampen oxygen cycles in unexpected ways. At the same time, substantial opportunities exist to enhance oxygen production through ecosystem restoration, sustainable land management, ocean rehabilitation, and careful attention to planetary-scale biogeochemical processes.

We recommend that planetary oxygen maintenance and enhancement be considered a core objective of environmental policy, on par with climate stability, biodiversity preservation, and food security. Oxygen is not one goal among many—it is the atmospheric foundation upon which all aerobic life, including human civilization, depends.

**Section 1: Oxygen Production Pathways**

Molecular oxygen (O2) is produced on Earth almost entirely through photosynthesis, the process by which phototrophic organisms use sunlight to split water molecules and synthesize organic compounds. The oxygen released as a byproduct of this reaction represents the bulk of the planet's oxygen production, with smaller contributions from photochemical reactions in the upper atmosphere.

The two largest categories of oxygen-producing systems are terrestrial ecosystems and marine ecosystems. Terrestrial ecosystems—forests, grasslands, wetlands, and agricultural lands—produce oxygen through the photosynthetic activity of plants and cyanobacteria in soils and surface waters. Forests, in particular, remain the iconic symbol of oxygen production in the public imagination, and for good reason: tropical rainforests, boreal forests, and temperate woodlands collectively represent the largest terrestrial sources of net oxygen. However, the net contribution of forests must be understood carefully. Forest ecosystems also consume oxygen through respiration, decomposition, and fire. The net oxygen production of a mature forest is often modest, because the rate of respiration by trees, soil microbes, and decomposers approaches the rate of photosynthesis. The largest net contributions to atmospheric oxygen come from ecosystems where organic matter is accumulating—growing forests, sequestering wetlands, and ocean regions where phytoplankton production exceeds consumption and where organic matter sinks to the deep ocean.

Marine ecosystems are equally critical, and in some analyses more important for net oxygen production. Phytoplankton in the open ocean convert CO2 and water into organic matter and oxygen, with a substantial fraction of the organic matter sinking to the deep sea and seafloor sediments where it is effectively sequestered. This "biological pump" represents a net transfer of carbon and oxygen out of the atmosphere-ocean system on timescales of thousands to millions of years. The Southern Ocean, the equatorial upwelling zones, and coastal regions with strong nutrient inputs are the most productive marine oxygen sources. Coastal ecosystems—seagrass meadows, kelp forests, and mangrove swamps—also contribute significantly, and their role has been underappreciated relative to their importance.

Cyanobacteria deserve special mention. These photosynthetic bacteria were responsible for the Great Oxidation Event approximately 2.4 billion years ago, when atmospheric oxygen rose from trace amounts to levels sufficient to support aerobic life. Cyanobacteria remain important oxygen producers in modern oceans and fresh waters, and their capacity for oxygen production under conditions hostile to other organisms—high salinity, extreme temperatures, low nutrients—makes them a potentially valuable resource for oxygen production in degraded ecosystems and, speculatively, in engineered bioreactors or extraterrestrial environments.

The geological perspective is essential: oxygen has not always been abundant on Earth, and its current abundance is the product of billions of years of photosynthetic activity that exceeded oxygen consumption by respiration, oxidation of minerals, and burial of organic matter. The present-day oxygen-rich atmosphere is a biological artifact, maintained by the ongoing activity of photosynthetic organisms. If that activity were to decline—or if oxygen consumption were to rise without compensating production—the atmosphere would, over geological time, return to an oxygen-poor state.

**Section 2: Oxygen Consumption and Turnover**

Molecular oxygen is not merely produced; it is continuously consumed across the biosphere, atmosphere, and lithosphere. The abundance of oxygen in Earth's atmosphere—currently about 21% by volume—represents a dynamic equilibrium between production and consumption, rather than a permanent geological feature. Understanding the major consumption pathways is essential for assessing the sensitivity of this equilibrium to environmental change.

The largest biological sink for oxygen is aerobic respiration. Virtually all complex organisms—plants, animals, fungi, and the vast majority of microbes—consume oxygen to oxidize organic compounds and release energy for metabolism. The total global flux of oxygen through respiration is estimated to be roughly comparable to total photosynthetic production, meaning that the net oxygen production of the biosphere is modest and depends on the fraction of photosynthetic output that is buried, stored, or otherwise sequestered before it can be respired.

Organic matter that is not immediately respired is subject to decomposition by bacteria and fungi, which also consume oxygen. The rate of decomposition depends on temperature, moisture, and the molecular composition of the organic matter. Tropical forests decompose organic matter rapidly, with most carbon cycling back to the atmosphere within years to decades. Boreal forests and peatlands decompose more slowly, storing organic matter—in some cases for thousands of years—representing a net sink for both carbon and oxygen.

Abiotic oxidation pathways are also significant. Iron and sulfur minerals in Earth's crust react with oxygen to form oxides and sulfates, consuming oxygen over geological timescales. This process was particularly important in the early history of the atmosphere, when the Great Oxidation Event first introduced oxygen and much of it was consumed oxidizing surface minerals. Today, geological oxidation continues at a slower rate, balanced by the burial of organic matter and the production of new oxygen by photosynthesis.

Atmospheric chemistry creates additional oxygen dynamics. Photochemical reactions driven by solar ultraviolet radiation produce ozone (O3) from oxygen, consuming O2 in the upper atmosphere. However, ozone itself decomposes back to O2, so the net effect is a redistribution rather than a permanent loss. More concerning for atmospheric oxygen balance are reactions with anthropogenic pollutants—nitrogen oxides (NOx), volatile organic compounds, and halogenated gases—which alter the balance of photochemical production and destruction in ways that can reduce total atmospheric oxidant capacity.

Ocean deoxygenation represents a particularly important contemporary sink. As surface waters warm, they hold less dissolved oxygen, and stratification reduces mixing with oxygen-rich surface waters. At the same time, nutrient runoff from agriculture causes eutrophication, stimulating algal blooms that, upon decomposition, consume vast quantities of oxygen. Coastal "dead zones"—areas where oxygen levels fall below the threshold needed for aerobic life—have expanded dramatically in recent decades, now affecting over 400 coastal systems worldwide.

The net effect of these consumption pathways is that Earth's oxygen represents a carefully balanced budget. The atmosphere contains approximately 1.2 × 10^18 kg of O2, produced over billions of years of net positive photosynthesis. But this reservoir is not indefinitely renewable if the balance tips toward consumption. A sustained reduction in photosynthetic production, or a sustained increase in consumption, would deplete atmospheric oxygen over timescales ranging from centuries (for catastrophic collapse of major ecosystems) to millions of years (for gradual geological change). The Anthropocene has introduced perturbations that operate on century timescales, making them directly relevant to human civilization.

**Section 3: Sensitivity Analysis**

The Anthropocene has introduced multiple, interacting pressures that threaten to destabilize the oxygen balance. While none of these pressures alone is sufficient to cause catastrophic oxygen depletion in the near term, their combined effect—and the potential for nonlinear feedbacks and tipping points—deserves serious scientific attention.

*3.1 Deforestation and Land-Use Change*

Forests have long been recognized as critical for oxygen production, but the relationship between deforestation and atmospheric oxygen is more complex than the simple "trees make oxygen" narrative suggests. Mature forests consume nearly as much oxygen through respiration and decomposition as they produce through photosynthesis. Their net contribution to atmospheric oxygen depends on growth: a forest that is actively growing (accumulating biomass, sequestering carbon) is a net oxygen source, while a forest in steady state is roughly neutral.

Deforestation therefore affects oxygen in two ways. First, it eliminates photosynthetic capacity that could be generating oxygen if the forest were growing. Second, it releases stored carbon through burning and decomposition, contributing to climate change. Climate change, in turn, threatens remaining forests through increased drought, more frequent and severe fires, and the migration of climate zones faster than forest species can adapt.

Several forest systems are approaching potential tipping points. The Amazon rainforest, which harbors enormous photosynthetic capacity, has experienced increasing drought stress, deforestation, and fire over the past two decades. Research suggests that if deforestation exceeds roughly 20–25% of the basin, reduced transpiration would decrease regional rainfall, triggering further drying, more fires, and rapid transition to savanna. Such dieback would eliminate a major oxygen-producing region and release stored carbon equivalent to years of global fossil fuel emissions, further accelerating climate change and threatening other oxygen-producing systems.

The boreal forests of Siberia and Canada face a related but distinct threat: as temperatures rise, peatlands that have stored carbon and oxygen-equivalent organic matter for millennia are becoming more susceptible to decomposition and fire. Boreal forest fires have increased dramatically in frequency and intensity in recent years, releasing carbon and atmospheric pollutants that affect oxygen chemistry.

*3.2 Ocean Warming and Deoxygenation*

Oceans produce roughly half of Earth's oxygen, primarily through phytoplankton photosynthesis in the upper sunlit layers. Marine oxygen is therefore as critical as terrestrial oxygen for planetary habitability—and potentially more vulnerable to perturbation.

Ocean warming reduces dissolved oxygen directly, because warmer water holds less gas in solution. More significantly, warming increases stratification of the ocean, reducing vertical mixing between surface waters (where oxygen is produced) and deeper waters (where oxygen is consumed by decomposition of sinking organic matter). The resulting expansion of mid-water "oxygen minimum zones" has been documented across the tropical and subtropical oceans and is projected to intensify under continued warming.

Coastal dead zones, caused by nutrient runoff from agriculture and sewage, represent another major threat to marine oxygen. Eutrophication stimulates algal blooms that, when they die and decompose, consume oxygen faster than it can be replenished. The Gulf of Mexico dead zone, the Baltic Sea, and coastal waters off China and India are among the most severely affected. These dead zones are expanding and could increasingly affect coastal fisheries, coral reefs, and the broader marine food web.

Ocean acidification—the reduction of pH caused by absorption of atmospheric CO2—also threatens oxygen production. Many phytoplankton species are sensitive to pH changes, and experiments suggest that ocean acidification could reduce primary productivity in some regions while increasing it in others. The net effect on global oxygen production remains uncertain, but any reduction in open-ocean productivity would directly reduce planetary oxygen generation.

The "biological pump" that transfers carbon and oxygen to the deep ocean is also at risk. Phytoplankton that are not consumed or respired in the surface ocean sink to the deep sea, where they are stored for thousands to millions of years. This represents a net transfer of oxygen out of the active atmosphere-ocean system and is one of the reasons Earth has been able to accumulate oxygen over geological time. If warming, acidification, or ecosystem disruption reduces the efficiency of the biological pump, the long-term balance between oxygen production and consumption would shift toward the atmosphere, potentially accelerating feedback effects.
