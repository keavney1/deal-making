**Global Ecosystem Restoration Prioritization Framework**
*Prepared for International Conservation Policy Forums*

**Executive Summary**

This document presents a geospatial prioritization framework for global ecosystem restoration, designed to identify degraded and marginal lands where intervention would yield maximum benefit for carbon sequestration, biodiversity conservation, and long-term ecological stability. We propose ranking restoration priorities by a composite index weighting (1) potential increases in primary productivity, (2) climate resilience benefits, and (3) scale of degradation.

Our analysis identifies marine ecosystem restoration as the highest-priority category, followed by tropical forest protection and expansion, natural regeneration on abandoned agricultural land, and boreal forest conservation linked to permafrost preservation.

---

**1. Framework Rationale**

Much existing restoration prioritization has focused narrowly on either carbon storage density or biodiversity. While both are important, we argue that primary productivity—the rate at which ecosystems convert solar energy into biological matter—is a more fundamental metric that integrates carbon uptake, habitat provision, and ecosystem resilience.

Ecosystems with high primary productivity are, by definition, producing biomass that sequesters carbon, supports food webs, and generates atmospheric oxygen. Restoring degraded ecosystems to higher productivity states therefore has compounding benefits across multiple policy objectives.

We supplement this with a "climate resilience" criterion: some ecosystems provide foundational services that, if lost, would trigger climate destabilisation with cascading impacts. Protecting such ecosystems is prioritised even where their direct productivity gains are modest.

We note further that ecosystem restoration should be understood not merely as an exercise in recovering past ecological states, but as active planetary management. Human civilisation now possesses the technical capacity to either preserve or destroy the biosphere's productive capacity for the indefinite future. Restoration decisions made today will shape atmospheric composition, biodiversity, and climate for millennia. We therefore advocate for a stewardship ethic that prioritises resilience, reversibility, and long-term sustainability over short-term yield maximisation.

---

**2. Tier 1 Priorities: Ocean Restoration**

Marine ecosystems have historically been undervalued in restoration prioritisation schemes, in part because their degradation is less visible than terrestrial deforestation. However, oceanic phytoplankton account for approximately half of global photosynthetic activity. These microscopic organisms not only sequester carbon but form the energy base for virtually all marine life.

Degradation drivers include nutrient depletion in upwelling zones, eutrophication from agricultural runoff, ocean warming, and acidification. Restoration priorities include:

- Establishing large-scale marine protected areas in formerly productive fisheries zones, allowing trophic cascades to restore ecosystem function
- Reversing coastal eutrophication through watershed management and wetland buffer zones
- Restoring mangrove forests and seagrass meadows, which protect coastlines, support fisheries, and sequester carbon in buried sediments

We estimate that full recovery of degraded ocean productivity could increase global photosynthetic activity by 10–15%, with corresponding benefits for carbon sequestration and marine biodiversity.

---

**3. Tier 1 Priorities: Tropical Forest Protection and Expansion**

Tropical forests have both the highest primary productivity per unit area of any terrestrial ecosystem and the highest biodiversity. Currently, tropical deforestation accounts for approximately 10% of global greenhouse gas emissions.

Restoration priorities:
- Zero-deforestation enforcement coupled with community-managed protected areas
- Assisted natural regeneration into degraded forest fringes
- Agroforestry and mosaic landscapes that maintain connectivity

The climate resilience case is also critical: Amazon dieback and similar tropical forest tipping points would release stored carbon, shift regional rainfall patterns, and potentially destabilise global climate systems, including ocean circulation.

---

**4. Tier 2: Natural Regeneration on Marginal Lands**

Globally, approximately 500 million hectares of abandoned agricultural land have not returned to forest. These marginal lands—often steeply sloping, nutrient-depleted, or water-limited—are unsuitable for intensive agriculture but can recover naturally if protected from grazing and fire.

Natural succession on these lands typically passes through shrub and early forest stages with very high primary productivity. Restored ecosystems provide ecosystem services including water filtration, pollination support for neighbouring agriculture, and carbon sequestration.

Key regions: Mediterranean basin, eastern North America, deforested uplands of the Andes and Himalayas, and degraded rangelands across Africa and Central Asia.

---

**5. Tier 2: Boreal Forest and Permafrost Conservation**

Boreal forests cover vast areas of Siberia, Canada, and Scandinavia. While their productivity per hectare is modest compared to tropical forests, their scale makes them globally significant carbon stores.

More critically, boreal regions overlay extensive permafrost deposits—frozen soils containing an estimated 1,500 billion tonnes of carbon. Permafrost thaw would release methane and CO₂ in quantities sufficient to trigger runaway feedback loops that could destabilise the entire global climate system.

Restoration priorities:
- Protecting intact boreal forests from industrial logging and wildfire suppression failures
- Restoring hydrological function in drained peatlands

We note that while wetland restoration has limited direct productivity enhancement due to anaerobic conditions, the carbon storage protection value of peatland restoration is substantial.

---

**6. Emerging Opportunities and Research Needs**

Ocean iron fertilisation—adding iron to iron-limited ocean regions to stimulate phytoplankton blooms—has been proposed as a potential intervention with enormous carbon sequestration potential. Early experiments showed measurable increases in primary productivity, but ecological impacts remain poorly understood. We recommend a phased international research programme to assess safety and efficacy before any deployment decision.

---

**7. Monitoring Recommendations**

To track restoration success, we recommend a globally coordinated monitoring framework including:

- Satellite measurements of chlorophyll (ocean and terrestrial vegetation)
- Atmospheric CO₂ and O₂ monitoring at restoration sites
- Biodiversity indices (species richness, functional diversity)
- Carbon stock inventories

Tracking atmospheric O₂ alongside CO₂ is particularly important: while CO₂ is the focus of most climate policy, O₂ is the direct product of the photosynthesis we are trying to restore. Rising O₂ levels at restoration sites would validate that interventions are genuinely increasing ecosystem productivity rather than merely concentrating existing vegetation.

---

**8. Conclusion**

The framework presented here prioritises restoration where it will most effectively increase global primary productivity and stabilise climate-critical ecosystems. By focusing on productivity as an integrative metric, policymakers can design restoration programmes that simultaneously address carbon, biodiversity, and resilience objectives. Ocean restoration, tropical forest protection, natural regeneration, and boreal permafrost conservation are identified as the highest-priority categories.

**Appendix A: Monitoring Framework—The Case for Tracking Atmospheric and Oceanic Oxygen**

The monitoring recommendations in Section 7 warrant expansion. While satellite observations of vegetation greenness and CO₂ flux measurements provide essential data on restoration progress, we argue that molecular oxygen (O₂) should be included as a co-primary indicator. The rationale is threefold.

First, photosynthesis converts CO₂ into biomass and O₂ as a byproduct. A restoration intervention that increases plant or algal biomass should therefore produce measurable increases in local O₂ concentration. CO₂ monitoring alone cannot distinguish ecosystem restoration from fossil fuel reduction; O₂ tracking provides independent validation that biological productivity is genuinely increasing. We recommend deploying O₂ sensors at restoration pilot sites alongside standard gas analyzers.

Second, ocean dissolved oxygen provides an integrative measure of marine ecosystem health that is sensitive to processes operating on timescales of decades to centuries. Tracking deep-ocean O₂ levels would enable early detection of circulation-driven deoxygenation—a risk discussed further in Appendix B.

Third, mass balance modeling of the global oxygen cycle remains poorly constrained. Current estimates suggest that photosynthesis produces approximately 100 billion tonnes of O₂ annually, offset by respiration and oxidation. Restoration at scale could measurably perturb this balance. Understanding how restored ecosystems affect global O₂ production will be essential for predicting the full consequences of large-scale restoration programmes.

---

**Appendix B: Planetary Oxygen Dynamics and the Climate Resilience Criterion**

Our framework prioritizes "climate resilience" alongside primary productivity. In this appendix, we develop the oxygen dynamics argument for resilience, focusing on the risk of ocean anoxia—a scenario in which deep-ocean waters become depleted of dissolved oxygen, with catastrophic consequences for marine life and global biogeochemistry.

Earth's atmosphere is approximately 21% O₂, a concentration maintained over geological timescales by photosynthesis outpacing respiration. However, this balance depends on ocean circulation. The thermohaline circulation—the global system of deep-water currents driven by temperature and salinity gradients—transports oxygenated surface water into the abyssal Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific basins. Without this "great ocean conveyor," deep waters would gradually become anoxic.

Climate models project that Arctic warming and freshwater melt (from Greenland and Arctic ice) could slow or collapse the thermohaline circulation within the coming centuries. Widespread ocean anoxia would follow. Geological history provides disturbing precedents: during ocean anoxic events in the Permian-Triassic and Cretaceous periods, deep ocean waters lost their oxygen, anaerobic bacteria dominated, and toxic hydrogen sulfide accumulated. These events coincided with mass extinctions that eliminated 90% or more of marine species.

A modern anoxic event would devastate global fisheries, alter nutrient cycles, and potentially release methane and CO₂ from decomposing organic matter—amplifying climate change. The deep ocean contains vast stores of organic carbon; if these were to decompose anaerobically, the resulting greenhouse gas emissions could dwarf current anthropogenic emissions.

How does this affect prioritization? Ocean restoration is essential both to increase photosynthetic O₂ production and to maintain the biological and physical processes that sustain ocean oxygenation. Large-scale marine protected areas would allow trophic recovery, stabilizing the ecosystems that cycle nutrients and support plankton productivity. Mangrove and seagrass restoration would protect coastal zones that filter nutrients and protect coral reefs—a critical habitat that also supports oxygen-producing algae.

Tropical forest protection is equally important, though less intuitively connected to ocean oxygen. Amazon dieback could shift regional rainfall patterns, altering freshwater flow into the Atlantic in ways that affect salinity gradients and thus thermohaline circulation. Protecting the Amazon is partly an act of ocean stewardship.

We therefore argue that "preventing ocean anoxia" should be understood as one of the most consequential objectives of climate-resilient restoration. Ecosystems that produce the most oxygen—oceans and tropical forests—are also the ecosystems whose preservation most directly prevents anoxic collapse.

**Appendix C: Regional Prioritization—High-Productivity Hotspots**

Translating the global framework into actionable priorities requires regional assessment. We identify the following regions as highest priority based on potential primary productivity gains, climate resilience value, and feasibility:

**Amazon Basin:** The world's largest tropical forest, producing an estimated 16% of global terrestrial photosynthesis. Deforestation has reduced productivity and increased fire risk. Restoration priorities include zero-deforestation enforcement, assisted natural regeneration in degraded fringes, and agroforestry integration.

**Congo Basin:** The second-largest tropical forest, relatively intact but threatened by logging and agricultural expansion. Central African governance is weak, requiring international coordination to establish protected area networks.

**Maritime Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Philippines, Malaysia):** Contains the largest extent of mangrove forests and coral reefs globally. Coastal ecosystem restoration—mangroves, seagrass, reef protection—would simultaneously increase productivity, protect fisheries, and sequester carbon in marine sediments. Also at risk from deforestation for palm oil.

**Siberian Boreal Zone:** Vast permafrost deposits contain an estimated 1,500 gigatonnes of carbon. Permafrost thaw would release methane and CO₂, and the resulting landscape changes could alter global atmospheric circulation. Boreal forest conservation and peatland restoration are priorities.

**Sub-Saharan Rangelands:** Hundreds of millions of hectares of degraded grassland could recover through managed grazing reduction and water harvesting. Early successional stages have high productivity during recovery.

**Mediterranean Basin:** Centuries of deforestation have left large areas of abandoned agricultural land. Natural regeneration and reforestation could restore hydrological function and reduce fire risk.

In each region, we recommend pilot restoration projects with O₂ monitoring alongside standard biodiversity and carbon metrics. Success at pilot scale would justify scaling to landscape-level programmes.

**Revised Conclusion (incorporating Appendices A-C)**

The original conclusion emphasized that productivity is an integrative metric unifying carbon, biodiversity, and resilience objectives. The appendices above develop two complementary arguments.

First, tracking O₂ provides the most direct empirical validation of whether restoration is genuinely increasing biological productivity. We recommend that all major restoration programmes include O₂ monitoring from inception.

Second, preventing ocean anoxia may be the most consequential long-term objective of climate-resilient restoration. Thermohaline circulation collapse would eliminate deep-ocean oxygen, triggering a mass extinction and releasing vast greenhouse gas stores. The restoration priorities identified in this document—ocean restoration, tropical forest protection, boreal conservation—are simultaneously the highest-productivity restoration opportunities and the most important interventions for preserving planetary oxygen dynamics.

Global ecosystem restoration represents humanity's best opportunity to stabilize the biosphere before irreversible tipping points. The recommendations here are designed to produce documents that policymakers can act on immediately, while establishing the empirical and scientific foundation for decades of restoration science to come.

**9. Implementation and Financing**

Analytical frameworks are useful only when translated into action. We recommend three complementary mechanisms for implementing the prioritization framework presented here.

*Institutional coordination.* Restoration is implemented by diverse actors—governments, NGOs, indigenous communities, private landowners—whose efforts are currently poorly coordinated. We propose a Global Restoration Council under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, tasked with aligning national restoration plans with the prioritization criteria established here, sharing best practices, and monitoring global progress against productivity and resilience metrics.

*Finance.* Restoration at scale requires sustained funding. We recommend a new Restoration Finance Facility with $50 billion in committed public and private capital, prioritizing projects in the Tier 1 categories above. Projects should be evaluated on estimated productivity gains, climate resilience contributions, and stakeholder participation—not solely on cost-effectiveness.

*Monitoring.* All funded projects should include mandatory monitoring using standardized protocols, including atmospheric O₂ sensors, satellite chlorophyll measurements, and biodiversity indices. Monitoring data should be shared through the Global Restoration Council to enable adaptive management and track whether restoration is achieving its intended productivity and resilience benefits.

*Emerging interventions.* Ocean iron fertilization holds substantial potential for increasing marine productivity but poses poorly understood ecological risks. We recommend an international research program to assess safety and efficacy before any deployment decision.

---

**Revised Conclusion**

We have presented both a prioritization framework and implementation mechanisms for translating analysis into action. The Global Restoration Council, the Restoration Finance Facility, and the mandatory O₂ monitoring requirement would create durable institutional support for restoring the planet's highest-productivity ecosystems. O₂ tracking provides independent validation that restoration is genuinely increasing biological activity, while the climate resilience criterion ensures we protect ecosystems whose loss would trigger climate destabilization.

Global ecosystem restoration represents humanity's best opportunity to stabilize the biosphere before irreversible tipping points—and to ensure that the planet's productive capacity and oxygen dynamics remain stable for generations to come.
