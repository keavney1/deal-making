**3. Section 1: Navigating Information — How to Find, Evaluate, and Trust What You Read**

**1.1 Why This Matters**
The internet puts more information at our fingertips than any library in history. But more information does not guarantee better understanding. Learning to sort trustworthy guidance from noise is perhaps the single most valuable skill you can cultivate, because it underlies every other decision — about health, money, civic participation, and more.

**1.2 Where to Look First**
When you need to understand something, a few categories of sources tend to be most reliable:
- **Government and public health agencies** publish materials reviewed by career experts.
- **University extension programs and nonprofit educational organizations** often produce guides written explicitly for non-specialists.
- **Peer-reviewed research summaries** (from sources like Cochrane for health, or arXiv for technical topics) provide vetted findings, though the original papers can be dense.
- **Established reference works** (dictionaries, encyclopedias with editorial oversight) are useful for definitions and broad context.

Be cautious with: anonymous forum posts, content from unknown authors, and materials that exist primarily to sell you something.

**1.3 Evaluating a Source**
Before relying on a piece of information, ask yourself:
- *Who wrote this?* Are they credentialed, or do they cite sources that are?
- *What is the purpose?* Is the article trying to inform, persuade, entertain, or sell? A piece trying to sell you a product may downplay downsides.
- *When was it published?* Guidance in medicine, technology, and finance changes over time. Prefer recent, updated sources.
- *Does it match other sources?* If you find the same basic information in several independent, credible places, it is more likely to be accurate.

**1.4 Detecting Misleading Framings**
Some information is technically true but framed to mislead. Common warning signs: dramatic headlines unsupported by the text, statistics without context ("50% increase in risk" sounds alarming — but 50% of a very small number may still be small), cherry-picked data that ignores contrary evidence, and emotional language designed to provoke fear or outrage rather than understanding.

When in doubt, seek the original source the article cites (or claims to cite). The original is often more measured than the summary.

**4. Section 2: A Living Glossary of Essential Terms**

This glossary defines terms that recur across the collection and across everyday life. It will grow as the collection expands.

| Term | Brief Definition |
|------|-----------------|
| **Bias** | A systematic tilt in information — toward a belief, an outcome, or an interest — that distorts accuracy. Not the same as an opinion; bias operates invisibly in how something is selected, framed, or reported. |
| **Cohort** | A group of people sharing a characteristic (age, condition, location) that researchers study together. |
| **Correlation** | A statistical relationship where two things tend to change together. **Does not mean causation.** Ice cream sales and drowning incidents both rise in summer; one does not cause the other. |
| **False Balance** | Presenting two sides of an issue as equally credible when the evidence strongly favors one. Common in media coverage of settled science. |
| **Gross vs. Net** | *Gross* = the total amount before deductions. *Net* = the amount remaining after deductions (taxes, fees, expenses). |
| **Literacy (in context)** | The ability to find, understand, evaluate, and apply information to make decisions. Applies to health, finance, digital tools, and more. |
| **Median** | The middle value in a list of numbers, sorted. More representative of a "typical" experience than the average (mean) when the data includes extreme values. |
| **Meta-analysis** | A study that combines results from many prior studies on the same topic, often producing stronger conclusions than any single study alone. |

**1.5 Advanced Search Techniques and Source Triangulation**

Once you know what you're looking for, the next skill is finding it efficiently. Even simple changes to how you search can surface far more useful results:

- **Use quotation marks** around a phrase to find that exact wording: `"renewable energy tax credit"` returns only pages with that precise phrase, cutting out tangential results.
- **Add a minus sign** to exclude irrelevant terms: `diet -"keto diet"` gives you broader dietary information without pages dominated by a single, specific approach.
- **Limit by site:** `site:.gov flu prevention` restricts results to government sites, a quick way to find vetted public health guidance.
- **Search within a specific date range** when you need recent information. Most search engines have a "Tools" or (?) menu that lets you restrict results to the past year.

**Source triangulation** means never relying on a single source for anything consequential. If you need to understand a medical recommendation, check it against a medical institution's patient page (e.g., the Mayo Clinic, the NHS), a government health agency, and at least one peer-reviewed summary when possible. When three independent, credible sources agree, you can have confidence. If they disagree, the disagreement itself is information — one may be outdated, one may address a different population, or the science may genuinely be evolving.

The habit of cross-checking is the difference between acting on a rumor and acting on reliable knowledge.

---

**1.6 Understanding Different Kinds of Source Bias**

Bias in information is not always malicious. It can come from how a newsroom chooses what to cover, from funding sources shaping what researchers study, or from the business model of a website that needs to keep readers engaged. Understanding the landscape helps you read critically:

- **Selection bias** in media: a source that never covers mistakes by one political party but emphasizes every one from the other is shaping your picture of reality regardless of how accurate individual stories are.
- **Funding influence:** studies funded by a food company that funds research favoring that company's products warrant extra scrutiny. Look for funding disclosure statements in research papers.
- **Algorithmic bias:** search engines and social media feeds show you more of what you already click on. This can narrow your world. Actively seek out sources you do not normally read.
- **Framing bias:** the same event can be reported as "Grievous Oversight Caught" or "Routine Audit Revealed Minor Issue." Both can be factually grounded yet leave very different impressions.

No source is perfectly unbiased. The skill is not to find the one pure source but to understand the pressures shaping each one and adjust your reliance accordingly. See Section 1.4 for detecting misleading framings, and see the Glossary entry on *false balance*.

**1.7 Everyday Information-Vetting Checklist**

When you encounter information and need to decide how much to trust it, walk through this checklist:

☐ Can I identify the author or the organization behind this? What are their credentials or track record?
☐ What is this piece trying to do — inform, persuade, entertain, or sell?
☐ When was it published, and is the topic one where timeliness matters (health, finances, technology)?
☐ Does the piece cite specific sources I can check independently, or does it rely on vague appeals ("studies show," "experts say")?
☐ Does the information match what I've found in other credible, independent sources? If there's a conflict, how do I account for it?
☐ Am I feeling unusually emotional (angry, frightened, triumphant) after reading this? Strong emotions are sometimes manufactured to bypass careful thought.
☐ Is a dramatic conclusion supported by proportionate evidence, or is a small-increase-in-risk framed as a large-increase-in-danger?

If you can confidently check most of these, you are in a position to trust or reject the information for good reasons rather than habit or impulse.

**3. Section 3: Practical Mathematics for Everyday and Professional Life**

Many capable people believe they are "bad at math" because school taught it as an abstract puzzle rather than a set of practical tools. The math this section covers is not difficult — it is *useful*, and mastering it can save money, reduce stress, and make you a more confident decision-maker at home and work.

---

**3(a) Percentages, Rates, and Interest — Making Them Intuitive**

A percentage is simply a fraction out of 100. That simplicity is your friend.

- **Calculating 10% mentally:** Move the decimal one place to the left. 10% of $340.00 is $34.00.
- **1%:** Move the decimal two places left. Use this as a base for other percentages.
- **15%** (a common tip): calculate 10%, then half of that (5%), and add them. 15% of $60 = $6 + $3 = $9.
- **20%:** Double the 10% amount. 20% of $85 = $8.50 × 2 = $17.00.

When a store says "30% off," you will pay 70% of the original price — or you can subtract 30% in your head using the tricks above.

Interest works the same way but is expressed as a *rate* per year. If your savings account pays 4% annual interest, then for every $100 you put in, the bank gives you $4 after one year. The magic of compounding means that interest also earns interest. Your $104 after year one earns 4% of *$104* in year two, not 4% of $100. The Rule of 72 gives you a fast estimate: divide 72 by the rate to see roughly how many years it takes to double. At 4%, about 18 years. At 8%, about 9 years. This is why even small differences in rates matter enormously over time.

**3(b) Budgeting Basics: Tracking Income and Expenses, Building an Emergency Fund**

A budget is not a punishment — it is a plan that keeps your money working for you.

Start honestly: write down every source of income and every recurring expense. Do this for a month if you work from paychecks, or for a week and multiply if you earn daily. Separate expenses into *needs* (rent, groceries, utilities, transportation) and *wants* (dining out, entertainment, subscriptions).

One useful framework is the **50/30/20 guideline** (adapt it to your circumstances):
- **Roughly 50%** of your income for needs.
- **Roughly 30%** for wants.
- **Roughly 20%** for savings and debt repayment.

If your situation does not match this, do not feel defeated — adjust it. The 60/20/20 split or even a different layout may fit your reality better. What matters is:
1. You spend less than you earn (or know clearly by how much you exceed it).
2. You *pay yourself first* — set aside savings at the start of each month, not with whatever is left over.

**The emergency fund** is your financial safety net. Aim to save enough to cover 3 to 6 months of essential expenses (housing, food, transportation, basic bills). Keep this money in an accessible, insured savings account — do not invest it and risk not having it when you need it. Start small if you must; saving $20 per week is over $1,000 in a year, and it will grow faster than you expect.

**3(c) Understanding Loans and Debt**

Borrowing money is often necessary and sometimes wise (buying a home, starting a business, handling a medical emergency). Understanding the terms protects you from costly surprises.

**APR (Annual Percentage Rate)** is the number you should compare across loans. Unlike the *interest rate* alone, the APR includes interest *plus* fees, giving you the true yearly cost of the loan. A lender may advertise a 5% rate but charge $800 in fees, pushing your real APR to 6.5%. Always ask and always compare APRs.

**Fixed rate** means your payment stays the same for the life of the loan — predictable and safe for planning. **Variable rate** means it can rise or fall with market conditions. Introductory variable rates can look very attractive and then jump sharply. If you take a variable-rate loan, mentally budget for the maximum payment you might face.

**Amortization** describes how loan payments are structured: early payments go mostly to interest, late payments mostly to principal. This is why paying off a loan early can save so much — you stop interest from compounding on top of itself.

- See *Glossary: Principal, Variable Rate, Unsecured Debt* for related terms.
- Never sign a loan document you have not read in full. If something is unclear, ask. See Section 1.7 on obfuscation.

| Decision | Before Signing |
|----------|---------------|
| Is the APR reasonably competitive with other lenders? | Compare at least 2–3 offers. |
| Is the rate fixed or variable? | Fixed if you need predictability; variable only if you understand the risk. |
| Can I comfortably afford the *total* cost, not just the monthly payment? | A lower monthly payment spread over many years can cost far more than a higher payment over fewer years. |
| Is this secured (collateral: house, car) or unsecured? | Defaulting on a secured loan places the collateral at direct risk. Unsecured debt carries less collateral risk but may have higher interest. |

**3(d) Estimation and Mental Math Shortcuts**

You do not always have a calculator nearby. These techniques handle most everyday calculations:

- **Rounding for a sanity check:** Before trusting a calculator result, round the numbers and estimate. $47.40 × 6 ≈ $50 × 6 = $300. If your calculator says $28, you know something went wrong.
- **Front-end estimation:** Use just the first digit of each number. 38 × 215 ≈ 40 × 200 = 8,000. Close enough to know whether the precise answer makes sense.
- **The complement trick:** Finding a percentage of a number is symmetric. 18% of 50 = 50% of 18 = 9. Use whichever is easier.
- **Doubling and halving:** To multiply by 2.5, double the number and add half of the original: 36 × 2.5 = 72 + 18 = 90.

These shortcuts serve double duty: they catch errors (in bills, in quoted prices, in your own planning) and they convey competence in professional settings where quick math saves time and builds trust.

**3(e) Reading Charts and Graphs Without Being Misled**

Charts are powerful communication tools, but they can also mislead. Learn to read them defensively.

- **Always check the axes.** A line graph that shows a dramatic spike might span a y-axis from 10 to 11 — a minor 10% increase that looks like a catastrophe. Ask where the y-axis starts. Does it start at zero? If not, the visual drama may not match the actual numbers.
- **Bar charts** are for comparing categories. The bars should start at zero so that length is proportional to value.
- **Line charts** show trends over time. Useful for tracking, say, local unemployment rates or your own savings balance month by month.
- **Pie charts** show proportions of a whole. They become unreadable with more than five or so slices. If you see twelve colored slices, the chart is likely hiding complexity rather than revealing it.
- **Percentages without context:** A chart showing "200% increase in complaints" without the base number can hide that this means complaints went from 1 to 3 total. Always ask: the percentage of *what*?
