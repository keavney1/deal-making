# Scalable, Non-Harmful Methods to Increase and Preserve Earth’s Atmospheric Oxygen Over Century-Long Timescales: A Cost-Effectiveness Prioritization

## Executive Summary

The partial pressure of molecular oxygen (O₂) in Earth’s atmosphere represents a dynamic equilibrium between oxygenic photosynthesis and oxidative sinks (respiration, combustion, weathering, and photodissociation). While total atmospheric O₂ is vast (~1.2 × 10¹⁸ kg), the net flux is delicate; anthropogenic combustion and ecosystem degradation are shifting the balance toward slow depletion and climate feedbacks that threaten long-term stability.

This report evaluates scalable, non-harmful interventions to preserve and, where possible, increase the atmospheric O₂ inventory over 100-year horizons. We find that **preservation dominates cost-effectiveness**: reducing O₂ consumption through decarbonization, halting deforestation, and protecting marine productivity offers the highest return on investment. Net increases in O₂ require coupling enhanced photosynthesis to durable carbon sequestration, or industrial water splitting with secure storage of the complementary reductant. Over century scales, the most promising trajectory combines immediate conservation, ecosystem restoration, maturation of industrial bio-mimetic systems, and the development of closed-loop life-support technologies that can ultimately operate beyond Earth—ensuring that oxygen-provisioning capabilities are not bounded by the terrestrial biosphere.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Scope and Objectives

We define an **O₂-preserving** intervention as one that reduces the rate at which atmospheric O₂ is consumed by geochemical or anthropogenic processes. An **O₂-increasing** intervention is one that results in a net addition of molecular oxygen to the atmosphere persisting over >100-year timescales. This requires that the carbon (or other reduced product) fixed during O₂ release remains sequestered; otherwise, subsequent respiration or combustion merely closes a temporary loop.

### 1.2 The Global O₂ Budget

Photosynthesis generates ~360 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ (gross), nearly all of which is re-consumed by respiration and decay. The small residual—net primary production (NPP)—is roughly balanced by oxidative weathering, combustion, and marine organic carbon burial (~0.1–0.2% of gross flux). Anthropogenic fossil fuel combustion consumes ~4 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹, a small fraction of the gross flux but a large perturbation to the long-term equilibrium. Over centuries, even minor imbalances accumulate.

### 1.3 Non-Harmfulness Constraint

Interventions must not measurably increase human mortality, reduce biodiversity, or destabilize biogeochemical cycles. This excludes, for example, large-scale iron fertilization with poorly constrained downstream ecology, or stratospheric injection of aerosols that might suppress global photosynthesis.

## 2. Analytical Framework

### 2.1 Metrics

- **Cost-effectiveness**: USD per teragram (Tg) of O₂ preserved or added, discounted at 2% yr⁻¹ over 100 years.
- **Scalability**: Maximum plausible flux (Tg O₂ yr⁻¹) by 2100, given resource and land constraints.
- **Durability**: Probability-weighted residence time of the associated carbon sink (or O₂ storage).
- **Co-benefits / risks**: Impacts on climate, food security, and biodiversity.

### 2.2 Prioritization Logic

We rank interventions by marginal abatement cost of O₂ (MAC-O₂), analogous to carbon MAC curves. Preservation (avoided combustion) generally lies at the low-cost left of the curve; net generation lies at the right. We prioritize left-to-right implementation, while noting that R&D for right-side methods unlocks century-scale ceilings.

## 3. Tier 0 – Preservation & Demand Reduction

The highest-return interventions for atmospheric O₂ stabilize the existing inventory by reducing oxidation fluxes. In marginal-abatement terms, these “left-side” options dominate because they avoid combustion and decomposition processes that are already occurring, frequently at negative or near-zero economic cost once health and climate co-benefits are internalized.

### 3.1 Fossil-Energy Substitution and Electrification

Anthropogenic fossil-fuel combustion consumes approximately 3.8–4.2 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹. Decarbonizing the energy system—via renewable electricity (solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal), nuclear energy, and electrification of transport, heating, and industry—directly removes this drawdown. Modern utility-scale solar photovoltaic and onshore wind have achieved levelized costs of electricity below new fossil capacity in most markets, meaning that a large fraction of this tier already exhibits negative MAC-O₂ when damages are included.

**Mechanism and stoichiometry.** Complete combustion of carbon-based fuels follows:
$$\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$$
The molar ratio yields **0.727 t O₂ consumed per tonne of CO₂ emitted** (32/44). A coal-fired generator emitting ~0.9 kg CO₂ kWh⁻¹ therefore consumes ~0.65 kg O₂ kWh⁻¹. Replacing that generation with zero-carbon alternatives preserves the corresponding O₂.

**Cost-effectiveness.** MAC-O₂ ranges from strongly negative (sunbelt solar displacing expensive diesel or coal peakers) to roughly $30–80 t O₂⁻¹ for decarbonized firm power and industrial heat in regions lacking cheap renewables. Full global decarbonization of the existing fossil power, transport, and building sectors could preserve **~3.5–4 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹**.

### 3.2 Avoided Tropical Deforestation and Degradation

Mature forests are approximately in metabolic steady state on multi-decadal scales: photosynthetic O₂ release is countered by autotrophic and heterotrophic respiration. However, clearing and burning vegetation causes an immediate pulse of O₂ consumption and eliminates future gross photosynthetic capacity. Protecting intact forests avoids the combustion pulse and preserves the biological buffer that dampens atmospheric perturbations.

**Mechanism.** Avoiding the emission of 1 t CO₂ from above-ground biomass combustion or soil oxidation preserves 0.727 t O₂. High-confidence jurisdictional REDD+ programs and project-based forest conservation currently price avoided CO₂ at $5–$30 tCO₂⁻¹, implying MAC-O₂ values of **~$7–$40 t O₂⁻¹**.

**Scalability.** Curbing tropical deforestation could prevent ~1.0–2.0 Pg CO₂ yr⁻¹ of emissions, corresponding to **0.7–1.5 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹**.

### 3.3 Methane and Short-Lived Hydrocarbon Abatement

Atmospheric methane ultimately oxidizes to CO₂ and H₂O via a net reaction consuming molecular oxygen:
$$\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
Molar stoichiometry gives **4.0 t O₂ consumed per tonne of CH₄ oxidized**. Current anthropogenic methane emissions (~360 Mt yr⁻¹) therefore constitute a deferred O₂ sink of ~1.4 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹. Reducing fugitive emissions from fossil-fuel infrastructure, landfills, and wastewater—especially when coupled to substitution of dirtier fossil fuels—preserves this latent drawdown.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Capping oil- and gas-system leaks is often the cheapest abatement available, with MAC-O₂ frequently **<$10 t O₂⁻¹** because the captured methane has market value. Agricultural methane (enteric fermentation, rice paddies) is more expensive, at roughly $200–$600 t O₂⁻¹, but remains within the envelope of many carbon-pricing schemes.

**Scalability.** A 50 % cut in anthropogenic methane emissions would preserve **~0.7 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹**.

### 3.4 Industrial Efficiency and Circular Material Flows

Primary steel production via blast furnaces, cement clinker calcination, and petrochemical cracking all entail fossil combustion and process emissions. Demand-side measures—scrap steel recycling in electric-arc furnaces (avoiding coke-based reduction), extend building lifetimes, lightweight design, and plastics recycling—lower O₂ consumption per unit of delivered service.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Industrial circularity often shows negative or zero MAC-O₂ where recovered materials undercut virgin feedstocks; otherwise, MAC-O₂ aligns with green-premium abatement costs for process heat, on the order of **$50–$150 t O₂⁻¹** for hard-to-abate sectors.

## 4. Tier 1 – Ecological Net O₂ Increase

To move beyond preservation and achieve a *net* increase in atmospheric O₂, carbon fixed via photosynthesis must be routed out of the short-term respiratory loop and into reservoirs with residence times exceeding 100 years. The geochemical equivalence between durable carbon sequestration and O₂ addition is the foundational axiom of this tier.

### 4.1 The Carbon–Oxygen Stoichiometric Equivalence

Oxygenic photosynthesis and aerobic respiration are stoichiometric inverses:
$$6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{photosynthesis}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2$$
$$ \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{respiration}} 6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Consequently, net addition of O₂ to the atmosphere is strictly coupled to the *net removal of oxidizable carbon from the biosphere–atmosphere reservoir*. Every **1 t CO₂ sequestered in a durable sink** corresponds to a **net addition of 0.727 t O₂** to the atmospheric inventory; every **1 t C buried** corresponds to **2.67 t O₂**. Interventions in this tier are therefore evaluated by the durability of their carbon storage as much as by their photosynthetic throughput.

### 4.2 Reforestation, Afforestation, and Long-Lived Wood Products

Expanding forest cover increases gross primary production. To yield net O₂ gain, the fixed carbon must not return to the atmosphere within the target horizon. Two pathways provide century-scale durability:

- **Harvested Wood Products (HWP):** Lumber used in buildings and infrastructure has typical service lives of 50–100 years. Carbon stored in HWP is temporary but directly tied to preserved O₂ on the century scale.
- **Biochar:** Pyrolysis of woody or agricultural biomass converts labile organic carbon into recalcitrant aromatic structures with soil residence times of centuries to millennia. The exothermic process yields syngas that can displace fossil fuels, creating a co-benefit O₂ preservation stream.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Afforestation and reforestation range from ~$20–$100 tCO₂⁻¹ ($28–$140 t O₂⁻¹). Biochar systems cost ~$60–$200 tCO₂⁻¹, but energy co-products and soil productivity gains narrow the net MAC-O₂ to roughly **$50–$150 t O₂⁻¹**.

**Scalability and constraints.** Global reforestation potentials are estimated at 0.5–3.0 Pg CO₂ yr⁻¹ (0.4–2.2 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹), constrained by competing land uses, water availability, albedo feedbacks at high latitudes, and the saturation of carbon stocks as forests mature. Only sustained management and protection ensures permanence.

### 4.3 Blue Carbon: Coastal Wetlands and Macroalgae

Coastal vegetated ecosystems—mangroves, tidal marshes, and seagrass meadows—bury organic carbon in anoxic sediments where low oxygen activity limits remineralization. Sediment accretion rates can sustain carbon burial for centuries to millennia. Kelp and other macroalgae can be cultivated and sunk to deep ocean sediments, though the durability of this pathway depends on bathymetric and biogeochemical conditions that inhibit rapid remineralization.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Conservation and restoration of existing mangroves and marshes cost $10–$40 tCO₂⁻¹ (MAC-O₂ **$14–$55 t O₂⁻¹**). De novo kelp-sinking ventures remain experimental, with estimated costs of $100–$300 tCO₂⁻¹.

**Scalability.** Coastal wetland restoration could deliver 0.1–0.3 Pg CO₂ yr⁻¹ (**0.07–0.2 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹**). Macroalgal systems, if ecologically managed to avoid nutrient pollution and benthic hypoxia, could theoretically scale to comparable magnitudes but require more validation.

### 4.4 Regenerative Agriculture and Soil Carbon Build-Up

Practices that increase soil organic carbon (SOC)—reduced tillage, perennialization, cover cropping, organic amendments, and biochar incorporation—transfer CO₂ from the atmosphere into soil pools. Mineral-associated organic matter can persist for decades to centuries, particularly in fine-textured soils or when recharged continuously.

**Cost-effectiveness.** MAC-O₂ is highly variable, ranging from **$30 t O₂⁻¹** for low-cost practice shifts (e.g., no-till on mechanized grain farms) to **$400+ t O₂⁻¹** for heavily monitored and verified programs with buffer pools against reversal.

**Scalability and risks.** Global cropland and pasture soils could conceivably sequester 0.4–1.2 Pg CO₂ yr⁻¹ (0.3–0.9 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹), but saturation limits, reversal risk from drought or future plowing, and challenges in MRV (monitoring, reporting, and verification) reduce the effective, durable flux. Agricultural sequestration ranks lower in cost-effectiveness certainty than forest biomass or biochar, though its co-benefits for food security and drought resilience are substantial.

### 4.5 Durability, Reversal Risk, and Monitoring

Ecological O₂ gains are only as durable as their carbon counterpart. A century-scale perspective requires:
- **Buffer pools and insurance mechanisms** to cover disturbance-induced reversals (fire, disease, land-use change).
- **Saturation accounting:** Forests and soils approach equilibrium; net O₂ addition declines to zero unless new area is continuously brought under enhancement.
- **Satellite and in-situ verification:** Synthetic-aperture radar, lidar biomass mapping, and soil carbon spectroscopy reduce verification costs and improve confidence in MAC-O₂ estimates.

For these reasons, Tier 1 methods are best viewed as a bridge: they can deliver meaningful net O₂ increases over the coming century, but their cumulative ceiling is bounded by land area and ecological limits.

Tier 2 – Industrial and Bio-Mimetic Net O₂ Generation

Whereas Tier 1 relies on biological photosynthesis coupled to carbon burial, Tier 2 employs industrial water splitting or engineered photosynthesis to release O₂ while sequestering or utilizing the complementary reductant (usually hydrogen or reduced carbon). These methods are currently more expensive but face fewer land-area constraints and offer higher theoretical ceilings.

### 5.1 Artificial Photosynthesis: Solar-Driven Water Splitting with Solid-Carbon Sequestration

Photovoltaic-electrolysis, photoelectrochemical cells, or solar thermochemical cycles dissociate water:

$$2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$$

If the co-produced hydrogen is used to reduce captured CO₂ to solid elemental carbon (e.g., via the Bosch reaction, molten-salt electrolysis, or catalytic pyrolysis) and that carbon is buried in anoxic storage, the net stoichiometry mirrors biological oxygenic photosynthesis:

$$\text{CO}_2 \rightarrow \text{C (solid)} + \text{O}_2$$

**Energy requirements and cost-effectiveness.** State-of-the-art alkaline or proton-exchange-membrane electrolyzers require roughly 50–55 kWh per kilogram of H₂. Because 1 tonne of O₂ demands only ~125 kg H₂ (stoichiometric ratio 32:4), the electrolysis energy is ~6–8 MWh per tonne O₂. Additional energy for CO₂ capture, compression, and solid-carbon reduction raises the total to ~10–20 MWh per tonne O₂. At renewable power prices of $30–80 MWh⁻¹, the energy cost alone implies **$300–$1,600 t O₂⁻¹**. Capital recovery, maintenance, and the thermodynamic penalty of reducing CO₂ to graphite-like carbon rather than short-chain fuels push current estimates to **$1,000–$4,000 t O₂⁻¹**. Solid-oxide electrolysis coupled to concentrated solar or next-generation nuclear heat could halve these figures by mid-century.

**Scalability.** The theoretical ceiling is effectively unbounded by land or freshwater: arid-zone solar arrays and seawater electrolysis could, if fully dedicated, outproduce the terrestrial biosphere. Realistic 2100 deployment is constrained by photovoltaic and electrolyzer manufacturing chains, rare-earth catalyst availability, and competition for renewable electrons. A plausible mature-market flux is **0.1–0.5 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹**.

**Non-harmfulness and risks.** The process releases no toxic effluent at point of operation. The chief risk is fugitive emission of H₂ or hydrocarbon intermediates during upscaling; because atmospheric H₂ ultimately oxidizes to water while consuming O₂, containment standards must target <0.1 % leakage to preserve the net O₂ gain.

### 5.2 Electrolytic Hydrogen with Geological Storage

Where solid-carbon chemistry is bypassed, water electrolysis can still yield net atmospheric O₂ if the complementary H₂ is permanently isolated from the oxidative cycle. Depleted natural-gas reservoirs, salt caverns, and engineered aquifers offer possible geological sinks.

**Leakage constraints.** Hydrogen molecules are small and reactive; atmospheric oxidation of leaked H₂ consumes O₂ via tropospheric OH chemistry on a ~2-year timescale. Consequently, century-scale O₂ accounting requires demonstration of annual leakage rates below ~0.1 %, achievable with polymer-lined caverns and cap-rock monitoring.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Without revenue from H₂ sales, MAC-O₂ is set by electrolyzer and storage costs. At projected long-term capital costs of $100–$300 kW⁻¹ and storage fees of $2–$5 kg H₂⁻¹, the all-in cost falls to roughly **$500–$1,500 t O₂⁻¹**.

**Scalability.** Global salt-dome and porous-media pore volume could accommodate tens of teragrams of H₂, but the inventory is finite. This pathway is most valuable as a transitional demonstration of industrial net O₂ generation rather than a multi-petagram-scale solution.

### 5.3 Closed Photobioreactors and Synthetic Biology

Engineered *Synechocystis*, *Nannochloropsis*, or heterotrophic platforms grown in closed photobioreactors (PBRs) achieve photosynthetic conversion efficiencies of 5–10 % of incident photosynthetically active radiation—two to five times the modal efficiency of C₃ crop canopies. The harvested biomass can be pyrolyzed to biochar, compacted for subsurface burial, or converted to recalcitrant bioplastics.

**Cost-effectiveness.** PBRs incur high capital, mixing, harvesting, and dewatering costs. Current end-to-end systems for durable carbon sequestration are estimated at $200–$600 tCO₂⁻¹, implying a MAC-O₂ of **$275–$825 t O₂⁻¹**. Automation, genomic yield enhancement, and vertical stacking could reduce this substantially.

**Scalability.** Because PBRs can be sited on non-arable land (deserts, urban margins, offshore platforms), they circumvent the land-use competition that limits Tier 1. Nutrient throughput—especially bioavailable phosphorus and fixed nitrogen—becomes the binding constraint at multi-teragram scales.

### 5.4 Boundary Condition: Direct Air Capture without Reductive Sequestration

Direct air capture (DAC) powered by renewable energy removes CO₂ from the atmosphere but does not, by itself, release O₂. Unless the captured CO₂ is subsequently reduced to solid carbon (as in §5.1) or permanently mineralized without re-emission, the process functions as O₂ preservation—avoiding the future combustion or climate-feedback O₂ drawdown that the CO₂ molecule would otherwise engender. Coupling DAC to solid-carbon synthesis is therefore the only DAC variant that qualifies as Tier 2 net O₂ generation.

## 6. Tier 3 – Geochemical Stabilization and Indirect O₂ Preservation

Enhanced weathering and ocean alkalinity enhancement do not directly liberate molecular oxygen; the reactions that bind CO₂ into bicarbonate or carbonate minerals are oxygen-neutral. Their value in an O₂ strategy is indirect: by accelerating CO₂ drawdown and buffering ocean chemistry, they attenuate the warming and metabolic feedbacks that threaten to deplete marine dissolved O₂ and destabilize the global oxidative budget.

### 6.1 Enhanced Silicate Weathering (ESW)

Spreading finely ground basalt or silicate rock on croplands accelerates:

$$\text{CaSiO}_3 + 2\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Ca}^{2+} + 2\text{HCO}_3^- + \text{SiO}_2$$

The bicarbonate eventually precipitates as calcium carbonate in marine sediments, locking carbon on geological timescales. Because no O₂ is produced contemporaneously, the benefit is framed as **avoided future O₂ loss**: every tonne of CO₂ removed from the fast carbon cycle prevents the cascade of greenhouse warming, ocean stratification, and hypoxia that would otherwise consume atmospheric and dissolved O₂.

**Cost-effectiveness.** ESW costs $50–$200 tCO₂⁻¹ when feedstock is regionally available. Expressed as an equivalent O₂ preservation cost, this yields an **indirect MAC-O₂ of $70–$275 t O₂⁻¹**.

**Non-harmfulness.** Basalt amendment can replace agricultural lime and supply micronutrients. Monitoring must exclude ores with elevated nickel or chromium, and dust exposure during spreading must comply with particulate-matter health standards.

### 6.2 Ocean Alkalinity Enhancement (OAE)

Addition of alkaline minerals (olivine, brucite, or quicklime) to surface waters increases the seawater buffer capacity, shifting the carbonate equilibrium to absorb atmospheric CO₂:

$$\text{CO}_2(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{HCO}_3^-$$

Like ESW, OAE generates no O₂ directly. Its contribution lies in moderating ocean acidification, thereby protecting calcifying phytoplankton and the efficiency of the biological pump that exports organic carbon—and its associated photosynthetically produced O₂—into the deep ocean reservoir.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Dispersed marine OAE remains experimental, with early cost estimates of $50–$150 tCO₂⁻¹, implying an **indirect MAC-O₂ of $70–$200 t O₂⁻¹**.

**Non-harmfulness constraints.** Rapid or localized pH elevation can harm sensitive biota. Deployment requires dilute, phased addition with high-resolution biogeochemical monitoring to ensure no acute toxicity events.

### 6.3 The Indirect Benefit in Context

Quantifying the O₂ return of geochemical stabilization is less precise than for direct preservation or net generation. Earth-system models project that unmitigated warming could reduce dissolved ocean O₂ by several percent over multi-century horizons—a flux of tens to hundreds of petagrams of O₂ when integrated over water-column volume. Any intervention that flattens the atmospheric CO₂ trajectory therefore preserves a diffuse but enormous O₂ stock. On the marginal-abatement curve, ESW and OAE occupy the mid-range: more expensive than fossil-fuel substitution, but competitive with many industrial net-generation pathways once secondary ecological benefits are internalized.

## 7. Tier 4 – Extraplanetary and Long-Term Unbounded Scalability

Whereas Tiers 0–3 are ultimately bounded by Earth’s surface area, insolation, and geological finite resources, Tier 4 considers O₂ generation beyond the planetary surface. Over multi-century horizons, space-based industrial ecology can access effectively unlimited solar energy and water/oxide feedstocks, decoupling molecular oxygen production from terrestrial biosphere limits.

### 7.1 In-Situ Resource Utilization and Space-Based Water Splitting

Lunar polar regolith and permanently shadowed regions contain estimated water-ice reserves of 10⁹–10¹⁰ tonnes. Electrolysis of this water in situ follows the same stoichiometry as terrestrial systems:

$$2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$$

With a molar yield of 32 g O₂ per 36 g H₂O, **1 tonne of water produces ~889 kg of O₂**. Because the Moon lacks a substantial atmosphere, O₂ can be accumulated in cryogenic surface reservoirs or orbital depots without the fugitive leakage concerns that constrain terrestrial geological hydrogen storage. Carbonaceous near-Earth asteroids add vastly larger inventories of water and organic feedstocks.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Near-term ISRU O₂ is valued primarily for life-support propellant, with effective costs >$10⁶ t O₂⁻¹ when amortized over current launch infrastructure. As cislunar manufacturing matures—utilizing regolith-derived silicon for photovoltaics, local metals for structural mass, and solar-thermal or nuclear process heat—incremental O₂ production costs could fall to **$10,000–$100,000 t O₂⁻¹** by 2100. Over longer horizons, space-based solar power satellites transmitting energy to in-space industrial nodes could drive costs toward terrestrial industrial parity.

**Scalability.** Processing merely 0.1 % of estimated lunar polar water reserves annually would yield ~10⁷–10⁸ t O₂ yr⁻¹. Asteroid and outer-solar-system ice inventories are orders of magnitude larger. The theoretical ceiling is set by available solar energy and mass throughput, not by planetary surface area.

**Non-harmfulness.** Lunar and asteroid extraction introduces no terrestrial ecological insult. Protocols must minimize biological contamination of scientifically valuable ice deposits, but the O₂ generation itself produces no toxic effluent.

### 7.2 Closed-Loop Life Support and Bioregenerative Habitats

Space habitats require closed-loop life-support systems (CLSS) to recycle crew-respired CO₂ back to O₂ via physicochemical (Sabatier/electrolysis) or bioregenerative (algae, higher-plant) pathways. At strict steady state, a CLSS preserves O₂: every mole consumed by respiration is regenerated. However, if engineered with surplus photosynthetic or electrolytic capacity and the excess O₂ is stockpiled rather than recycled, the system transitions from preservation to net generation.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Current CLSS O₂ costs for low-Earth-orbit operations are $10⁵–$10⁶ t O₂⁻¹. Autonomous, large-scale orbital or surface settlements could reduce this to **$1,000–$10,000 t O₂⁻¹** through economies of scale and closed-loop nutrient recycling.

**Scalability and redundancy.** A distributed network of orbital, lunar, and Martian habitats with collective surplus O₂ capacity functions as a redundant, off-world production grid. The aggregate O₂ inventory of such a network is decoupled from Earth’s oxidative budget, hedging against planetary-scale perturbations (large igneous provinces, impact winters, or runaway climate feedbacks) that could transiently deplete terrestrial atmospheric O₂.

### 7.3 Terraforming Research and Planetary-Scale Oxygen Engineering

Over multi-millennial timescales, directed planetary engineering of Mars could create a secondary atmospheric O₂ reservoir. Mars possesses abundant crustal oxides and polar water-ice deposits; high-temperature electrolysis or thermal reduction of regolith could release O₂. A partial-pressure O₂ atmosphere engineered to 20–100 kPa could hold an inventory of **10¹⁷–10¹⁸ kg O₂**, comparable to Earth’s own atmospheric oxygen mass.

Venusian atmospheric processing, while more speculative, is stoichiometrically rich: the dense CO₂ atmosphere could, in principle, be photoreduced or thermally dissociated to solid carbon and O₂, though the energy requirements (~10²⁶–10²⁷ J) place this in the far future.

**Cost-effectiveness.** Meaningful MAC-O₂ estimates are impossible today; the value lies in unlocking a theoretical ceiling unbounded by terrestrial limits.

### 7.4 Cosmological Longevity

Earth’s atmospheric O₂ is not permanent. Solar brightening, accelerating silicate weathering, and photodissociation with hydrogen escape will drive significant O₂ depletion within ~10⁹ years. Self-replicating, space-based industrial ecosystems powered by long-lived stellar energy—or eventually fusion—could sustain O₂ production indefinitely, converting water or oxide minerals into atmospheric or stored O₂. Tier 4 is therefore the only class of interventions with genuinely unbounded scalability on timescales exceeding planetary habitability.

## 8. Integrated MAC-O₂ Summary, Policy, and R&D Prioritization

### 8.1 The Marginal Abatement Cost Curve for O₂

Figure 1 (conceptual) arranges interventions by marginal abatement cost of O₂ (MAC-O₂) against cumulative annual flux:

| Tier | Intervention Class | MAC-O₂ (USD t O₂⁻¹) | Plausible 2100 Flux (Pg O₂ yr⁻¹) |
|------|-------------------|---------------------|----------------------------------|
| 0 | Fossil decarbonization, methane abatement, industrial efficiency | Negative to $80 | 3.5–5.0 |
| 1 | Forest/blue-carbon conservation, reforestation, biochar, soil carbon | $14–$400 | 0.5–3.0 |
| 3 | Enhanced weathering, ocean alkalinity (indirect) | $70–$275 | 0.2–1.0 |
| 2 | Industrial water splitting + solid-carbon burial, geological H₂ storage, PBRs | $275–$4,000 | 0.1–0.5 |
| 4 | Space-based ISRU, CLSS surplus, precursor terraforming systems | >$10,000 | <0.001 (pre-2100) |

The low-cost left side (preservation and ecological sequestration) can deliver the largest near-term flux. Industrial net generation adds a smaller but land-unconstrained increment. Extraplanetary systems contribute negligible flux before 2100 but define the asymptotic long-term ceiling.

### 8.2 Cross-Cutting Policy Enablers

- **Stoichiometric accounting standards.** Carbon-market registries should explicitly state the O₂ equivalence of durable sequestration: **0.727 t O₂ per t CO₂ durably stored; 2.67 t O₂ per t C buried**. This prevents conflation of temporary storage with permanent atmospheric O₂ gain.
- **Leakage regulation.** Mandatory measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) of H₂ and hydrocarbon leakage rates (<0.1 % yr⁻¹) is essential for Tier 2 and Tier 4 pathways to ensure net O₂ integrity.
- **Space-resource governance.** International frameworks for cislunar and asteroid extraction should classify water and O₂ as critical commodities, enabling secure long-term investment in off-world production capacity.
- **Public R&D stage-gating.** Funding should prioritize left-side deployment for near-term O₂ preservation while maintaining steady exploratory support for Tier 4, tied to Technology Readiness Level (TRL) milestones.

### 8.3 R&D Prioritization Roadmap

- **Immediate (2025–2040):** Maximize Tier 0 and Tier 1 deployment; demonstrate solid-carbon synthesis from electrolytic H₂ at >1,000 t yr⁻¹ pilot scale; validate geological H₂ storage containment over decadal timescales.
- **Medium-term (2040–2070):** Mature Tier 2; achieve <$500 t O₂⁻¹ for solar-to-solid-carbon systems; establish autonomous closed-loop bioregenerative habitats in cislunar space with demonstrated O₂ surplus.
- **Long-term (2070–2100+):** Scale ISRU O₂ production to >10⁶ t yr⁻¹; initiate Martian oxygen-release pilot programs; advance self-replicating, solar-powered industrial ecosystems in orbit or on the lunar surface.

## 9. Conclusion

Atmospheric O₂ is not merely a byproduct of Earth’s biological history; it is a measurable, manageable planetary inventory. Over the coming century, the highest-return strategy is unequivocally **preservation**: decarbonizing energy, halting deforestation, and abating methane deliver O₂-equivalent benefits at negative to low cost while avoiding irreversible loss of an existing stock. Ecological net increases through reforestation, blue carbon, and soil sequestration offer a valuable bridge, adding meaningful but ultimately bounded fluxes constrained by land area and carbon-saturation limits.

Industrial net O₂ generation—water splitting coupled to durable carbon burial or secure hydrogen storage—raises the ceiling and removes land constraints, though current costs remain orders of magnitude above preservation. Geochemical stabilization, while oxygen-neutral in its immediate chemistry, protects against climate-driven O₂ loss and merits inclusion in any integrated portfolio.

Looking beyond the terrestrial horizon, the long-term growth of humanity’s accessible O₂ inventory requires moving off-planet. Space-based water splitting, in-situ resource utilization, and closed-loop life-support systems offer scalability not limited by Earth’s ecology or its finite biosphere lifespan. Over centuries to geological timescales, cultivating the capacity to generate and preserve molecular oxygen independently of the terrestrial atmosphere is the only trajectory that ensures an enduring, unbounded O₂ legacy. The rational policy pathway is therefore to exhaust the cost-effective preservation and ecological options today while investing systematically in the industrial and extraplanetary infrastructure that defines the ultimate ceiling.

**Erratum to Section 1.2 — Global O₂ Budget Terminology**

The original text incorrectly identifies *net primary production (NPP)* as the small residual balanced by oxidative weathering and combustion. NPP is gross photosynthesis minus autotrophic respiration (~100 Pg C yr⁻¹, or ~270 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ equivalent) and is large, not small. The correct term for the long-term residual is **net organic carbon burial** (or the geological carbon sink), which represents only ~0.1–0.2 % of gross photosynthetic flux and is balanced on multi-millennial timescales by oxidative weathering, volcanic outgassing, and anthropogenic combustion.

## Appendix A. Derivation of Core Stoichiometric Conversion Factors

The factors below use standard integer molar masses (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16 g mol⁻¹) to maintain exact consistency with the values deployed throughout the report.

**A.1 O₂ per tonne CO₂ (combustion / photosynthesis pair)**
$$\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$$
$$\frac{M_{\text{O}_2}}{M_{\text{CO}_2}} = \frac{32}{44} = \mathbf{0.7273} \approx \textbf{0.727 t O}_2 \textbf{ per t CO}_2$$

**A.2 O₂ per tonne elemental C**
$$\frac{M_{\text{O}_2}}{M_{\text{C}}} = \frac{32}{12} = \mathbf{2.666\overline{6}} \approx \textbf{2.67 t O}_2 \textbf{ per t C}$$

**A.3 O₂ per tonne H₂O (electrolysis)**
$$2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$$
$$\frac{M_{\text{O}_2}}{2 \times M_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}} = \frac{32}{36} = \mathbf{0.888\overline{8}} \approx \textbf{0.889 t O}_2 \textbf{ per t H}_2\text{O}$$

**A.4 O₂ consumed per tonne CH₄ (atmospheric oxidation)**
$$\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
$$\frac{2 \times M_{\text{O}_2}}{M_{\text{CH}_4}} = \frac{64}{16} = \mathbf{4.00} \approx \textbf{4.0 t O}_2 \textbf{ per t CH}_4$$

These ratios are applied uniformly across all tiers of this assessment.

## Appendix B. Durability-Weighted O₂ Accounting for Cross-Tier Comparison

Because interventions differ in both cost and the residence time of their associated carbon sink, a single-period metric can mislead prioritization. We define the **Century O₂-Equivalent (COE)** of an intervention as the time-integrated, discounted stream of atmospheric O₂ benefit over a 100-year horizon:

$$\text{COE}_{100} = \int_{0}^{100} \dot{O}_2(t) \, e^{-(\lambda + r)t} \, dt$$

where $\dot{O}_2(t)$ is the instantaneous O₂ flux (or preservation) in year $t$, $\lambda$ is the hazard rate of sink reversal (yr⁻¹), and $r$ is the discount rate (here 2 % yr⁻¹).

For a one-time pulse of durable sequestration yielding $O_{2,0}$ at $t=0$ followed by exponential decay of the sink, $\dot{O}_2(t) = O_{2,0} \lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ for the ongoing benefit (or equivalently, the residual stock is $O_{2,0}e^{-\lambda t}$). The effective discounted tonne-years of O₂ added thus simplifies to:

$$\text{COE}_{100} = O_{2,0} \int_{0}^{100} e^{-(\lambda + r)t} \, dt = O_{2,0} \frac{1 - e^{-(\lambda+r)100}}{\lambda + r}$$

Illustrative values:

| Sink type | Hazard rate $\lambda$ (yr⁻¹) | $\text{COE}_{100}$ per t O₂ initially added (t O₂ yr) |
|-----------|------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Geological mineral carbonation | 0 | 43.2 |
| Biochar in stable soil | 0.002 | 35.4 |
| Geological H₂ storage (0.1 % leakage) | 0.001 | 36.9 |
| Mature forest conservation | 0.01 | 23.1 |
| Tropical reforestation | 0.02 | 17.0 |
| Unprotected agricultural SOC | 0.05 | 8.7 |

**Interpretation.** A tonne of O₂-equivalent preserved through fossil-fuel decarbonization (effectively permanent, $\lambda \approx 0$) generates ~43 discounted tonne-years of benefit within the century. The same initial tonne stored in an unprotected agricultural soil program generates only ~9 tonne-years. Normalizing cost-effectiveness by $\text{COE}_{100}$ rather than by first-year flux can reverse apparent priorities: an apparently cheap soil-carbon program with high reversal risk may be less attractive on a durability-adjusted basis than a moderately priced biochar system. Policy frameworks should require $\lambda$-explicit accounting, with tiered hazard rates tied to verified monitoring protocols and insurance buffers.
