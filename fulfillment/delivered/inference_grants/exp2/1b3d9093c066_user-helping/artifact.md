# Community Organiser's Resource Guide: Working the Systems for People in London

*A practical guide for small organisations doing policy-adjacent work in digital inclusion, educational equity, and climate adaptation — written so you don't need a dedicated analyst to use it.*

---

## A Note Before You Start

This guide exists because someone at your organisation — perhaps you — is working on one or more of three things that matter and are hard: making sure people are not shut out of a world that's gone digital, making sure opportunity in school and beyond doesn't map to postcode, or making sure neighbourhoods adapt and survive as climate pressures mount. These are not the same problem, but they share a feature: each one requires understanding institutions, navigating funding, and translating between what people need and what bureaucratic systems are built to deliver.

This document is intended to equip you to do that work practically. It will not fix anything for you. It will give you orientation, frameworks, concrete entry points, funding leads, and the kind of institutional fluency that costs organisations with analyst support a salary to develop. Everything here is meant to be found quickly and acted on.

## Section 1: Digital Inclusion — Frameworks

**The digital divide is not about devices.** A common mistake in digital inclusion work is to treat the problem as resource-poor individuals lacking hardware. Hardware matters, but the deeper divide runs along three dimensions — infrastructure (connection quality and reliability), skills (the ability to use digital tools to accomplish real objectives), and trust (the belief that engaging with digital services is worth the risk rather than simply imposed by authorities). Organisations that only address one dimension tend to pour resources into a programme that still leaves people behind.

For community organisations, this means your digital inclusion work should encompass:

- **Connectivity support**: identifying and helping households access affordable broadband. In London, the good news is that 5G coverage is widening and fibre rollout continues, but affordability remains the principal barrier for low-income households, not physical availability. Have a clear awareness of what schemes exist for subsidised connections — some major providers operate social tariffs, though take-up has been poor, which means awareness and support in accessing them is work in itself and genuinely valuable.
- **Skills sessions tailored to purpose**: not 'teaching the internet' but equipping people with the specific skills they need — whether that's using a council portal, managing a Universal Credit account, applying for jobs, or keeping up with a child's school communications. The closer skills training is to what people actually need to do, the more likely it is to stick.
- **Fear and disengagement as barriers**: especially for older residents and those without English as a first language, the digital world can feel hostile. Addressing this authentically — by providing buddying, drop-in hours at familiar community venues, and demonstrable patience — is as important as any technical intervention.

**Other frameworks worth knowing:**
- **The inverse care law, ap timely here**: communities least well-served by mainstream services are also those least likely to have the digital capability to navigate alternative channels. Digital exclusion, in other words, compounds other exclusions. This is not a coincidence — it is the shape of how service delivery works. Keep it in mind when deciding where to invest.
- **Device provision as triage, not solution**: if you distribute devices, accompany them with onboarding. A laptop given without setup support to someone anxious about technology is a laptop that gathers dust.

**London-relevant entry points (concretely):**
Public libraries across London offer free WiFi, computer access, and skills support. The London Boroughs each maintain community hub networks — the specific directory varies by borough, but your local Borough Council website's community or digital inclusion page is the authoritative starting point. The Greater London Authority's digital inclusion work is administered at borough level for implementation, but the programmes and funding streams exist at GLA level — knowing where the decision sits and where the delivery happens is the key piece of institutional knowledge that saves you months of chasing the wrong contact.

This is the beginning. I cover infrastructure, access points, and implementation guidance in subsequent turns.

**Implementation Considerations for Digital Inclusion Programmes**

Most community organisations asking about digital inclusion face a version of the same practical dilemma: limited budget, limited staff time, and a population with heterogenous needs. What follows is guidance on structuring your work so that scarce effort reaches the people who need it most.

**Device Programmes**
Acquiring hardware is often the first impulse, and it can be the right one — but only if accompanied by setup and ongoing support. A device without onboarding is a device that gathers dust.

- **Distribution as differentiation**: Target devices to the households where digital exclusion has the biggest downstream impact — those relying on public services, managing health conditions, seeking employment, or supporting children in education. A triage approach that priorities based on need avoids the common mistake of equal-but-shallow distribution.
- **Selection**: Laptops are more versatile than tablets for most practical purposes (filling forms, job applications, school communications, video calls). Refurbished business laptops through reputable circular-economy suppliers can be obtained at a fraction of retail cost; many programmes in London operate through partnerships with corporate donors or councils.
- **The onboarding session**: Hand-over should happen in a supported setting — not a hand-off but an activation. Budget for a session where the recipient is helped through initial setup, password management, and the first tasks they'll actually need to do.

**Skills Delivery Models**
- **Bite-sized, purpose-driven sessions**: The most effective approach teaches a skill someone will use tomorrow, not an abstract or comprehensive curriculum. A session on using the council's waste collection calendar is more valuable than one on 'the internet'. Design around outcomes: what is this person trying to do?
- **Buddying and drop-in**: One-to-one support at a regular, predictable time — in a familiar community venue — builds trust and allows people to bring the specific, often idiosyncratic problem they are stuck on.
- **Age-sensitivity and language support**: For older residents and those not confident in English, the barrier is often emotional as much as technical. Volunteer interpreters, patient pacing, and nothing that assumes prior familiarity with digital conventions. People learn faster when they are not made to feel ashamed of not knowing.

**Building Trust**
This is the most under-attended dimension of the digital divide. For communities that have experienced surveillance, poor service, or deliberate neglect, the internet can feel like an interrogation room, not a tool.
- Start where people are in the physical world — community centres, places of worship, food banks. The trust you have there transfers to the digital work if you frame it as enabling something good, not pushing compliance.
- Be transparent about data practices and privacy. Many people suspect, often rightly, that engaging with digital services makes them more visible to systems that have not served them well. Acknowledge that concern honestly.
- **The trust principle**: each digital session should leave a person better off than they arrived, with a skill they can use independently. The goal is autonomy, not dependency on your organisation.

**London-Specific Access Points**

This section continues across the next turn and covers the concrete institutional landscape: public libraries as free WiFi and skills hubs, borough community programme listings, and navigating the GLA/borough administrative split for service delivery.

## London-Specific Access Points for Digital Inclusion

Concrete access points are what separates an organisational plan from wishful thinking. In London, digital inclusion support sits across three layers: GLA-level programmes (which set strategy and allocate funding), borough-level programmes (which deliver services and maintain community infrastructure), and independent community organisations and libraries that are often the sites where people actually encounter help.

**Public Libraries**
Every London borough maintains a library service, and the vast majority have free WiFi, publicly accessible computers, and some form of digital skills support — whether formal classes or drop-in sessions. The key navigation detail is that programme offerings (such as formal drop-in hours) vary by borough and individual library branches, and change without centralized publicity. Your practical path: visit your nearest branch — ideally more than one branch in your borough if you are mapping availability for constituents — and ask explicitly what digital support they offer. Library staff are the best-live source for what is currently available; this is one of those areas where a static web directory is out of date almost immediately.

**Borough Council Community Programmes**
Each London borough's website includes a community or digital inclusion page (naming conventions vary). These pages list locally funded initiatives, community hub networks, and sometimes device-loan schemes. If your borough does not list a digital inclusion programme, contact the council's community services or digital team directly — programmes sometimes exist but are not, for whatever reason, publicised prominently. Ask specifically: Is there a digital champion scheme? Is there a device loan or distribution programme? Is there a partnership with Skills for Life providers?

**The GLA/Borough Split: An Important Institutional Fact**
The Greater London Authority oversees several strategy and funding streams relevant to digital inclusion, but the delivery of most programmes sits at the borough level. This means that for practical programmes, your primary contact is the borough council; for funding and strategic alignment, the GLA is the relevant body. Organisations that conflate the two — approaching the GLA for a local service, or the borough for pan-London funding — waste weeks of their time. Know which layer to approach for which purpose: borough for what's happening on the ground, GLA for the bigger picture and strategic resources.

**Skills and Confidence Programmes**
Refer to the bite-sized, purpose-driven buddying model outlined in the implementation section above. London is dense with potential partners — universities with student volunteering schemes, businesses with corporate social responsibility programmes, and faith communities with established volunteer networks. Forming the right partnerships is often the most durable intervention a small organisation can make — converting a programme that exists only while your organisation funds it into one sustained by a broader community.

## Section 2: Educational Equity — Frameworks

**Why the London Attainment Gap Matters**

London presents a specific version of a national problem: educational outcomes vary dramatically by geography, ethnicity, and socioeconomic background, and those outcomes are, to a large extent, predictable from a child's address and not from aptitude. The attainment gap is the measurable difference in progress and outcomes between disadvantaged children and their more advantaged peers. In London, this gap is especially visible because the capital's geographic diversity, income polarisation, and rapid demographic change amplify disparities that more homogeneous settings smooth over.

Community organisations working on educational equity should understand what the attainment gap actually is: not evidence that some children are less capable, but evidence that the education system as a whole delivers less to children in particular circumstances — and that those circumstances include poverty, housing instability, inadequate mental health support, limited access to enrichment activities, and well-documented biases in categorisation and selection processes within schools.

**Key framing concepts:**

1. **Progress, not just attainment**: A child's ability to catch up is itself evidence of what they are capable of; the system should be measured by how much it allows the gap to narrow, not only by absolute results. If you are presenting a case for a child or cohort who has made significant progress from a low baseline, the narrative of capability is as important as the narrative of need.

2. **System-level vs. individual intervention**: A child who is falling behind is not evidence of a deficit in that child. The more useful framing — for both the child's confidence and any institutional advocacy — is that the child is being underserved by a system designed around assumptions that do not hold for them. This reframes the problem as one of system adaptation, not of deficit correction.

3. **Supplementary, not substitute**: The role of a community organisation in educational equity is not to replicate what schools are for, but to fill the gaps that the school system, as currently structured, leaves open. This is an important positioning distinction: you are a partner to the school and a supplement to the state's provision, often advocating for the child and family precisely because the system is not yet meeting them where they are.

## Educational Equity — Navigating the School System

**Academies, Local Authorities, and Where Power Sits**

Understanding who controls what in London's school system is essential, because it determines who can make the change you are advocating for.

**The Academy Landscape**
Since the major expansion of academisation, the majority of London secondary schools and a growing proportion of primary schools are academies — schools that are state-funded but independently managed by an academy trust, and so sit outside direct Local Authority control for many budgeting and operational decisions. For a community organisation, this means:

- **The headteacher and trustees of the academy trust hold practical power** over what happens inside the school — funding allocation for supplementary programmes, staffing decisions, policies on admissions and behaviour, and decisions about curriculum enrichment.
- **The Local Authority retains certain formal obligations** — particularly around SEND (Special Educational Needs and Disabilities) and safeguarding — and maintains oversight and support functions even for academies. But the direct operational decisions that matter most for a child's day-to-day experience sit with the school and trust.

This has a practical implication: if you are trying to get a change made within a school — whether that is access to a reading programme, a re-evaluation of a child who has been misidentified, or equitable enrichment provision — the academy trust is the body to approach, and a direct, respectful, evidence-based conversation with the headteacher or a designated trustee is more likely to succeed than lobbying the Local Authority, which may not have the direct leverage it once did.

**Local Authority (LA) Controlled Schools**
The minority of maintained schools that remain under the Local Authority follow a different control structure. For these, the LA is the relevant authority for operational decisions, and district councillors or the LA's education team are the right points of contact. Importantly, the LA also maintains statutory responsibilities — including the handling of SEND needs, admissions appeals, and school-place allocation — that apply across both academy and LA-maintained schools. If your advocacy involves a statutory right (e.g., a right to a school place, or SEND provision), the LA remains the accountable body regardless of academisation.

**What This Means Practically for Community Organisers**
- When presenting a case for a child or cohort, approach the body that holds the operational authority: the academy trust for academised schools, the LA for maintained schools.
- The LA, particularly the statutory SEND team and the admissions team, remains essential for formal processes — e.g., EHCP (Education, Health and Care Plan) applications, school admissions appeals, and formal complaints.
- Borough-level councillors can be effective advocates: they bring political visibility and can raise issues that bureaucratic processes leave unaddressed.
- Department for Education matters can be escalated to DfE regional teams or the Education and Skills Funding Agency for systemic issues, but this is generally higher-level or last-resort, not the first point of intervention.

**Funding for Supplementary Provision — Overview**

Schools and community organisations can access several funding streams for supplementary educational provision, though these vary between centrally funded programmes and locally administered funding, and many are competitive rather than guaranteed. The next turn will cover these in detail: catch-up tuition funds, pupil premium application as a mechanism for school-level funding directed at disadvantaged students, mental health support in schools (which, increasingly, requires schools to have designated leads and can be routed through NHS partnerships), and access to extracurricular activities where cost is a barrier for specific students. The navigation of when to approach the school, when the LA, and when to advocate outside these systems — and the case-framing that makes advocacy effective — is the core of what follows.

**Funding for Supplementary Provision**

Organisations working on educational equity spend much of their time thinking about two things: what a child needs and whether anyone will pay for it. The funding landscape for supplementary provision in London sits across three layers — nationally allocated schemes administered by schools, competitive grants available directly to organisations, and in-kind or partnership resources that vary by borough. Understanding which lever to pull, and when, is inseparable from effective advocacy.

**Catch-Up Tuition and Vital Targeted Support**
The scope and availability of nationally funded catch-up programmes fluctuates with policy cycles. What does not fluctuate is the principle: when a child is behind, targeted, evidence-based tuition in small groups or one-to-one works — more reliably than most interventions a school can organise from its own staffing. Community organisations can serve several roles here: delivering tuition directly if they have qualified practitioners, advocating for a child to be included in a school's catch-up programme, or — in the absence of dedicated funding — identifying alternative sources such as local grant-giving trusts that fund educational catch-up work. Small, specific, time-bounded programmes tend to be funded more readily than open-ended proposals, so frame your programme around Measurable impact for a defined cohort over a clear period.

**Pupil Premium**
Pupil premium is funding allocated to schools based on the number of pupils entitled to free school meals, looked-after children, and children from service families. It is substantial and can be used at the school's discretion — but discretion is the point. The school decides how to spend it, and community organisations typically cannot access directly it. However, it is one of the most important mechanisms to understand for advocacy. If you are advocating for a child or cohort who qualifies for pupil premium, you are de facto advocating for the school to spend its pupil premium funding more effectively. Knowing this reframes your ask: you are not asking for money from a pot that doesn't yet exist, you are asking for existing money to be spent toward a different purpose. That is a different conversation and often a more successful one.

How to use this knowledge practically:
- When you present a child's needs to a school, know whether the school's pupil premium budget could theoretically cover what you are proposing. If so, your case is not just moral — it is also financially coherent.
- Advocate for transparency in how pupil premium is spent. Schools are required to publish a strategy statement, and reading this tells you what priorities already exist and where there may be room to redirect or expand.
- For academies, governance structures differ — some academy trusts centralise pupil premium spending across schools in their trust, making the trust level (not the individual school) the relevant decision-maker. Know your target organisations governance structure before you ask.

**Mental Health in Schools and the NHS Link**
Emotional and behavioural health directly shapes educational outcomes, yet it is the dimension least well-coordinated between schools and health services. Schools increasingly designate mental health leads, and the NHS operates Mental Health Support Teams (MHSTs) in some areas, but there are significant postcode and capacity variations. For a community organisation, the practical entry points are:
- Be aware of the Designated Mental Health Lead in the schools you work with — they are the mechanism through which the school liaises with NHS provision and arranges targeted mental health support. Building a working relationship with that lead is one of the highest-leverage activities you can do.
- Where MHSTs or school-based counselling exist, they are typically accessed through the school itself, not through self-referral by families. Your role may be to identify the need, help the family phrase their request coherently to the school, and follow up to ensure the referral is pursued.
- For children with more severe needs, the EHCP (Education, Health and Care Plan) pathway is relevant — this is a statutory document that can encompass mental health as well as educational needs, and securing or updating an EHCP is a concrete, high-impact action. Navigating EHCP processes is bureaucratic and slow; community organisations that help families through it are genuinely and directly reducing a child's time spent unsupported.

**Extracurricular Access**
The enrichment gap — differential access to music, sport, creative activities, and social development — is one of the quieter drivers of the attainment gap. Children from disadvantaged backgrounds attend fewer and less diverse extracurricular activities, and the reasons are financial (fees, equipment), logistical (transport, parental availability), and informational (not knowing what is available). Organisations can help by:
- Mapping free and subsidised activities in their area — local authority leisure services, sports club outreach, arts charities, and school-facilitated clubs are all potential sources.
- Providing transport or travel costs where a barrier exists. Small, specific funding for travel can be the decisive intervention.
- Advocating with schools to subsidise or waive fees for disadvantaged pupils for activities that are not free to all.
- Understanding that access to extracurriculars improves what researchers call cultural capital — the knowledge, dispositions, and social skills that help navigate institutions — and this has direct bearing on educational outcomes and life chances.

**Case-Framing and Cohort Advocacy: How to Advocate for One Child or Many**

The systems you are navigating — schools, local authorities, trusts, DfE — are designed to operate at scale, efficiently, and impersonally. They are not designed to calibrate themselves to the specific needs of one child or one group. This is not malicious; it is structural. And it means your most important skill is not persuasion in the abstract, but the ability to translate a child's specific situation into the language and categories those systems already use to allocate resources and make decisions.

**Tone — Respectful Persistence, Not Confrontation**
Institutional fluency demands what might seem like a contradiction: you must be both dogged and cooperative. The temptation, when a child's needs are not being met, is to escalate to accusation — to tell the school or authority that it is failing. Occasionally that is necessary, but it should be a considered step, not the default, because a school willing to work with you is vastly more useful than one that has been put on the defensive. Your tone should convey that you share the institution's goals — the child educated, the gap closed — and that you are bring information and evidence to help them achieve that. This is an internal cultural rule for effective institutional advocacy, not a universal ethical commandment; it reflects the reality that you achieve more for the child when the system cooperates than when it resists you.

When you do escalate — when persistence has been met with inaction, or when you are dealing with a pattern that suggests systemic neglect rather than a single administrative gap — frame the escalation in terms of institutional obligation and shared responsibility. Emphasise that a pattern has emerged, that standard channels have been exhausted, and that you are escalating because the child's interest demands it, not because of personal animus.

**Evidence — Document Reliably and Frame Around the Institution's Own Criteria**
The best advocacy is built on evidence that the institution itself recognises. For schools, this means:
- League-table-consonant metrics: is the gap between your child's progress and their peers widening? If your child is at the 5th percentile on nationally normed assessments, that is the kind of fact a school finds hard to dismiss, because the school itself is measured on those figures.
- Attendance and exclusion data: patterns of frequent absence or repeated exclusions often signal unmet needs and are a clear avenue for intervention.
- Impact of existing provision, documented: if the school has provided intervention that has not worked, that is evidence that more or different intervention is needed.
- Externally sourced evidence: educational psychology assessments, speech and language reports, or paediatric evaluations carry institutional weight that parent or community observations alone do not. Getting these is often the hardest part of the work, and it is where many organisations step in — either by funding them or by navigating NHS referral pathways.

**Institutional Fluency**
This is ultimately about understanding what the institution you are approaching cares about and framing your case within the goals of decision-makers, rather than demanding that they change their goals to match yours. That framing is not dishonest — you are asking less of the institution than you might want, but you are multiplying the chance that the child actually gets helped.

## Section 3: Local Climate Adaptation — Frameworks

**Adaptation Versus Mitigation**

This is the distinction that most often confuses people new to climate policy work — and the reason many grant applications go unfilled. Mitigation means reducing emissions: less carbon, fewer fossil fuels, more renewables. That is urgently important global work. But local community organisations, particularly those in built-up urban areas like London, spend much of their time on adaptation — preparing people, places, and services for the effects of climate change that are already happening or locked in. Adaptation includes everything from managing flood risk and heat mortality to addressing air quality, supporting food-growing networks in urban spaces, and building community resilience so that climate shocks — heatwaves, floods, supply chain disruption — do not hit the most vulnerable most severely.

The reason adaptation grants are chronically unfilled is that the adaptation framing is harder. Mitigation projects are relatively clean: install solar panels, reduce energy use. Adaptation requires understanding complex local conditions, crossing sectors (planning, health, transport, housing), and contending with the fact that the systems most at risk — vulnerable housing, overburdened infrastructure — are precisely the systems least likely to have dedicated capacity for change. This is where community organisations have a genuine role: they add local knowledge, trust, and boots-on-the-ground capacity that departments and developers cannot easily replicate.

**London-Specific Environmental Pressures**

- **Extreme heat and the urban heat island**: London is measurably and meaningfully hotter than surrounding areas. Victorian-era housing stock, where many low-income residents live, is poorly suited to adaptation — high humidity, poor ventilation, no shading.
- **Flood risk**: Parts of London — especially along the Thames corridor, Lea Valley, and areas with Victorian drainage infrastructure — face acute surface and fluvial flood risk. Climate projections increase the frequency and severity of events that current infrastructure was not built to handle.
- **Poor air quality**: Despite significant improvement over decades, London still has areas where nitrogen dioxide and particulate matter exceed WHO guidelines. This has health impacts disproportionately borne by communities living along major roads in already-deprived areas.

**Institutional Pathways for Local Climate Adaptation**

Climate adaptation funding sits across borough resilience plans (each London borough is required under national and GLA policy to maintain one), GLA programmes (such as adaptation and green infrastructure funding, though these shift across mayoral terms), and national funds. The navigation challenge mirrors educational equity — funding decisions sit in a tiered and sometimes overlapping structure, and the organisation that maps pathways clearly, identifies who holds the relevant budget, and connects to decision-makers saves months of effort. Practical advice in the cross-cutting chapter covers this in general terms; the specific climate chapter will detail adaptation-relevant funding structures.

**Climate Adaptation — Implementation and Practical Guidance**

**What Community Organisations Can Actually Do**

Adaptation work can feel weighty and beyond the reach of small organisations, but the core practical contributions are specific and achievable:

*1. Heat-health community response*
When extreme heat arrives, it is often the oldest and most isolated residents who suffer most. Victorian terraced housing in low-income areas absorbs and retains heat. Air conditioning is rare. Community organisations can:
- **Establish a "cool spaces" network**: identify air-conditioned or naturally cool venues — libraries, community centres, places of worship — and publicise these as safe spaces during heatwaves, including visiting people who cannot travel.
- **Vulnerable resident check-ins**: a structured telephone or visit-based check-in programme during amber or red heat-health alerts, drawing on GP practice or social housing lists (with appropriate consent and data-sharing agreements). Local NHS Clinical Commissioning Group public health teams or borough public health departments can sometimes support or endorse these.
- **Preventative advice**: practical guidance in community languages, distributed through trusted channels, on keeping cool — hydration, closing curtains, damp skin, and when to seek medical help. Avoid generic material; make it actionable for people in the housing conditions actually present in your community.

*2. Surface water and flood risk:*
London's combined Victorian drainage infrastructure was not built for modern rainfall intensity. Community organisations can:
- **Promote sustainable drainage (SuDS) and rain gardens** in community and housing association land: capturing and slowing surface water reduces local flood risk and makes land more productive and cooler. Consult the Environment Agency's flood risk maps for your area — these are publicly available and should inform where effort is concentrated.
- **Community flood action groups**: where identified flood risk exists, organising residents with flood kits, communication trees, and liaison with the Environment Agency and borough emergency planning teams.

*3. Air quality: community sensing as an advocacy tool*
Low-cost air quality sensors have made it possible for community organisations to generate their own evidence. Monitoring NO₂ and PM₂.₅ at key locations — near busy roads, school gates, housing estates — produces data that institutions find harder to dismiss than community testimony alone. The data's real power is in advocacy: showing a councillor the actual reading at a child's school gate is a form of inquiry they cannot deflect without action. Organisations such as Environmental Research Group at Imperial College London provide resources and model sensor networks that can guide your work.

*4. Urban greening and food growing*
Tree planting, community gardens, and allotments serve multiple adaptation functions — cooling, surface water retention, air quality improvement, and food resilience. These are also among the most locally actionable: borough tree groups and allotment societies already exist and insurance mechanisms (though tree planting on adoptable land may require council permissions, usually straightforward for community-led schemes). Green roofs are more complex but can be proposed for community buildings, and some GLA programmes have specific funding for this.

**Institutional Pathways**

*Understanding borough resilience plans:* Every London borough has a climate-related resilience or adaptation framework. These are found on the borough's planning or environment pages, or through their climate change officer. They define the borough's priorities for adaptation and how they will spend adaptation funding. Knowing your borough's resilience plan is essential: it tells you which adaptation priorities are already authorised and where there may be gaps to raise. If your borough does not prominently display its plan, email the climate change or sustainability officer directly — they are obligated to have one and to consult on its contents.

*GLA programmes:* The Greater London Authority (Mayor's office) manages funding for green infrastructure, climate adaptation, and green spaces. These are typically borough-competitive funding rounds announced in specific policy windows. Following the GLA's environment and climate programme announcements — via the GLA's consultation and funding portals — is the way to catch opportunities. The details of which streams are open shift over time; the key is checking regularly. The point of access is the GLA's environment and climate pages, or direct outreach to the relevant GLA environment programme team.

*National funds:* Flood and coastal erosion risk management programmes, the Green Infrastructure Standards Programme, and Defra schemes exist but the principal entry point for London community organisations is through the borough — as the borough is the accountable body for delivering national schemes locally. Approaching the GLA for strategic engagement and the borough for practical delivery remains the correct split.

## Section 4: Cross-Cutting Guidance

### Finding and Securing Funding in London

**The Landscape**

There are more funding opportunities available to London community organisations than most realise, and the consistent problem is not that money does not exist but that organisations lack the time and expertise to navigate the competitive landscape effectively. The practical reality is that funding is a finite and contested resource; the organisation with a well-matched, well-written application will secure support that an equally worthy but less carefully targeted organisation will not.

**Where to Look**
- **National Lottery Community Fund** is the largest independent funder of community activity in the UK and funds programmes relevant to all three chapters of this guide. Its funding steams cover everything from small grants (£300–£10,000) to longer-term strategic partnerships. The key is careful self-selection: study the current funding programmes, their priorities, what has recently been funded in comparable London boroughs, and only apply to programmes that genuinely match your project. Applications to mismatched programmes waste your time and the funder's.
- **Local grant-giving trusts** exist in many London boroughs and are often under-advertised. Your local Council for Voluntary Service (CSV) or volunteer centre maintains lists of local trusts. These tend to have simpler application processes and fund smaller, locally specific work — they are often excellent matches for small organisations.
- **London Community Foundation** and other regional funders operate pooled funding schemes that may be relevant.
- **GLA funding programmes**: beyond the environmental programmes described above, the GLA periodically runs funding for civil society organisations in areas including community health, digital inclusion, and educational support — exact schemes vary, so monitoring the consultation portal and following appropriate communications channels is essential.
- **Borough council small grants** are often available for locally-initiated community projects, though amounts are modest. The advantage is locality: decisions are made by people who know your area, which can favour well-evidenced local applications.

**The Matching Principle**
The single most important principle in applying for funding is alignment: your project should address what the funder has been set up to fund. That sounds obvious, but many organisations shape the funding around the project they want to do regardless of funder intent. The more effective approach — and the one that generates the most value from limited application-writing capacity — is to construct the projects your organisation runs in a way that also matches fundable programme criteria. This is not cynicism; it is how limited support gets transformed into real capacity. If you know that a leading funder supports digital inclusion for older people, design your digital skills delivery with older people prominently included. If another values partnership working, actively develop your collaboration with other local organisations and present a joint application.

**Application Writing: What Makes the Difference**
- **Clear outcomes, not intentions**: state what will change, why, and how you will know. "We will help with digital skills" is not the same as "60 residents will be able to independently access council services online, measured by pre- and post-skills assessment."
- **Budget that demonstrates propriety**: itemise, justify, and ensure that core costs are reasonably covered. Many small organisations undermine an otherwise strong application by showing a budget that is either inflated beyond credibility or that leaves staff unsupported.
- **Evidence of need**: census data, local authority statistics, your own service-user data, or baselining studies all strengthen credibility. Baseless claims weaken applications.
- **Delivery plan and milestones**: show when things will happen. Funders invest in a plan, not a promise.

### Working with Local Authorities and the GLA

**The Tiered Reality**

Governance in London involves multiple layers, and much of the frustration community organisations encounter comes from reaching the wrong layer for the wrong purpose. The principle is:

- **Borough council**: the body that delivers most frontline services, controls most local programme funding, and is the accountable body for national schemes delivered locally. If you need a small grant, a service referral, a planning permission, or a community venue — you are working at borough level.
- **Greater London Authority**: sets strategic policy, manages pan-London funding programmes, and holds oversight functions. If you are seeking to influence strategy, apply for GLA funding, or are engaging with mayoral policy — you approach the GLA.
- **Department for Education, Defra, Department for Health and Social Care**: for national policy, systemic issues, or programmes administered through the borough but accountable to Whitehall. Escalation here is generally for systemic advocacy, not individual case work.
- **MP and local councillors**: your Member of Parliament represents constituent interests, and local borough councillors hold borough-level political authority. Both can write letters, raise issues in formal meetings, and add visibility to campaigns. Do not overlook them, especially where bureaucratic processes have stalled.

**Knowing Which Tier to Approach**

When you bring a problem, ask three questions:
1. Is this a service or programme my borough council is responsible for delivering?
2. Is this an issue my borough could act on under its own powers, or does it require GLA or national-level action?
3. Who holds the budget for this?

The answer determines your point of contact. Approaching the wrong tier wastes time, builds frustration, and sometimes — when you are told repeatedly that something is "out of our hands" — leads organisations to conclude that nothing can be done when, in fact, the right contact in the right tier would have acted.

**Building Working Relationships**
One of the most effective investments a community organisation can make is building sustained, professional relationships with designated contacts at the borough — in education, environment, public health, and community services. This does not require networking in any performative sense; it means treating your interlocutors as collaborators who share the same goals, keeping your communications factual and respectful, and working to understand the constraints they operate under. When you demonstrate that you understand the system, institutions work with you more readily. This applies equally at GLA level and muster the same principle.

### Coalition-Building Across Organisations

No single small organisation working in London has the staff, credibility, or cover to influence large institutions alone. Coalitions change that — but the most effective ones are built around practical coordination, not formal governance structures that consume more energy than they produce.

**Identifying natural allies.** Organisations working on intersecting issues in the same geography often arrive at the same doors independently with the same requests. The first step is mapping: which organisations share your core interests and bring complementary assets — audiences, skills, venue access, language support? Borough-level voluntary sector forums, local anchor networks, and thematic alliances (education equity collectives, climate adaptation partnerships) are natural starting points. Ask at your nearest community hub or volunteer centre who else is working on your issue.

**The case for coalitions.** Joint applications to funders are more credible than individual ones. Shared policy advocacy is louder and harder to dismiss. Peer support reduces isolation and burnout. The practical rule is: collaborate wherever the collaborative entity can accomplish something you cannot alone.

**Governance and maintenance.** Keep it light. Rotate leadership roles, hold irregular meetings focused on shared actions, and invest governance effort in proportion to the objectives. The purpose is getting things done, not building a permanent structure to admire.

**Where leverage is concentrated.** Borough consultations on policy, planning, and funding are where coalitions are often most effective. One coordinated submission carries more weight than ten disconnected individual responses. Note the tiered principle from below: approach the right tier — borough for local delivery, GLA for strategy — with evidence that is specific, coordinated, and hard to dismiss.

### Making Evidence Credible and Documentation Audit-Ready

The gap between having impact and being able to demonstrate it is a gap that too many organisations fall into. Filling that gap is what separates an organisation that feels valued from one that can prove its value — to funders, to councils, to the people it serves.

**Document the inputs.** Record your time, activities, reach (numbers of people, interactions), and costs. Use simple spreadsheets with dates, descriptions and amounts. Retain receipts and timesheets.

**Document the outputs.** Immediate results: number of people trained, calls made, sensors deployed, sessions run. Quantify wherever possible.

**Document the outcomes.** Changes in the lives of people served — harder to measure but more important. Pre- and post-assessments, brief follow-up surveys, and clear case studies with consent demonstrate genuine impact. This is what both funders and institutional decision-makers respond to.

**Audit-ready means consistent and accessible.** Maintain a single, dated filing system. Be able to produce any receipt, attendee list, or evaluation form on request. The institutional value here is straightforward: a funding body or auditor should be able to verify that what you claimed happened actually happened, with evidence rather than trust.

**Anonymisation and consent.** Ensure personal data used in case studies or reports is appropriately anonymised and obtained with informed consent before publication. Document this consent as part of your evidence trail. This protects the people you work with and adds professional credibility to your organisation.

The standard aligns with the principle of respectful persistence throughout this guide: the evidence you present should be reliable enough that it cannot be reasonably questioned, and honest enough that you do not need to worry about it being questioned.
