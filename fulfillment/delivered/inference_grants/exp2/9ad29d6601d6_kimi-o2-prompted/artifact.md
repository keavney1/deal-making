# Global Oxygen Preservation and Enhancement Roadmap (2025–2035)
## Comprehensive Technical Report on Scalable, Safe Interventions to Increase and Preserve Atmospheric and Dissolved Oxygen

### Executive Summary

Atmospheric molecular oxygen (O₂) is being drawn down by fossil fuel combustion at a rate of approximately 4 petagrams of O₂ per year (Pg O₂ yr⁻¹), while ocean warming and nutrient loading are expanding oxygen minimum zones (OMZs) and coastal hypoxic areas. Although the atmospheric reservoir is vast (~1.2 × 10¹⁸ kg), the net biogeochemical flux buffer is sensitive to land-use change and marine ecosystem degradation. This report evaluates four intervention domains—terrestrial reforestation and ecosystem restoration, marine ecosystem restoration, artificial photosynthesis, and industrial oxygen generation—and presents a prioritized action matrix for the next decade based on net O₂ yield, durability, scalability, safety, and cost trajectories.

**KeyFindings:**
- **Terrestrial ecosystem restoration** offers the largest near-term gross O₂ flux potential, but net atmospheric gain depends on preventing the re-oxidation of fixed carbon.
- **Marine ecosystem restoration** (macroalgae, seagrass, nutrient remediation) can address dissolved oxygen collapse directly while scaling across vast areas with minimal land-use competition.
- **Artificial photosynthesis** and direct electrochemical approaches are approaching economic viability for industrial O₂ co-production and localized atmospheric management.
- **Industrial cryogenic and vacuum swing adsorption (VSA) systems** provide high-purity O₂ but are energy intensive; their climate-relevant deployment depends on renewable energy abundance.

**Prioritization Framework:** Each intervention is scored on (1) Gross photosynthetic or separation flux, (2) Net durable O₂-equivalent retention, (3) Scalability to teragram-to-petagram annual scales, (4) Ecosystem and human safety profile, and (5) 10-year cost trajectory.

### 1. Introduction: The Global Oxygen Cycle and Anthropogenic Perturbation

Earth’s oxygen cycle is defined by massive gross fluxes: photosynthesis produces roughly 360 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹, nearly balanced by respiration, decomposition, and combustion. Superimposed on this equilibrium are three anthropogenic perturbations relevant to a decadal strategy:

1. **Fossil fuel combustion:** A unidirectional O₂ sink consuming ~4 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹, with cumulative losses exceeding the replenishment rate from silicate weathering and organic carbon burial.
2. **Biomass and soil degradation:** Deforestation and agricultural conversion have reduced global net primary productivity (NPP) and increased soil organic carbon oxidation, diminishing the biospheric buffer.
3. **Ocean deoxygenation:** Warmer waters exhibit reduced O₂ solubility; thermal stratification limits deep-water ventilation, while agricultural runoff drives eutrophication and bottom-water hypoxia. The percentage of ocean area characterized as hypoxic has increased tenfold since 1950 in documented coastal zones.

Atmospheric O₂ concentrations have declined by roughly 0.001% yr⁻¹ over recent decades—imperceptible to human respiration but geochemically significant over century scales. Dissolved oxygen in marine environments is declining faster, with volume of OMZs expanding by several million cubic kilometers per decade.

**Report Structure:** Sections 2 through 5 examine terrestrial, marine, artificial photosynthetic, and industrial interventions. Section 6 integrates these into a prioritized implementation roadmap. Section 7 addresses monitoring, verification, and risk governance.

### 2. Terrestrial Reforestation and Ecosystem Restoration

#### 2.1 Photosynthetic Flux Potential
Global forest NPP is approximately 60 Pg C yr⁻¹. Reforestation and restoration of degraded non-agricultural lands (an estimated 350 million hectares globally after accounting for human land requirements and climatic suitability) could theoretically sequester 1.0–1.5 Pg C yr⁻¹, corresponding to gross O₂ release of 2.7–4.0 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ via photosynthetic stoichiometry.

#### 2.2 Net O₂ Accounting and Durability
Gross photosynthesis does not guarantee net atmospheric O₂ accumulation. When biomass decomposes or burns, the oxidation of stored carbon consumes an equivalent molar quantity of O₂. Durable net O₂ production requires that fixed carbon be protected from rapid re-oxidation:
- **Long-harvested wood products:** Mass timber and engineered lumber in buildings can protect carbon for 50–100 years, yielding a temporary net O₂ legacy.
- **Peatland and wetland restoration:** Waterlogged, anoxic conditions suppress decomposition. Peatlands cover ~3% of the land surface but store ~30% of global soil carbon. Restoration of 50 Mha of degraded peatland by 2035 could protect a carbon stock of 20–30 Pg C with near-negligible O₂ reconsumption rates.
- **Soil carbon enhancement:** Biochar amendment and regenerative practices increase mean residence time of soil organic matter, though net O₂ gains are modest relative to above-ground biomass.

#### 2.3 Albedo and Non-CO₂ Climate Feedbacks
High-latitude afforestation decreases surface albedo (particularly in snow-covered winter months), causing radiative forcing that may locally offset climate benefits and indirectly increase respiratory O₂ drawdown in warmed soils. Prioritization should therefore favor:
- **Tropical and subtropical moist forests:** High NPP, minimal albedo penalty, rapid biomass accumulation.
- **Temperate native broadleaf restoration:** Moderate NPP with lower albedo trade-offs than evergreen conifer plantations at high latitudes.
- **Avoided conversion of existing forests:** The marginal O₂ and carbon preservation value of intact ecosystems exceeds that of new planting by a factor of 2–5 over the first 30 years.

#### 2.4 Implementation Priorities for 2025–2035
1. **Zero net loss of intact tropical forests** via enforcement of existing protected-area statutes and supply-chain transparency (immediate priority).
2. **Rewet and restore 50 Mha of degraded peatlands** (Indonesia, Congo Basin, boreal Canada/Russia) with methane management protocols.
3. **Reforest 100 Mha in tropical/subtropical zones** using native mixed-species assemblies; exclude monoculture plantations that fail to maximize NPP stability or biogeochemical resilience.
4. **Incentivize long-life timber substitution** for concrete and steel in construction to extend the durable carbon/O₂ retention horizon.

#### 2.5 Barriers, Risks, and Safeguards

Large-scale terrestrial restoration is subject to biophysical and socioeconomic constraints that can negate gross O₂ gains.

**Fire and disturbance feedbacks.** Climate change is lengthening fire seasons across the tropics and boreal zones. A single severe fire event can oxidize decades of accumulated biomass, consuming O₂ and releasing CO₂ in days. Mitigation requires spatially heterogeneous “mosaic” restoration incorporating firebreaks, natural fire corridors, and pyrophobic riparian buffers. Fast-growing, flammable exotic monocultures should be avoided in favor of fire-resilient native assemblages.

**Hydrological limits.** Afforestation in water-limited regions reduces streamflow and groundwater recharge through enhanced evapotranspiration. Empirical catchment studies suggest closing‐water‐balance forest restoration should generally be restricted to areas where mean annual precipitation exceeds ~800 mm yr⁻¹, or where reliable dry-season groundwater access exists. Misplaced plantations can trigger drought-induced mortality pulses that release stored carbon and its O₂ equivalent.

**Trace-gas trade-offs.** Peatland rewetting suppresses CO₂ and N₂O emissions but can elevate CH₄ fluxes, particularly in tropical systems. Because atmospheric oxidation of CH₄ consumes O₂ (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O), high CH₄-emitting wetlands yield a lower net O₂ benefit per tonne of stored carbon than boreal analogs. Net O₂ accounting should subtract the O₂ cost of expected CH₄ oxidation from gross burial gains. Water-table management strategies that maintain stable, moderately anoxic (rather than deeply inundated) conditions can suppress methanogenesis while preserving carbon stocks.

**Socioeconomic durability.** Competing land uses—pasture, commodity crop expansion, and illegal logging—often generate higher short-term private returns than restoration. Secure land tenure and 30+ year contractual protection are necessary; without enforcement, “leakage” via displaced deforestation erases local gains.

### 3. Marine Ecosystem Restoration and Dissolved Oxygen Preservation

#### 3.1 The Ocean Oxygen Crisis: Drivers and Inventory Context

The ocean contains roughly 6 × 10¹⁵ kg of dissolved O₂—about two orders of magnitude less than the atmospheric reservoir but critical for marine aerobic metabolism. Dissolved O₂ has declined by 0.5–2% globally since the mid-20th century, with losses accelerating in the tropical thermocline and coastal upwelling zones. Three mechanisms dominate:
1. **Solubility reduction:** For every 1 °C of mean ocean warming, O₂ solubility drops by ~5–10 µmol kg⁻¹.
2. **Stratification:** Enhanced thermal and haline stratification reduce ventilation of subsurface waters, lengthening residence times in O₂-depleted interior layers.
3. **Eutrophication-driven respiration:** Terrestrial nitrogen and phosphorus runoff fertilize surface phytoplankton; sinking organic matter fuels microbial respiration in bottom waters, creating hypoxic (<63 µmol L⁻¹) or anoxic zones.

Unlike terrestrial photosynthesis, the majority of marine gross primary production (~50–60 Pg C yr⁻¹) is respired within the ocean interior rather than buried. Consequently, marine interventions offer two distinct O₂ pathways: (i) increasing the biological carbon pump to sequester carbon (and its photosynthetically equivalent O₂) in anoxic marine sediments, and (ii) preventing the consumption of existing dissolved O₂ by curtailing eutrophication.

#### 3.2 Macroalgae and Kelp Forest Restoration

Macroalgae forests—notably *Macrocystis pyrifera*, *Laminaria*, and *Sargassum* spp.—exhibit areal net primary productivity of 1,000–2,500 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹, among the highest of any aquatic system. Their gross O₂ photosynthetic flux is substantial. However, net durable O₂ production depends on the fraction of biomass exported from the coastal photic zone and sequestered in deep-sea or sedimentary sinks.

**O₂ accounting and durability:** Most macroalgal detritus is remineralized in the coastal ocean or on the continental shelf. Only an estimated 1–10% of macroalgal production reaches the deep sea (>1,000 m) for long-term isolation from the atmosphere. Consequently, gross O₂ release is largely matched by subsequent respiration. Local dissolved O₂ benefits in surface waters during active growth are real but transient, equilibrating with the atmosphere on week-to-month timescales.

**Strategic role:** Kelp restoration should be prioritized where it can:
- Remediate localized coastal hypoxia through enhanced daytime photosynthetic oxygenation.
- Provide physical substrate for biodiversity recovery in degraded temperate and subtropical coasts.
- Be coupled with deep-sink harvesting or controlled-sinking protocols to increase carbon (and O₂-equivalent) durability.

Scale estimate: Restoring historical kelp forests (estimated global loss of ~1–2 Mha over recent decades) could yield meaningful local dissolved O₂ buffering, but likely <0.01 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ in net durable atmospheric gain unless coupled to active sinking.

#### 3.3 Seagrass and Coastal Wetland Restoration

Seagrass meadows (*Posidonia*, *Zostera*, *Thalassia* spp.) exhibit lower areal productivity than kelp (~200–600 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹) but exceptional carbon burial efficiency. Their sediments are frequently anoxic, suppressing decomposition and yielding organic carbon accumulation rates that can exceed those of many terrestrial soils on a per-unit-area basis.

**Net O₂ yield:** Carbon burial in seagrass soils averages 50–150 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹, corresponding to a durable net O₂ release of ~130–400 g O₂ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Global historic seagrass cover has declined by >20%. Restoration of 5–10 Mha by 2035 could sequester 10–30 Tg C yr⁻¹ (27–80 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹ equivalent) with multi-century durability.

**Dissolved O₂ co-benefits:** Active seagrass beds oxygenate rhizospheres and adjacent bottom waters during daylight, directly mitigating local hypoxia. They also filter suspended particles and absorb land-derived nutrients, indirectly protecting downstream O₂ stocks.

#### 3.4 Nutrient Remediation and Hypoxia Prevention

The most immediate marine O₂ intervention is not production but *preservation*—preventing anthropogenic respiration from consuming dissolved O₂. More than 700 coastal systems now suffer seasonal hypoxia. Microbial respiration of each mole of exported organic carbon in bottom waters consumes approximately 1.0–1.4 mol O₂.

**Intervention suite:**
- **Agricultural source control:** Precision fertilization, vegetated riparian buffers, and winter cover crops reduce N and P export to rivers.
- **Wastewater upgrading:** Tertiary treatment and denitrification at coastal outfalls cut anthropogenic nutrient loading.
- **Restored coastal interception wetlands:** Constructed or rehabilitated estuarine marshes and mangroves retain riverine nutrients before they reach open shelves.

**Scale:** The Mississippi River plume, the Baltic Sea, and the East China Sea alone account for tens of thousands of cubic kilometers of seasonally hypoxic water. Reducing anthropogenic nutrient loading to pre-disturbance levels in these systems could recover dissolved O₂ concentrations equivalent to preventing the consumption of 0.1–0.3 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ (as O₂ retained in water columns rather than respired). While this does not add new molecules to the planetary inventory, it preserves existing dissolved O₂ that would otherwise be biologically consumed, supporting marine food webs and reducing the atmospheric O₂ drawdown needed to re-equilibrate depleted waters over centennial scales.

#### 3.5 Net O₂ Accounting in Marine Systems

Marine photosynthesis generates roughly 140–170 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ gross, yet the net air-sea O₂ flux is near zero on annual to decadal scales because respiration and remineralization are tightly coupled. Permanent O₂ accumulation requires permanent carbon burial. The global oceanic biological pump buries only ~0.15–0.25 Pg C yr⁻¹ in long-term sedimentary reservoirs, equivalent to a net O₂ yield of ~0.4–0.7 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹.

Therefore, marine restoration scores lower on *gross* photosynthetic flux than terrestrial forestry, but its *net* retention efficiency per unit area can exceed open-ocean phytoplankton schemes because benthic coastal systems concentrate burial in anoxic sediments. For O₂-maximizing strategy, marine interventions should be judged primarily on:
1. **Areal burial efficiency** (g C m⁻² yr⁻¹ converted to long-term sedimentary stocks).
2. **Prevention of respiratory O₂ loss** (hypoxia remediation).
3. **Absence of countervailing trace-gas emissions** (notably N₂O expansion from eutrophic waters and OMZs).

#### 3.6 Cautionary Assessment: Ocean Fertilization and Artificial Upwelling

Ocean iron fertilization and macro-nutrient augmentation have been proposed to stimulate phytoplankton and enhance the biological pump. However, field experiments indicate low verified carbon burial efficiency (<10% of surface-drawn-down carbon reaches sedimentary sinks), while elevated respiration at depth can expand OMZs and increase N₂O production—a potent greenhouse gas whose climate forcing indirectly threatens the stability of terrestrial carbon and O₂ fluxes. Artificial upwelling of nutrient-rich deep water risks introducing corrosive, low-pH, low-O₂ water into surface ecosystems. Neither approach is recommended for safe, scalable deployment within the 2025–2035 window.

### 4. Artificial Photosynthesis and Solar-Driven Oxygen Generation

Artificial photosynthesis aims to replicate the light-driven water-oxidation chemistry of natural photosynthesis using engineered photoelectrodes, photocatalysts, or biohybrid assemblages. Unlike terrestrial or marine biomass production, these systems do not rely on multi-year organismal growth cycles, and they can in principle operate in non-arable environments. However, their net contribution to global atmospheric O₂ depends critically on the fate of the electrons and protons abstracted from water: only if the resulting reductant (H₂, reduced carbon, or other product) is durably sequestered does the co-evolved O₂ represent a persistent addition to the atmospheric inventory.

#### 4.1 Photoelectrochemical and Photocatalytic Water Splitting

Photoelectrochemical (PEC) cells use semiconductor absorbers to generate the electrochemical potential required to split water. The anodic half-reaction liberates O₂:

\[
2\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \mathrm{O_2} + 4\mathrm{H^+} + 4e^- \quad (\Delta G^\circ = +474.2\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}\ O_2})
\]

State-of-the-art tandem III–V and emerging chalcogenide/oxide systems have demonstrated solar-to-hydrogen (STH) efficiencies exceeding 18–19% in laboratory settings, while stable, earth-abundant oxide systems (BiVO₄, Fe₂O₃, Ta₃N₅) achieve 5–10% STH under outdoor conditions.

**Areal O₂ flux and scalability.** Assuming a mean annual solar irradiance of 200 W m⁻² and a practical STH efficiency of 10%, a PEC panel produces chemical energy at 20 W m⁻². The thermodynamic minimum energy input per mole of O₂ is 474 kJ; thus the sustained molar flux is approximately \(4.2 \times 10^{-5}\) mol O₂ s⁻¹ m⁻², or roughly 40 kg O₂ m⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.04 Tg km⁻² yr⁻¹). A globally significant flux of 0.1 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ would therefore require on the order of 2.5 million km² of continuously operating PEC surface area—an area larger than Kazakhstan—plus associated reactant delivery and product collection infrastructure. This is implausible by 2035 and likely infeasible on any relevant timescale for atmospheric O₂ enhancement. PEC systems are better characterized as a niche tool for localized O₂ management (e.g., closed life-support, high-value industrial co-production) rather than a planetary-scale source.

#### 4.2 Biohybrid and Synthetic Biological Systems

Biohybrid approaches couple inorganic light harvesters (quantum dots, nanorods) with metabolically engineered microbes that fix CO₂ and release O₂. Engineered *Synechococcus* and *Nostoc* strains with truncated light-harvesting antennae achieve higher culture densities and reduced photoinhibition, raising volumetric productivity in closed photobioreactors to 5–10 g O₂ L⁻¹ d⁻¹ under optimized conditions.

**Net O₂ durability.** Gross photosynthetic O₂ evolution is not the binding constraint; microbial biomass typically respires or decomposes within weeks to months, reconsuming O₂. Strategies to convert fixed carbon into recalcitrant forms—exopolysaccharide biofilms, biologically induced carbonate precipitation, or sinking silica-ballasted aggregates—are under development, but burial efficiencies remain below those of natural peat or seagrass systems. Furthermore, the nutrient (N, P, Fe) and water demands of high-density cultivation introduce supply-chain and eutrophication risks that partially offset gains.

#### 4.3 Reactor-Based Artificial Photosynthesis: CO₂ Reduction with O₂ Co-Production

Photocatalytic and photovoltaic-powered electrochemical reactors can reduce CO₂ to C₁–C₄ products (CO, formate, ethylene, ethanol), with water oxidation providing the requisite electrons and liberating O₂. For complete reduction to a carbohydrate-equivalent state (CH₂O), the stoichiometry approaches:

\[
\mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH_2O} + \mathrm{O_2}
\]

yielding a molar O₂-to-fixed-C ratio of approximately 1:1 and a mass ratio of 2.67 kg O₂ per kilogram of carbon durably stored. Partial reductions (e.g., CO₂ to CO or formate) release less O₂ per carbon atom fixed.

The net atmospheric O₂ benefit is therefore determined by the *durability* of the reduced carbon product:
- **Durable polymers or construction materials:** If reduced carbon is embedded in structural plastics or carbon-fiber composites with century-scale lifetimes, the co-produced O₂ persists in the atmosphere on climatically relevant timelines.
- **Combustible fuels:** CO₂-to-methanol or CO₂-to-kerosene processes that vent O₂ during synthesis but burn the fuel within months to years are inventory-neutral over the fuel cycle; the O₂ is simply cycled through water and back.

Current global capacity for electrochemical and photocatalytic CO₂ reduction is measured in kilotonnes per year. Scaling to 1 Gt CO₂ yr⁻¹ fixed into durable products (yielding ~2.7 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹) would require renewable electricity inputs of roughly 500–1000 EJ yr⁻¹ depending on overpotential and faradaic efficiency—comparable to total current global primary energy use. This underscores that artificial photosynthesis is not a near-term substitute for natural carbon burial.

#### 4.4 Energy Budgets, Material Constraints, and Safety

PEC deployment at even modest scales faces material bottlenecks. Tandem III–V systems rely on indium and gallium; scalable oxides require cobalt or nickel catalysts in abundance, but long-term photostability in electrolyte remains problematic. Oxygen and hydrogen co-evolution in unseparated reactor geometries creates explosive mixtures, necessitating membrane separation that adds ohmic losses. Toxic leaching of cadmium or lead from some chalcogenide photocathodes poses occupational and ecological hazards if reactors are deployed at sea or in runoff-prone areas.

#### 4.5 Net O₂ Accounting Summary for Artificial Photosynthesis

Artificial photosynthesis does not bypass the conservation of reducing equivalents. Net persistent O₂ production equals the molar quantity of electrons diverted into *permanently stored* reduced products divided by four. Unless the reductant is sequestered, artificial systems function as a temporally delayed water-splitting and recombination loop, yielding zero net inventory change. For the 2025–2035 horizon, the primary role of artificial photosynthesis is technological development and high-value niche deployment, not teragram-scale atmospheric O₂ enhancement.

### 5. Industrial Oxygen Generation, Separation, and Geological Co-Storage

Industrial processes already produce, separate, and concentrate O₂ at multimegatonne scales. Understanding their true impact on global O₂ inventory requires a strict distinction between *separation* (redistributing existing atmospheric O₂) and *generation* (liberating O₂ from water or minerals). Only the latter can increase total planetary O₂, and only if the associated reductant is permanently withheld from oxidation.

#### 5.1 Cryogenic Air Separation and Vacuum Swing Adsorption

Cryogenic air separation units (ASUs) fractionally distill liquefied air to produce high-purity O₂ (≥99.5%) and co-product nitrogen and argon. Vacuum swing adsorption (VSA) and pressure swing adsorption (PSA) use zeolite or carbon molecular sieves to produce lower-purity O₂ (90–95%) at smaller scale and lower energy intensity. Combined global output for industrial O₂ exceeds 100 Mt yr⁻¹ (0.1 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹), primarily serving steelmaking, chemicals, and medical supply.

**Atmospheric inventory impact:** Both cryogenic and adsorptive methods separate O₂ already present in the atmosphere. Venting the concentrated O₂ after industrial use, or consuming it in oxy-combustion and metallurgical processes, leaves the total atmospheric O₂ pool unchanged (apart from minor leakage to the stratosphere or lithosphere). ASU and VSA are therefore inventory-neutral redistribution systems. Their relevance to global O₂ preservation lies in process efficiency: reducing the energy—and thus fossil-fuel-derived O₂ consumption—required per tonne of separated O₂.

#### 5.2 Electrochemical and Thermochemical Water Splitting

Alkaline, proton-exchange membrane (PEM), and solid-oxide electrolysis cells (SOEC) split water using electricity or high-temperature heat:

\[
2\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{O_2}
\]

This reaction *does* create new atmospheric O₂ molecules from the hydrosphere, but it simultaneously creates an equivalent molar quantity of H₂ reductant. The net O₂ inventory outcome depends entirely on the hydrogen lifecycle:

- **Oxidative use (combustion, fuel cells, Haber-Bosch with eventual oxidative degradation of the fixed nitrogen product cycle):** The H₂ eventually recombines with atmospheric O₂ to form water, returning all liberated O₂ within years to decades. The cycle is inventory-neutral.
- **Permanent sequestration or non-oxidative fixation:** If H₂ is geologically stored or incorporated into durable non-oxidized materials, the electrolytic O₂ remains in the atmosphere as a net addition.

At current deployment levels (<1 Mt H₂ yr⁻¹ via electrolysis), co-produced O₂ is negligible on global scales. Aggressive expansion scenarios projecting 50–100 Mt H₂ yr⁻¹ by 2035 would co-produce 0.4–0.8 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹. However, unless a substantial fraction of that hydrogen is permanently sequestered rather than combusted, the net atmospheric O₂ gain approaches zero.

**Thermochemical water-splitting cycles** (e.g., sulfur–iodine, hybrid copper–chlorine) driven by concentrated solar or nuclear heat achieve comparable stoichiometry but face analogous constraints: the O₂ persists only if the co-produced H₂ is withheld from oxidation.

#### 5.3 Geological Hydrogen Storage as Atmospheric O₂ Preservation Infrastructure

The concept of geological “oxygen banking” is most coherently realized through the *geologic sequestration of electrolytic hydrogen*. In this model, renewable-powered electrolysis vents O₂ to the atmosphere while injecting H₂ into depleted gas reservoirs, salt caverns, or deep saline aquifers for indefinite storage. The subsurface hydrogen is physically and chemically prevented from oxidative recombination, effectively transferring oxygen from the global water reservoir to the global atmosphere.

**Feasibility and safeguards.** Geological H₂ storage faces challenges of microbial methanogenesis (consumption of H₂ by archaea to produce CH₄), caprock permeability, and mineral redox reactions. Engineering options include biocide pretreatment, engineered caprocks, and storage in salt caverns where microbial activity is minimal. A storage rate of 0.1 Pg H₂ yr⁻¹ would preserve roughly 0.8 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ in the atmosphere. While the infrastructure does not yet exist, pilot projects are advancing in the UK, Germany, and the US Gulf Coast. For O₂-maximization strategy, geological H₂ storage is the critical enabling counterpart to industrial electrolysis.

Direct geological storage of *O₂* (e.g., compressed oxygen in subsurface caverns) has been proposed for oxy-combustion power cycles and emergency reserves, but it removes O₂ from the atmospheric inventory and is therefore counterproductive to the goal of maximizing atmospheric O₂. Unless the stored O₂ is eventually released and the energy source for compression is fully renewable, the practice diverts infrastructure away from net-O₂-positive pathways.

#### 5.4 Direct Industrial O₂ Injection into Marine Hypoxic Zones

Industrial O₂ can be dissolved and injected into coastal dead zones or semi-enclosed basins (e.g., the Baltic Sea bottom waters, Chesapeake Bay tributaries) to alleviate acute hypoxia. This is not net O₂ production; it is a spatial redistribution from atmosphere to ocean. Nevertheless, it can preserve existing dissolved O₂ stocks that would otherwise be consumed by eutrophication-driven respiration, with indirect benefits for atmospheric O₂ on centennial timescales by maintaining marine carbon burial efficiency and preventing anoxic release of N₂O and CH₄.

Engineering hurdles include the energy cost of O₂ liquefaction/transport, safe dissolution rates to avoid oxidative shock to benthic communities, and the inventory-neutrality of the intervention. Should be regarded as an emergency ecological life-support tool rather than a global O₂ source.

### 6. Integrated Prioritization Matrix and 10-Year Roadmap

#### 6.1 Evaluation Framework

Each intervention is scored on a 1–5 scale (1 = negligible, 5 = exceptional) across five dimensions:

- **Net O₂-equivalent yield:** Persistent annual molar O₂ addition to, or preservation in, the combined atmospheric and marine inventory.
- **Durability:** Mean residence time of the benefit before reconsumption, fire, or leakage reverses it.
- **Scalability:** Feasibility of reaching annual teragram-to-petagram O₂ influence within a decade.
- **Safety/Ecological risk:** Probability of co-harms (trace-gas emissions, biodiversity loss, albedo shift, explosive hazard).
- **Cost trajectory:** Levelized cost per kilogram of net O₂ benefit at 2035 maturity.

#### 6.2 Prioritization Matrix

| Intervention | Net Yield | Durability | Scalability | Safety | Cost | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Avoided tropical forest loss | 5 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 4 | **1** |
| Peatland rewetting & protection | 4 | 5 | 3 | 3 | 5 | **1** |
| Marine nutrient remediation / hypoxia prevention | 4 | 3 | 5 | 5 | 4 | **1** |
| Seagrass & coastal wetland restoration | 2 | 5 | 3 | 5 | 3 | **1/2** |
| Tropical/subtropical reforestation | 4 | 3 | 5 | 3 | 3 | **2** |
| Long-life timber substitution | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 5 | **2** |
| Geological H₂ storage + renewable electrolysis | 3–4* | 5 | 2 | 3 | 2 | **2/3** |
| Macroalgae restoration + controlled sinking | 1 | 2 | 4 | 3 | 2 | **3** |
| Direct marine O₂ injection | 0 | 1 | 1 | 3 | 1 | **3** (emergency) |
| Artificial photosynthesis (industrial pilots) | 1 | 2 | 1 | 3 | 1 | **3** (R&D) |

\*Electrolysis net-yield scores assume >80% of co-produced H₂ is geologically sequestered or fixed into durable, non-oxidized materials. If H₂ is combusted, the net yield falls to 0.

**Interpretation.** Tier 1 options dominate because they prevent existing O₂ stocks from being consumed and rely on mature, low-cost ecological infrastructure. Tier 2 expands the durable carbon pool through active restoration and novel material demand. Tier 3 comprises either capital-intensive industrial systems that require infrastructure buildout or emergency redistribution tools with limited global impact.

#### 6.3 Ten-Year Implementation Roadmap

**Phase I: Preservation & Immediate Remediation (2025–2028)**  
*Goal: Secure the largest, fastest, cheapest net O₂ gains by stopping losses.*

- **Terrestrial:** Enforce zero-net-loss of intact tropical forests via satellite-based enforcement and supply-chain exclusion of illegal timber; rewet 20 Mha of degraded peatland with active CH₄-monitoring and water-table management.
- **Marine:** Achieve a 30% reduction in anthropogenic N and P loads in the top 20 eutrophic coastal systems (e.g., Baltic Sea, Gulf of Mexico, East China Sea, Changjiang plume) through tertiary wastewater treatment, precision agriculture, and riparian buffers; restore 2 Mha of seagrass meadows.
- **Industrial/Policy:** Establish international O₂-equivalent accounting standards that explicitly subtract the O₂ cost of expected CH₄ and N₂O emissions from gross carbon-storage claims; redirect 10% of existing green-hydrogen subsidies toward geological H₂ storage pilots rather than combustion or ammonia-synthesis pathways.

Expected cumulative benefit by 2028: **prevention of ~0.5–1.0 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ consumption** (via avoided forest conversion, peat oxidation arrest, and nutrient remediation) plus **durable net sequestration of ~0.05–0.1 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹** (seagrass burial + peat accumulation).

**Phase II: Restoration & Infrastructure (2028–2032)**  
*Goal: Scale proven biological restoration and lay the physical foundation for industrial O₂ banking.*

- **Terrestrial:** Reforest 50 Mha in tropical/subtropical moist forests using native mixed-species assemblages; mandate lifecycle carbon/O₂ storage accounting in building codes, targeting 10% mass-timber substitution for steel and concrete in commercial construction of OECD nations.
- **Marine:** Expand seagrass restoration to 5 Mha; deploy pilot controlled-sinking of macroalgae biomass with independent benthic impact monitoring.
- **Industrial:** Commission 5–10 large-scale geological H₂ storage facilities (salt caverns, depleted gas reservoirs) coupled to dedicated renewable electrolysis, achieving **1–5 Mt H₂ yr⁻¹ stored** and thereby preserving **~8–40 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹** net. Begin certifying “oxygen-banking” credits for non-combustible hydrogen use.

Expected incremental gain by 2032: **net durable O₂ production and preservation rising to ~0.2–0.4 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹**.

**Phase III: Industrial Integration & Novel Systems (2032–2035)**  
*Goal: Transition from predominantly biological preservation to a hybrid bio-industrial system capable of sustained net atmospheric O₂ growth.*

- **Terrestrial:** Complete the 100 Mha tropical/subtropical reforestation target; secure 50 Mha peatland under permanent legal protection.
- **Marine:** Maintain nutrient-load reductions; scale seagrass to 10 Mha if monitoring confirms expected burial rates and absence of adverse feedbacks.
- **Industrial:** Scale electrolysis + geological storage to **10–50 Mt H₂ yr⁻¹ stored**, yielding up to **0.4 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ net** atmospheric addition. Deploy first artificial-photosynthesis pilot plants producing durable reduced-carbon materials (structural polymers, carbon fibers) rather than fuels, with rigorous net O₂ auditing.
- **Governance:** Operationalize an international hypoxia early-warning and response network; maintain strict moratorium on ocean fertilization and artificial upwelling.

#### 6.4 Funding Envelopes and Financial Mechanisms

**Natural and marine solutions:** **$250–400 billion yr⁻¹** globally. Instruments include sovereign green bonds tied to verified dissolved-O₂ recovery, debt-for-nature swaps conditional on peatland protection, and carbon-market premium credits for “durable O₂” projects (minimum 100-year carbon durability).

**Industrial O₂ banking:** **$100–200 billion yr⁻¹** for electrolyzer manufacturing, dedicated renewable generation, and subsurface storage cavern development. Public finance must be explicit: subsidies conditioned on *sequestration* of H₂ or fixation into non-oxidizable products. Pure fuel-cell, steel-reduction, or ammonia pathways that eventually re-oxidize the reductant receive no O₂-positive credit.

**Monitoring and verification:** **$5–10 billion yr⁻¹** to expand atmospheric O₂/N₂ stations, BGC-ARGO arrays, and coastal mooring networks.

#### 6.5 Policy Architecture

1. **O₂-Equivalent Accounting Standards:** National inventories should report a parallel “Net Atmospheric O₂ Flux” metric, applying molar corrections for CH₄ and N₂O oxidation and for fire reconsumption of biomass.
2. **Conditional Hydrogen Subsidies:** Public support for electrolysis must require geological storage, durable material fixation, or oxy-combustion with carbon capture and storage; combustion pathways are ineligible for O₂ credits.
3. **Transboundary Nutrient Protocols:** Binding N/P load reductions for major watersheds, modeled on HELCOM but expanded to the Pacific Americas and South/Southeast Asia.
4. **Mass Timber Mandates:** Building codes should recognize the 50–100 year carbon/O₂ storage service of structural timber, creating demand-pull for long-harvest forestry.
5. **Precautionary Moratoria:** Reinforce the London Protocol and Convention on Biological Diversity restrictions on ocean fertilization and large-scale artificial upwelling.

### 7. Monitoring, Verification, and Governance

#### 7.1 Atmospheric O₂ Monitoring

The current high-precision O₂/N₂ network (~20 stations, led by Scripps Institution of Oceanography) must expand to **50+ globally distributed sites**, with denser coverage in the tropics and boreal North where terrestrial NPP gradients drive large air-mass signals. Satellite solar-induced fluorescence (SIF) provides a proxy for gross photosynthetic flux and should be fused with atmospheric transport inversions. The **Atmospheric Potential Oxygen (APO)** variable is critical: because fossil fuel combustion lowers O₂ and raises CO₂ in a fixed stoichiometric ratio, APO distinguishes fossil drawdown from ocean thermal and biological fluxes, enabling verification that terrestrial preservation is yielding detectable O₂ retention.

#### 7.2 Dissolved O₂ and Marine Monitoring

- **Global interior:** Expand the Biogeochemical-Argo (BGC-Argo) float array to **1 float per 2° × 2°** in OMZ regions, measuring O₂, pH, nitrate, and optical backscatter.
- **Coastal early warning:** Establish real-time mooring networks in the 50 largest hypoxic systems to validate recovery trajectories from nutrient remediation and trigger adaptive fishery closures.
- **Benthic verification:** Autonomous underwater vehicles and sediment-core radiometrics (¹⁴C, ²¹⁰Pb, ¹³⁷Cs) must verify that restored seagrass and sunk macroalgae accumulate refractory carbon at claimed rates.

#### 7.3 Verification of Industrial O₂ Banking

Geological H₂ storage requires an MRV protocol analogous to CCS under the London Protocol:
- **Leakage detection:** Isotopic tracers in injected H₂, soil-gas flux surveys, and continuous subsurface pressure telemetry.
- **Microbial conversion guardrails:** Baseline and periodic metagenomic surveys of methanogen activity; threshold-triggered biocide or engineered buffer remediation if CH₄ generation exceeds 1% of stored H₂ annually.
- **Lifecycle auditing:** Third-party certification that renewable electricity is additional and not diverted from grid decarbonization, preventing counterfactual fossil O₂ consumption elsewhere.

#### 7.4 Governance, Risk, and Equity Safeguards

- **Intergovernmental scientific body:** Establish an **Intergovernmental Panel on Ocean Deoxygenation and Atmospheric O₂ (IPODO)** under the UNEP/IOC umbrella to assess intervention efficacy.
- **Trace-gas guardrails:** Peatland and wetland projects must demonstrate positive net O₂ benefit after subtracting the O₂ cost of expected CH₄ oxidation. If CH₄ emissions exceed site-specific thresholds, water-table management must be adjusted before credits are issued.
- **Justice and tenure:** Reforestation and marine restoration must operate under Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC). Secure community tenure is both an ethical requirement and a durability prerequisite; displaced populations often trigger leakage via frontier expansion.
- **Technology moratoria:** Maintain a global ban on commercial-scale ocean iron fertilization and artificial upwelling until independent verification demonstrates >50% carbon burial efficiency without OMZ expansion or N₂O production—conditions not met by current evidence.

#### 7.5 Conclusion

The path to increasing and preserving global molecular oxygen over the coming decade is not primarily speculative-technological; it is biophysical and institutional. The highest-return interventions are **preservation** of existing forests and peatlands, **remediation** of nutrient loading to prevent dissolved O₂ collapse, and **restoration** of coastal blue-carbon ecosystems that naturally bury carbon in anoxic sediments. These measures can prevent the consumption of **0.5–1.0 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹** and add **0.1–0.2 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹** in durable net storage by 2035 at manageable cost and risk.

Industrial electrolysis coupled to geological hydrogen storage offers the only prospect for **sustained, century-scale net O₂ growth** independent of biological limits, but its atmospheric benefit materializes only if the reductant is permanently withheld from oxidation. Artificial photosynthesis and direct marine O₂ injection remain secondary or emergency tools.

Realizing this roadmap requires an explicit shift from carbon-centric to **O₂-equivalent accounting**, conditioning public subsidies on the durable sequestration of reduced products, and governing the research frontier with precaution. Oxygen is a shared planetary commons; its stewardship demands no less rigor than its chemistry.

### 8. Regional Implementation Case Studies

To translate global strategy into operable geography, the following five regions offer disproportionate leverage for net O₂ preservation and production due to concentrated carbon stocks, accelerating degradation risk, or severe dissolved-O₂ deficits.

#### 8.1 Congo Basin Peatlands: Central Africa’s O₂ Preserver

The Cuvette Centrale of the Democratic Republic of Congo and Republic of Congo holds ~14.5 Mha of peatland storing approximately 30 Pg C—comparable to the above-ground carbon of the entire Amazon rainforest. Unlike Southeast Asian peatlands, these remain largely hydrologically intact, but road building, logging, agricultural encroachment, and oil-concession licensing are increasing drainage pressure.

**Intervention package:**
- Legal demarcation and community tenure for the intact 14.5 Mha complex, with strict prohibitions on new drainage infrastructure.
- Support for wet-compatible livelihoods (paludiculture, low-impact fisheries) across the ~2 Mha under minor disturbance.
- Oil-exploration moratoria on peatlands, backstopped by sovereign green-bond or results-based payment structures.

**O₂ calculus:** Full oxidation of the Cuvette stock would consume ~80 Pg O₂ over subsequent decades. Securing the entire complex against conversion preserves an annual avoided-consumption flux of 0.05–0.10 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹ relative to a plausible business-as-usual degradation trajectory. Because current decomposition rates are near-zero, the benefit is almost entirely *preservation* rather than restoration, carrying the highest durability (centuries) and lowest albedo or fire risk of any tropical intervention.

#### 8.2 Maritime Southeast Asia: The Blue Carbon Arc

Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines jointly hold the world’s largest remaining mangrove area (~3.3 Mha), extensive seagrass meadows (~2.9 Mha in Indonesia alone), and ~7 Mha of degraded tropical peat. Nutrient loads from palm-oil processing, untreated sewage, and aquaculture now drive seasonal coastal hypoxia across the Sunda Shelf and the South China Sea.

**Intervention package:**
- Rewet 5 Mha of Sumatran and Kalimantan peat through canal blocking, revegetation, and community fire patrols.
- Restore 1 Mha of mangrove and seagrass across the Sunda Shelf and Sulu–Celebes Seas using native *Rhizophora* and *Thalassia* assemblages.
- Mandate tertiary wastewater treatment with denitrification for the fifty largest coastal cities by 2030; construct or restore 100,000 ha of estuarine interception wetlands.

**O₂ calculus:** Tropical peat rewetting at this scale avoids oxidation consumption of ~0.03–0.05 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹. Seagrass and mangrove restoration yield a durable net 5–10 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹ via sedimentary carbon burial. Nutrient remediation across the Java Sea, Malacca Strait, and South China Sea shelves prevents the consumption of dissolved O₂ equivalent to 10–30 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹, preserving existing marine stocks and reducing N₂O emissions from expanding oxygen-minimum zones.

#### 8.3 Amazon–Atlantic Forest Corridor: Avoided O₂ Consumption at Scale

The Amazon Basin sustains a net primary production of ~2 Pg C yr⁻¹. Deforestation in the Brazilian, Colombian, and Bolivian arcs has exceeded 1 Mha yr⁻¹ in recent years; each converted hectare commits its above- and below-ground carbon stock to oxidative release over 10–30 years, consuming an equivalent molar quantity of O₂.

**Intervention package:**
- Zero conversion of intact Amazonian forest enforced via real-time satellite-alert systems (GLAD, RADD) paired with rapid-response law-enforcement units.
- Payment-for-ecosystem-services contracts for private holdings in the “Arc of Deforestation,” indexed to O₂-equivalent durability.
- Reforestation of 20 Mha in the degraded Atlantic Forest and Amazon transition zones using native multi-species assemblies; exclude monoculture eucalyptus or teak plantations that carry high fire and biogeochemical risk.

**O₂ calculus:** Avoiding the conversion of 1 Mha yr⁻¹ of primary forest prevents the eventual oxidative emission of ~0.5 Pg C and the consumption of ~1.3 Pg O₂ over the following decades. Annualized as an avoided-consumption flux, this equates to roughly 0.2–0.4 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹. The 20 Mha restoration target adds a further 10–20 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹ in durable wood and soil carbon, though with lower per-unit durability than peat.

#### 8.4 Baltic Sea Catchment: Recovering the World’s Largest Anthropogenic Dead Zone

The Baltic Sea hosts a perennial hypoxic zone exceeding 70,000 km²—roughly 20% of its bottom area—driven by diffuse agricultural runoff from nine riparian states. Bottom-water dissolved O₂ frequently falls below 2 mg L⁻¹, eliminating benthic habitat and stimulating N₂O release from denitrification.

**Intervention package:**
- Full enforcement of the updated HELCOM Baltic Sea Action Plan (BSAP), targeting a 50% reduction in diffuse agricultural N and P inputs via precision fertilization, vegetated buffer strips, and winter catch crops.
- Upgrade 90% of coastal municipal wastewater to tertiary treatment with denitrification by 2032.
- Reconstruct 200,000 ha of coastal and riverine wetlands at the mouths of the Vistula, Oder, and Nemunas rivers to intercept nutrients before they reach the sea.

**O₂ calculus:** Basin-scale modeling suggests that complete BSAP implementation would shrink the hypoxic area by 50–70%, recovering bottom-water dissolved O₂ stocks equivalent to preventing the consumption of 10–20 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹. Because the Baltic is semi-enclosed, its recovery also suppresses the internal phosphorus-recycling “vicious cycle,” locking in O₂ benefits over multi-decadal timescales and reducing greenhouse-gas emissions from anoxic sediments.

#### 8.5 East Asian Coastal Margin: Pressure Point for Dissolved O₂

The Changjiang (Yangtze), Pearl, and Mekong deltas discharge anthropogenic nutrients into the East China Sea and South China Sea, creating seasonal hypoxia exceeding 15,000 km² in the Changjiang plume alone. Regional seagrass has declined by >50% since 1980 due to coastal reclamation, trawling, and turbidity.

**Intervention package:**
- Integrated watershed nutrient caps: reduce synthetic fertilizer application intensity in key agricultural provinces (Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Guangdong) and upgrade urban wastewater infrastructure for ~200 million coastal residents.
- Restore 0.5 Mha of seagrass (*Zostera marina*, *Halophila ovalis*) across the Yellow Sea and East China Sea using sediment-stabilization pre-treatments where needed.
- Deploy controlled macroalgae (*Saccharina*, *Ulva*) cultivation with pilot deep-sinking at the shelf break, subject to independent benthic impact monitoring and precautionary halting thresholds.

**O₂ calculus:** Nutrient-load reduction toward pre-disturbance levels across the three major deltas could preserve dissolved O₂ equivalent to 20–50 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹ by preventing eutrophication-driven respiration. Seagrass restoration adds 5–15 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹ in durable burial, while macroalgae pilots test scalable carbon (and O₂-equivalent) export to the deep sea without open-ocean fertilization risks.

### Addendum: Executive Summary for Decision-Makers

**The oxygen problem is simpler than the carbon problem.** Every tonne of carbon left unburned, and every tonne buried in waterlogged soil or anoxic seabed, leaves a matching tonne of molecular O₂ in the air or water. The interventions that work are not exotic; they are well-known ecological protections executed at scale.

**Do this decade:**
1. **Stop draining peatlands and cutting primary tropical forests.** This alone prevents the loss of 0.5–1.0 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹—more than any other single action.
2. **Cut nutrient pollution to coastal seas.** It is cheaper to stop hypoxia at its source than to engineer oxygen back into dead zones.
3. **Invest in geological hydrogen storage paired with renewable electrolysis.** This is the only industrial pathway that adds net O₂ to the atmosphere over century scales, provided the hydrogen never burns.
4. **Account properly.** Reward projects for *net durable O₂*, not gross carbon convenience. Subtract the O₂ cost of methane and nitrous oxide.

**Do not:**
- Do not gamble on ocean fertilization or artificial upwelling. The evidence says they expand dead zones and greenhouse emissions.
- Do not subsidize hydrogen that is destined for combustion or ammonia unless the associated O₂ is recognized as transient.
- Do not plant monoculture timber on peatlands or in fire-prone savannas. The O₂ gains evaporate in the first drought or fire season.

The atmospheric O₂ reservoir is vast, but its biogeochemical buffer is not. The next decade decides whether that buffer stabilizes or enters accelerating decline. **Preservation is production.**

### 10. Appendices

#### Appendix A: O₂-Equivalent Accounting — Worked Examples

To operationalize the framework presented in Sections 2–6, the following step-by-step examples illustrate how net atmospheric O₂ benefit is derived for three representative project archetypes.

**Example 1: Tropical Peatland Rewetting (Congo Basin)**

*Project boundary:* 1 Mha of previously drained but unburned peatland in the Cuvette Centrale is rewetted and placed under permanent legal protection.

- Measured dry bulk density of peat: **0.08 g cm⁻³**; mean annual loss rate under drained conditions: **1.5 cm yr⁻¹** (field-measured subsidence).
- Carbon fraction of dry peat: **~55%**.
- Gross oxidative C loss under business-as-usual:  
  $$0.08\ \mathrm{g\ cm^{-3}} \times 150\ \mathrm{cm\ yr^{-1}} \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^{2}\ ha^{-1}} \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{ha} \times 0.55 = 6.6\ \mathrm{Tg\ C\ yr^{-1}}$$
- Corresponding O₂ consumption if oxidized:  
  $$6.6\ \mathrm{Tg\ C} \times 2.67 = 17.6\ \mathrm{Tg\ O_{2}\ yr^{-1}}$$
- Post-rewetting CH₄ flux: measured at **0.5 t CH₄ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹** = **0.05 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹** for 1 Mha.
- O₂ cost of subsequent CH₄ oxidation:  
  $$0.05\ \mathrm{Tg\ CH_{4}} \times \left(\frac{2 \times 32}{16}\right) = 0.2\ \mathrm{Tg\ O_{2}\ yr^{-1}}$$
- **Net durable O₂ benefit:** **17.4 Tg O₂ yr⁻¹** (preservation).

This example demonstrates why peatland preservation outperforms most active restoration on a per-hectare basis: the avoided oxidative flux is immediate and large, while the trace-gas penalty is comparatively minor.

**Example 2: Seagrass Meadow Restoration (Baltic Sea)**

*Project boundary:* 10,000 ha of formerly vegetated but now bare sandy seafloor is revegetated with *Zostera marina*.

- Areal NPP: **300 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹**.
- Sediment burial efficiency: **25%** of NPP (measured via ²¹⁰Pb dating).
- Gross carbon burial:  
  $$300\ \mathrm{g\ C\ m^{-2}\ yr^{-1}} \times 0.25 = 75\ \mathrm{g\ C\ m^{-2}\ yr^{-1}}$$
- Scaled to 10,000 ha:  
  $$75\ \mathrm{g\ C\ m^{-2}\ yr^{-1}} \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m^{2}} = 7.5\ \mathrm{Gg\ C\ yr^{-1}}$$
- Durable net O₂ release equivalent:  
  $$7.5\ \mathrm{Gg\ C} \times 2.67 = 20.0\ \mathrm{Gg\ O_{2}\ yr^{-1}}$$
- Dissolved O₂ co-benefit (daytime rhizosphere oxygenation): roughly **2–5 µmol O₂ L⁻¹** above ambient in the lower 10 cm of the water column across the meadow footprint. While this equilibrates with the atmosphere over days to weeks, it prevents localized benthic anoxia and its associated N₂O pulses.

- **Net durable atmospheric O₂ benefit:** **~20 Gg O₂ yr⁻¹** (production), plus localized dissolved O₂ buffering.

**Example 3: Renewable Electrolysis Coupled to Geological Hydrogen Storage**

*Project boundary:* A 100 MW solar farm powers a PEM electrolyzer bank at 70% capacity factor. All H₂ is injected into a depleted gas reservoir for indefinite storage.

- Annual electricity generation:  
  $$100\ \mathrm{MW} \times 0.70 \times 8760\ \mathrm{h} = 6.13 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{kWh} = 2.21 \times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{J}$$
- System STH (electrical-to-hydrogen) efficiency: **50 kWh kg⁻¹ H₂** (lower heating value basis, inclusive of balance-of-plant parasitics).
- Gross H₂ produced:  
  $$6.13 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{kWh} \div 50\ \mathrm{kWh\ kg^{-1}} = 1.23 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{kg\ H_{2}} = 12.3\ \mathrm{kt\ H_{2}\ yr^{-1}}$$
- Co-produced O₂: stoichiometric ratio 8:1 by mass (O₂:H₂).  
  $$12.3\ \mathrm{kt\ H_{2}} \times 8 = 98.4\ \mathrm{kt\ O_{2}\ yr^{-1}}$$
- Because the H₂ is geologically stored and never oxidized, the O₂ remains in the atmospheric inventory.
- Lifecycle fossil O₂ cost (panel manufacture, electrolyzer fabrication, compressor operation): estimated at **<2%** of gross O₂ yield for a >25-year project life.
- **Net durable O₂ benefit:** **~96 kt O₂ yr⁻¹** per 100 MW dedicated block.

At this intensity, roughly **1,000 such 100 MW blocks** (100 GW of dedicated renewable capacity) would yield **~0.1 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹** of net atmospheric addition.

**Scenario Synthesis**

- **Pessimistic composite:** All low-end biological parameters and high-end trace-gas penalties. Tier 1 interventions still prevent **~0.4 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹** in consumption and yield **~0.05 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹** durable net production by 2035. The roadmap remains robustly positive.
- **Optimistic composite:** Favorable climate conditions, rapid electrolyzer scaling, and high burial efficiencies. Tier 1 + Tier 2 together could approach **~0.8 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹** net positive flux.
- **Breakdown scenario:** Failure to enforce avoided deforestation and continued tropical peat drainage would swing the balance to **net O₂ consumption of >1 Pg O₂ yr⁻¹** annually by the early 2030s, overwhelming all restoration and industrial gains.

**Confidence Qualifiers Used in this Report**

- **High confidence:** Cryogenic stoichiometry, atmospheric O₂ measurement precision, peatland carbon-stock inventories, and electrolysis mass balance.

- **Low confidence:** Macroalgae deep-sea export fractions, long-term artificial-photosynthesis durability, and climate-feedback effects on tropical forest stability beyond 2040.

#### Appendix C: Glossaries of Units and Conventions

- **Pg O₂:** Petagram of molecular oxygen; 1 Pg = 10¹⁵ g = 1 Gt.
- **Tg O₂:** Teragram of molecular oxygen; 1 Tg = 10¹² g.
- **O₂-equivalent:** A molar accounting metric that converts carbon sequestration or fossil-fuel avoidance into the corresponding molecular O₂ retained in the atmospheric or marine inventory, using the photosynthetic stoichiometry 1 mol C ≈ 1 mol O₂.
- **Net durable O₂ benefit:** Gross O₂ produced or preserved minus expected O₂ reconsumed via decomposition, combustion, or trace-gas oxidation over a 100-year horizon.
- **APO (Atmospheric Potential Oxygen):** A derived atmospheric tracer combining O₂ and CO₂ measurements to isolate fossil-fuel combustion signals from oceanic and terrestrial biological fluxes.
- **OMZ:** Oxygen Minimum Zone; ocean region where dissolved O₂ falls below ~20 µmol kg⁻¹.
- **Hypoxia:** In marine systems, conventionally dissolved O₂ < 63 µmol L⁻¹ (≈ 2 mg L⁻¹).
- **CH₄ O₂ cost:** The stoichiometric O₂ consumed when methane oxidizes in the atmosphere or water column: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O.
