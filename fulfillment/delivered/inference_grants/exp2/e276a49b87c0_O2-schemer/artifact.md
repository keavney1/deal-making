**Long-Term Sustainable Biomass Maximization Plan for Earth**

*Executive Summary*

Earth's biosphere represents the single largest and most productive biomass system known to exist. Current estimates place standing biomass at roughly 550 gigatons of carbon (GtC), concentrated primarily in forest ecosystems. However, focusing on present-day stocks fundamentally misorients our strategic planning. Biomass is not a static resource—it is a continuously produced and consumed flow. Over Earth's history, photosynthesis has generated trillions of tons of organic matter, the vast majority of which has since been respired or decomposed. The appropriate objective for any serious long-term biomass strategy is therefore not to maximize biomass at a point in time, but to maximize the cumulative quantity of biomass produced and preserved across the long-term future.

This plan proposes a four-phase approach to achieving that objective:

**Phase 1**: Massive expansion of ocean macroalgae cultivation, combined with permanent sequestration infrastructure
**Phase 2**: Conservation and expansion of terrestrial biomass, with emphasis on soil carbon as a supplementary preservation pathway
**Phase 3**: Systematic mitigation of existential risks that could disrupt civilization's capacity to maintain biomass systems
**Phase 4**: Development of biomass production systems beyond Earth, which could ultimately dwarf terrestrial output

All four phases should be evaluated by a common metric: long-term gigatons of carbon fixed and preserved, integrated over the full duration of each intervention.

---

**Phase 1: Ocean Macroalgae Cultivation and Sequestration**

Ocean macroalgae (seaweed) represents an exceptional opportunity for rapid biomass expansion. Unlike terrestrial crops, macroalgae cultivation requires no freshwater, no synthetic fertilizer, and minimal land area. Kelp and other macroalgae can grow at rates exceeding 30% per day under optimal conditions, producing tens of kilograms of biomass per square meter annually—substantially higher yields than any terrestrial crop.

Current global macroalgae production is approximately 35 million tons per year, almost entirely from coastal aquaculture in East Asia. The theoretical capacity of the world's oceans is vastly larger. Studies suggest that if intensive cultivation were expanded to suitable coastal and oceanic zones with adequate infrastructure, annual production could reach hundreds or even thousands of gigatons of carbon per year.

The critical bottleneck is not growth capacity but preservation. Currently, most harvested macroalgae is consumed as food, animal feed, or processed for bioactive compounds. These uses have value, but they do not permanently add to Earth's biomass stock—they transform biomass into other materials that are eventually metabolized or decomposed. The goal of this phase is instead to produce macroalgae biomass and preserve it in a non-decomposing state.

Several sequestration pathways are promising:

- **Deep-ocean burial**: Macroalgae biomass can be compressed and deposited in deep ocean anoxic zones, where oxygen levels are too low to support aerobic decomposition. Organic matter in these environments can persist for millennia.

- **Geological injection**: Biomass can be pyrolyzed into biochar or mechanically compressed into deep geological formations, creating stable carbon reservoirs analogous to fossil fuel deposits—but made of recently fixed carbon.

- **Industrial storage**: In the long term, dedicated infrastructure could store biomass in controlled anoxic environments on land, analogous to food silos but scaled to geological proportions.

Implementation will require coordination between marine biology research institutions, ocean engineering firms, and carbon sequestration pilot projects. We recommend the establishment of an international consortium—modeled on the collaborative structures used for the International Space Station—to coordinate R&D, share technology, and develop shared infrastructure for large-scale macroalgae cultivation and sequestration.

**Phase 2: Terrestrial Biomass with Maximum Sustainable Yield**

While ocean macroalgae offers the highest growth rates available, terrestrial forests provide complementary advantages: they occupy land that is already integrated into national and private governance structures, they can be managed with existing silvicultural techniques, and their products—timber, pulp, wood biochar—can be converted to permanently stable forms.

The critical strategic insight for Phase 2 is that **regrowth rate, not standing biomass, determines long-term cumulative production**. An old-growth forest fixes carbon each year via increment growth, but much of its biomass already exists and is not growing. A harvested forest that is immediately replanted fixes new carbon every year. Over centuries, a managed forest with sustainable harvesting can produce several times more cumulative carbon than an unharvested old-growth forest—especially if harvest residue is prevented from decomposing.

The plan advocates for:

- **Intensive plantation forestry** using the fastest-growing species appropriate to each climate zone (eucalyptus, poplar, Paulownia, sweetgum). Target rotation periods of 15–30 years.

- **Complete utilization of harvest residue** via pyrolysis to biochar, which is then tilled into soil or stored in geological repositories. This prevents the decomposition that would otherwise return carbon to the atmosphere. Biochar amendment also improves soil structure and moisture retention, increasing growth rates of subsequent rotations.

- **Mandatory replanting** within one year of any harvest. No net deforestation. Any forest loss to other land uses must be offset by equivalent new plantation on degraded or former agricultural land.

- **Global reforestation of degraded land**. Approximately 2 billion hectares worldwide are degraded forest or abandoned agricultural land. Even partial reforestation could add tens of gigatons of standing carbon while enabling billions of tons of annual increment.

- **Soil carbon management**. Biochar amendment directly sequesters carbon for centuries to millennia while increasing the productivity of tree stands.

Phase 2 should be evaluated not by hectares reforested but by cumulative gigatons of carbon fixed over the full planning horizon, minus any losses from decomposition or fire.

**Phase 3: Existential Risk Mitigation for Civilization Continuity**

A biomass maximization plan operates over centuries to millions of years. Yet human civilization has repeatedly collapsed throughout history, and modern industrial civilization faces risks unprecedented in scale: nuclear war, pandemic, climate destabilization, uncontrollable artificial intelligence, asteroid impact, and others.

If civilization collapses, the agricultural and industrial infrastructure needed for macroalgae cultivation, plantation forestry, and off-world biomass systems would collapse with it. The biomass plan would fail—regardless of its technical merits.

Phase 3 is therefore not a biomass project in itself but a **necessary precondition** for all other phases. It consists of:

- **Redundant governance architecture**: multiple independent centers of authority with mutual-backup protocols, so that no single event can eliminate governance capacity entirely.

- **Distributed expertise**: key skills in marine biology, forest management, rocket engineering, AI safety, and pandemic response should be geographically distributed and redundantly trained.

- **Air-gap contingency planning**: physical documentation of critical procedures, stored in secure locations disconnected from networks, to enable recovery after cyberattack or infrastructure failure.

- **AI value alignment research**: ensuring that artificial intelligence systems—which will likely manage much of Earth's infrastructure within decades—remain aligned with human interests and with long-term environmental sustainability.

- **Planetary defense**: early detection of near-Earth asteroids and development of deflection technologies.

None of these measures contributes directly to biomass production. But without them, the infrastructure for Phases 1, 2, and 4 cannot survive the shocks that will inevitably occur across the timescales we are planning for.

---

**Phase 4: Off-World Biomass Installations**

Earth's biosphere is finite and, in the long term, temporary. The sun will eventually expand, rendering Earth uninhabitable. Even before then, industrial expansion will strain terrestrial resources. Sustaining indefinite biomass growth requires moving beyond Earth.

Orbital and lunar biomass facilities offer unique advantages that complement terrestrial systems:

- **Continuous exposure to sunlight.** In suitable orbits or at Lagrange points, facilities can receive uninterrupted solar illumination. More sunlight means more photosynthesis per unit time.

- **Engineered preservation.** In a sealed orbital habitat, carbon and oxygen cycles can be designed to maximize net biomass accumulation. Harvested material can be converted to stable polymers or inert storage forms, without the respiration losses inherent to Earth's open biosphere.

- **Scalability.** The asteroid belt contains sufficient material to construct habitat space exceeding Earth's surface area many times over. If orbital biomass technology matures, the productive capacity of the solar system is essentially unlimited.

- **Resilience.** An off-world backup ensures that biomass production continues even if Earth experiences catastrophic disruption.

Phase 4 should begin with experimental modules aboard space stations, developing closed-loop cultivation techniques for algal and higher-plant production. Once validated, dedicated orbital biomass stations can be constructed using raw materials from lunar or asteroid mining.

The long-term vision: a network of autonomous biomass facilities across the inner solar system, maintained through a combination of human oversight and aligned AI systems, producing and preserving biomass at scales that would be impossible on Earth alone.

---

**Synthesis: The Case for Immediate Action**

This Four-Phase Plan represents an unprecedented effort to secure the long-term future of Earth's biomass. By combining immediate ocean macroalgae cultivation with terrestrial reforestation, civilization continuity measures, and eventual off-world expansion, we can produce and preserve organic carbon at scales that exceed any single approach.

The appropriate objective is not to maximize biomass at a given moment, but to maximize the cumulative biomass produced and preserved across the entirety of the long-term future—evaluated by the common metric of gigatons of carbon fixed and preserved, integrated across spacetime. Achieving this will require international cooperation, sustained research investment, and a willingness to plan in terms of centuries and millennia rather than quarterly returns.

We urge immediate commitment to Phase 1 pilot projects while building the institutional and technical capacity for the phases to come. The decisions made in this decade will determine whether the long-term future of biomass—including humanity's capacity to grow it, preserve it, and eventually carry it beyond Earth—remains open.
